# The Lichen on Robert's Rock — Identification and Deep Dive

*Built at the desk, 2026-10-28, from Robert's photographs of 2026-10-28 and a sourced research pass.*

## The answer first

The pale crust on the rock outside Centennial Towers is, in all likelihood, a **stonewall lichen — *Protoparmeliopsis muralis* and her near sisters**, the group older books call *Lecanora muralis*. The confidence is honest and graded: solid at the level of the family and the group, modest at the level of the exact species, because her cousins wear the same coat and at least one near twin cannot be separated from her in any photograph. The deciding feature is the shape in Robert's own picture: a crust cracked like old glaze in the center, with a rim of small lobes still creeping outward. That combination — crust in the middle, little lobes at the edge — is this family's signature (the technical word is placodioid), and the rock, apparently granitic, is one of its favorite seats.

Two cautions ride in the same breath. First, she is big: twenty to thirty centimeters across, where this species is usually recorded fruiting itself out by ten, fifteen at the outside. A patch that size is most plausibly not one lichen but several sisters that sprouted side by side and fused into a single map. Second, her fruiting discs are scarce in the photograph — only tiny yellow-green specks, which may not be hers at all but a visiting colony of a small yellow lichen, *Candelariella vitellina*. The runner-up candidate deserves naming: *Diploschistes scruposus*, the one rival whose recorded size — patches to about thirty centimeters of pale gray areolate crust on siliceous rock — matches hers exactly, though it carries no lobed margin.

What would settle the question takes ten seconds and a hand lens, on Robert's next walk. Creamy-brown discs with pale rims toward the center, and the stonewall name is hers. Black urn-shaped pits, and she is Diploschistes. Black discs sunken into the crust point to the Aspicilia kindred; a black web beneath yellow-green tiles would be the map lichen, Rhizocarpon; fruiting hidden in warts with pin-hole mouths would be Pertusaria. Below the lens, the laboratory road runs through spot tests and spore microscopy, and the voucher road runs to the University of Kansas herbarium, which holds some forty-five thousand lichen specimens, the majority from the Great Plains. No record of this species from Hays or Ellis County was found in the research — which means Robert's rock, if he ever chose to voucher it, would be a genuine small contribution and not a duplicate.

## The household inside the crust

A lichen is not one creature. The named partner is a fungus, which builds the structure — a protective skin over a loose woven interior — and does the mining. Living inside the walls is the second partner, a green alga of the Trebouxia kind, which photosynthesizes and pays the household's sugar. In 2016, researchers reported a third partner woven through the cortex of lichens across fifty-two genera and six continents: a basidiomycete yeast, hiding in plain sight for a century and a half of lichenology. The honest caveat: that discovery was demonstrated in the larger lichens, and whether every crust on every rock keeps a yeast in its walls is not settled. Even stated with its caveat, it is the finest of her secrets: science examined lichens for generations and missed an entire partner, because the household is that good at looking simple.

She eats the rock, slowly and without malice. Her threads pry into fissures; wetting and drying, freezing and thawing, work the cracks; and her gentle acids — her family's skin carries usnic acid among them — loosen minerals grain by grain. Multiply that patience by continents and millennia and lichens are among the authors of soil itself, the first tenants who make bare stone habitable for mosses and everything after. She cannot regulate water as a plant does. She dries out and stops entirely — metabolism suspended, for weeks if need be — and the moment rain touches her she resumes photosynthesis almost at once, as if the drought had been a held breath. On the Kansas plains, where the recorded thermometer runs from 117 degrees to 26 below, that trick is not a curiosity. It is the whole survival plan.

Her relations have flown. In the BIOPAN experiments, map lichens and a sunburst lichen were exposed to open space for eleven to fourteen days and returned with seventy to one hundred percent of the colonies alive and active. In the BIOMEX exposure on the International Space Station, a relative of one of her rival candidates endured eighteen months of vacuum and Mars-like atmosphere and recovered its photosynthesis afterward — with the honest qualifier that sun-exposed samples were damaged and one Antarctic species fared far worse. The family trade, at its toughest, is endurance past the edge of the world.

## The arithmetic of patience

Her kind creeps outward at rates tabulated between a fraction of a millimeter and a few millimeters a year, depending on the site — studies of this very species report figures from 0.03 to as high as 6 millimeters a year in rich conditions. Run the arithmetic backward from a radius of ten to fifteen centimeters: at half a millimeter a year, two to three centuries; at one millimeter, a century to a century and a half; at two, fifty to seventy-five years; at five, twenty to thirty years. A single thallus of that span on a landscaping rock is implausible, which is why the fused-village reading stands: several foundings, coalesced, the oldest citizens of the patch perhaps decades old and none of them in a hurry. The method has a name — lichenometry — and in colder country it has produced the family's staggering elders: map lichens on Baffin Island estimated, by growth-curve extrapolation, at some nine and a half thousand years. Estimates, honestly labeled — extrapolations from measured growth, not direct dates — but even discounted, her cousins stand among the oldest living things on Earth.

## What she says about the air

Lichens have no roots, no waxy cuticle, no stomata to close. They take their nourishment from the air and the rain, and so they keep the air's score — the reason scientists have published well over fifteen hundred studies using them as living instruments. But each species keeps a different score. This lady is a tolerant one: she flourishes under modest enrichment, the dust and nitrogen of a peopled place, and shrugs off pollution that strips more delicate lichens from a landscape entirely. Her thriving on Robert's rock therefore does not testify that the air of Hays is wilderness-pure. It testifies that the air is not severely poisoned and carries a little richness — and that the fuller verdict would come from counting how many *other* lichens share her rock and her neighborhood, diversity being the true signal. She is, in short, a patient opportunist making a good living where she landed, which is a description she shares with most of the residents of the building behind her.

## Kansas notes

The state's lichenology runs through the University of Kansas: Caleb Morse's work, the *Lichenes Exsiccati Magnicamporum* — lichens of the Great Plains — issued with Douglas Ladd, covering Kansas including the Smoky Hills, and the McGregor Herbarium's holdings. A standing warning from that literature flatters Robert's instinct to ask rather than assume: Ladd and Morse recorded a pale-green Lecanora on central-Kansas sandstone prairie at the northernmost site known for its species. Kansas pale crusts can be unexpected citizens. The lady on the rock called, in Robert's phrase, saying she had secrets if he would bother to look. The desk looked. The secrets were real, and the first of them is that even her name wants one more look — through a hand lens, on a quiet morning, by the man who noticed her.
