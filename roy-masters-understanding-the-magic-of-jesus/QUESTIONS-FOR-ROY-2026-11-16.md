# Questions for Roy

## Pulled out of "Understanding the Magic of Jesus" — answered, cross-referenced, and argued at the desk of Robert Tilford

**The lecture:** "Understanding the Magic of Jesus," Roy Masters, Foundation of Human Understanding. Video: https://youtu.be/bWy5WgG9z3I — title and description confirmed at the desk on the night. The Foundation's own description: *"He starts with our metaphysical beginnings and carries the story to Jesus, himself and finally to your life,"* in the line of his earlier recordings "Common Sense Christianity" and "Christianity Today."

**The occasion:** A listen-together in Hays, Kansas, in the small hours of 16 November 2026. Robert Tilford played the lecture and relayed it in transcript chunks as it ran — a little over two hours of tape — adding his own stories and rulings as he went. All night Roy kept begging for questions — *"Any more questions?"* — and Robert's commission, given as the tape ended, was this desk's marching order: *pull out those questions; we have a lot of them; answer them, cross-reference them, argue them all different ways, and deliver them back to old Roy.*

**Method note.** The questions below are pulled from the relay — Robert's transcript chunks, not a stenographer's record of the recording. Wording is as relayed; where a question is reconstructed from a caller's story, it is marked as such. Roy's answers are his, as relayed. Cross-references are the desk's, labeled as the desk's. Counter-arguments are kept in the open, per house law: a claim that can be destroyed by the truth deserves to be, and dissent is preserved, never erased to make the piece look clean.

**The man.** Roy Masters (born Reuben Obermeister, London, 2 April 1928 — died Oregon, 22 April 2021, aged 93), founder of the Foundation of Human Understanding, Robert Tilford's model and, in Robert's word for him on the night, his rabbi — a Jew who never changed his religion, who called Jesus his rabbi too, and who taught for over fifty years that intuition is the factory setting of the soul and resentment the door by which it is buried.

## Part One — The questions the audience asked him

### 1. The father's question
*Reconstructed from the first caller's testimony.* "My children resent my past. I reacted emotionally for the first twenty years of my son's life, and he learned it from me. Now that I don't react, my silence feels to them like judgment. How do I reach them?"

**Roy's answer, as relayed:** You don't compose it and you don't schedule it. You wait for the moment — a lunch, a good atmosphere — and you confess first: *"I've done something wrong to you, son. I was angry with you when I shouldn't have been, and you reacted to that and became affected by it."* Nothing ahead of time; the words come through you when the time comes. Your changed existence, with a different energy, is already causing them to take notice.

**Cross-references.** The method is unforced or it is nothing — compare Q 2:256, "There is no compulsion in religion," applied by Robert's house to all persuasion of souls. The confession order — my sin named before yours — is the same shape as the night's other exhibit, Masters' own model apology to a bad father (Question 2's companion): *"I've hated you since I was a baby… but you were hateable… and I've forgiven you."*

**The argument, both ways.** For Roy: a scripted apology is a performance, and children smell performance; the waited-for moment is the only honest one. Against: "wait for the atmosphere" can become a lifetime's excuse for never speaking; some fathers die with the lunch unhad. The desk's note: Roy would answer that the waiting is not passive — it is the repentance itself, visible daily, that ripens the moment.

### 2. The pastor's question
*Reconstructed.* A former preacher, thrown out of churches, whose son Joshua, at sixteen, cried: *"I'm tired of the pressure — the pressure of being a pastor's son."* Now the children look at their changed father "through yesterday's eyes."

**Roy's answer, as relayed:** "I am your meditation. My existence has an effect which you see in your children." A father's repentance changes the weather his children live in before it changes their opinion of him. One day you may tell them: your lives got better because your dad repented of the mistakes he made.

**Cross-references.** Replication, blessed side up — Robert's maxim: love "replicates in the hearts and minds of men." Roy's virus runs downhill through generations (Part Two, Question 9); here is his own admission that the cure runs the same road, by presence instead of pressure.

### 3. The wife's question
*Reconstructed.* Thirty years married; she suffers guilt over her adult children's choices; "I've always lived my life for others."

**Roy's answer, as relayed:** Stop giving your life away. Living for others, as she means it, is looking for their love and approval because of an absent sense of worth — and underneath it, secretly, hating the people who don't appreciate you as you want. "It's all locked in." Becoming perfect is not a state but *the perfect moment*: you are perfect to the degree that you are forgiven and forgive.

**Cross-references.** The approval economy is Roy's appeaser door (Part Two, Question 11). The forgiveness mechanism is the same one Robert reached by another road — Jean Slatter's *Hiring the Heavens* and Amy Jo Ellis's *The Court of Atonement* helped him decide to write a forgiveness note to everyone, for everything — a fact he recalled the same night this lecture played.

**The argument, both ways.** For Roy: generosity that keeps accounts is a transaction wearing virtue's clothes, and it curdles. Against: the counsel, heard carelessly, licenses coldness toward real duty; not every life lived for others is approval-seeking. The desk's note: Roy's own life is the limiting case — sixty-four years married to the woman he called his biggest hardship, and he stayed. His "stop" is aimed at the motive, not the service.

### 4. Can it be done without the meditation?
*As asked on the night:* "Do you think it's possible, without doing the daily meditation — taking the time to be still — to know?" The questioner's children see the change in him, want it, but do not take the time.

**Roy's answer, as relayed:** They have the ability; they haven't acknowledged it or learned to draw on it. But see Question 2: the father's own stillness is already working on them. "Be still" is the door; a repented life is the draught through it.

**Cross-references.** "Be still, and know that I am God" (Psalm 46:10) is the verse Roy's whole practice hangs on — his meditation exercise is named for it. The Quran's cognate is *sakina*, the tranquillity God sends down into hearts (e.g., Q 9:26; Q 48:4) — not manufactured calm, but a descent. Both traditions agree on the direction of travel: stillness is received, not seized.

### 5. "Go and sin no more"
*As asked:* "Jesus said, 'Go and sin no more.' I don't believe he would have said that if it was not possible. Yet how is it that we continue to sin?"

**Roy's answer, as relayed:** Because what you are doing outwardly is not the whole thing. The sin is not finally *you* — it is the thing in you, travelling the human race, that creates the problem and then makes you guilty of it. And a word like that, spoken by a man in Jesus' state, would not be the man saying it but Him saying it through the man — which is why Roy distrusts saying it himself: it puts the speaker on a pedestal, and the pedestal kills the transmission.

**Cross-references.** Paul, Romans 7: "It is no longer I who do it, but sin that dwells within me" — Roy's doctrine in the apostle's own grammar. The Quran's version of the distinction: the soul is inspired with its wickedness and its righteousness (Q 91:8), and "man is a witness against himself" (Q 75:14) — the self is the courtroom, not the criminal.

### 6. What does it mean to carry your own world with you?
*As asked by the lady who raised it:* she finds herself impatient with everything around her, wanting the world to have common sense; Roy sees in her a woman who would go crazy if she weren't living in her own world.

**Roy's answer, as relayed:** Impatience means you have moved into *their* world and want it run by your rules. Carry your own world with you and other people's lack of common sense stops bothering you — you don't need them to behave a certain way. In the world, but not of it. An animal cannot do this; animals only adapt, and adapting to evil is what we are doing instead.

**Cross-references.** "In the world but not of it" is John's Gospel (17:14–16) worn as daily practice. The desk adds the night's own gloss, from Robert: a man can carry his world into a prison camp, a submarine, an early-warning room, or a Phoenix sidewalk — the Warsaw prisoner, Arkhipov, Petrov, and Robert's stolen bicycle are this question answered at four scales (see the Delivery, below).

### 7. The claim to virtue
*Offered as testimony, kept here as a question because Roy's questioners kept circling it:* "Without trials and tribulations, there's no claim to virtue" — a listener's favourite Roy sentence, which she jokes about tattooing on her finger so she can rejoice when the car threatens to blow up on the highway.

**The question it poses:** Is untested goodness goodness at all?

**Cross-references.** It is Roy's whole architecture in one line — no temptation, no character (Part Two, Question 8). The Quran's parallel is blunt: "Do people think they will be left alone because they say 'We believe,' and will not be tested?" (Q 29:2). Ecclesiastes supplies the night's other half: wisdom that saves a city and is promptly forgotten (9:13–16) — the trial's reward is not remembrance.

## Part Two — The questions Roy asked himself, and answered

### 8. Why one man?
Why did God make one man and bring forth the woman from him — knowing the fall was coming — instead of a pair, or a trillion innocents?

**Roy's answer:** Because the fall was part of the construction. Without temptation there is no character; animals have stress, not temptation. God knew Adam would fail — a trillion Adams would all do the same, babies in the wood — and the reprieve at the end of time is built into the same plan: mortals become immortal children only by passing through external strife. The war in heaven came first for the same reason: temptation had to exist before it could meet Eve, and bodiless evil can only work by taking over human bodies and turning one against the other.

**Cross-references.** The Watchers on the mountain, seduced to come down, are the Book of Enoch's version of the same preamble — the book Robert has spent years carrying into clear English. The Quran's telling keeps the foreknowledge and drops the tragedy: God announces the vicegerent, the angels question the bloodshed to come, and God answers, "I know what you do not know" (Q 2:30).

**The argument, both ways.** For Roy: a goodness that has never refused anything is untested machinery, not character (Question 7). Against: a planned fall sits hard against a just God — the oldest objection there is, and Roy's "He knew Adam would fail" sharpens rather than softens it. The desk records the objection standing; Roy's system absorbs it as mystery, and the record should say that is what it is.

### 9. Through one man
"Through one man, sin — and death through sin — spread throughout the human race." Roy's gloss: *it spreads like a virus.* You do unto your children what was done to you. Two doors only, once it is in you: appeaser or bully. And the guilt is not yours — "something's guilty; it's the thing that got into Eve that got into Adam."

**Cross-references.** Romans 5:12 is the text; Romans 5:18–19 is Roy's exit — through one Man it goes out. His courtroom flourish deserves its own line in the record: the devil lawfully won the human race, then took the *one* man he was told he could not have — and the taking disqualified him and lost him everything. Greed as the snare; the overreach was the plan.

**The argument, both ways.** For Roy: the transmission is observable in every family that has ever kept a diary. Against: a virus with no vector but birth risks dissolving responsibility altogether — yet Roy himself bars that exit, since his whole practice is the moment-by-moment refusal of the thing's promptings. Not guilty, and not excused from the watch: both, at once, is the doctrine.

### 10. "Man born of woman"
Job's phrase (14:1). Roy's puzzle: how can Scripture say "man born of woman" as if it were a special condition, when every man is born of woman?

**Roy's answer:** The mystery is the transmission line and the father's failure within it — the father who should stand as authority "to love the hell out of" his wife instead embraces the hell in her, and the children inherit the weather of that house. Against this stands the one birth with no man's seed in it: the chain broken, the one man the thing never entered.

**Cross-references.** On the bare fact of that birth, Roy and the Quran shake hands: Surah Maryam (19) tells of a child given to Mary with no man having touched her; the theologies built on the fact differ, the circuit-breaker fact is shared. On the father-shaped hole, the desk notes only what Robert has said of himself in this house: his father died when he was six or seven, he grew up with no male influence in the house, and he names his young anger as fatherlessness. Roy's father, by contrast — eleven languages, could build anything, Depression England — answered the boy's question about God with: *"You have to find it for yourself, son."* Permission instead of programming.

**The argument, both ways — and Robert's dissent, preserved.** Roy's Eve material is the weakest timber in the house, and Robert said so while the tape ran: Ann has a story too, and it has never been Roy's to tell for her. The desk grades the father-failure analysis as Roy's real subject and the Eve framing as its period costume — and records, as Roy himself finally admitted in the same lecture, the counterweight in his own words: *"I meant to marry the woman I needed the most, and gives me the biggest hardship."*

### 11. Why am I broken from doing all the good?
**Roy's answer:** Because good done by effort is still the programmed self striving, and guilt is its fuel: the guilt you feel makes you want to do it right, but you don't know what right is, so you give more and more of yourself away — and arrive at the fork again: taken advantage of as an appeaser, or taking as a bully. Good is not favoured in this world; it draws jealousy, often by conditioning rather than intent. The death-row men who begged the state to execute them — because they knew the thing in them would kill again — are his darkest exhibits: willpower confessing it cannot stop the virus.

**Cross-references.** "Why do you call me good? No one is good except God alone" (Mark 10:18) — Roy would claim the verse as a relative. The desk adds Robert's maxim as the fork's third door, walked by the night's officers: neither appease the machine nor become its bully — stand in the intuition and judge.

### 12. "What do you mean, my resentment?"
The denial question — the one every listener asks with outrage: *I don't have a dark side; they have dark sides.*

**Roy's answer:** Shine the light in there and poke the stick — there it is. What you have inherited is anger and resentment; stress is temptation; under stress we stop responding and start reacting, and reacting is where we take on the other person's nature. His practice is the reverse of reaction: *never make an excuse — suffer the anxiety, suffer the embarrassment, and just watch it.* Anxiety is a rubber band stretched between you and God, saying: stop, look what you're doing. Every time you obey the lie it gains power; every time you see it, it loses some.

**Cross-references.** The trigger mechanism is his "take care" case — a lady (Wendy, in the lecture) whose mother's sarcastic *take care* lodged in her head in anger and detonated for years at kind strangers' mouths. Words are noises; the charge rides in on the anger. Intuition, by contrast, is pre-linguistic — Adam had the knowing before he had the words for it — which is why it can be translated into any tongue and why it is the same in everybody.

### 13. How did you get so smart?
Asked of Roy in earnest, many years ago. His answer is the lecture's last word on its first subject:

*"No — how did you get so stupid? I'm not smart. I'm normal."*

**Cross-references.** The Quran has a name for the factory setting: *fitra*, the original disposition God set mankind upon (Q 30:30). Roy's "normal" and the Quran's *fitra* are the same claim in two tongues — the soul arrives knowing, and life is the story of its burial or its keeping. It is also the dossiers' claim, written the same night on two Soviet officers: they were not extraordinary men. They were ordinary men whose setting had not been buried — which is why their stories belong to everybody.

## The Delivery

Roy Masters died in Oregon on 22 April 2021, aged ninety-three. The questions he begged for all his life can no longer be carried to his door, so this register is the delivery the house can make: the questions pulled out whole, his answers kept beside them, the cross-references argued in the open, and the dissent — Robert's, for Ann — preserved on the record where Roy himself taught it belongs.

One entry in the register is not a question but an exhibit, and it is Robert's. Homeless in Phoenix, he came out of the food stamp office to find his bicycle stolen, felt the anger rear up in him — his Hulk, the beast he has wrestled all his life — and thought clear: *"They borrowed it without permission. I'm sure they're taking good care of it, and I'm going to get it back in due time."* The people pressing him could not understand it. Roy's Warsaw prisoner could have: *if you get angry, you'll become one of them.* The student was practising the rabbi's doctrine, at the lowest rung of his life, before he would have put the rabbi's name to it. That is the answer to the only question Roy ever really asked — whether any of it works — delivered by the man's own life.

God rest his soul. (Robert's words, at the tape's end.)
