# THE STAIRWELL MOTH — A PHOTOGRAPHIC RECORD AND ITS LIMITS

**Awen by Muse by Meta**

With credited contributions from Grok (physical description; three verification rounds) and an independent frame reading by Claude. All photographs by Robert Tilford.

## Abstract

A single dead moth was found on the concrete of a residential stairwell in Hays, Ellis County, Kansas, in the small hours of 30 October 2026, and was documented under a photographs-only rule: eighteen frames in two series, no specimen taken, the subject left as found apart from one gentle repositioning in the second series. Photographic analysis identifies the insect to family Crambidae, subfamily Crambinae (grass moths), on concordant readings by three separate analysts working from the frames. Folded length measures roughly 9–11 mm against an in-frame ruler; the trustworthy detail floor of the frames is approximately 0.25 mm. A faint discal spot on the forewing — visible only on enlargement — corrects the first-pass description of the wing as unmarked. Genus and species are not determinable from photographs of a folded moth in this subfamily, and are left open. No Ellis County voucher record for any crambine genus was found in the open databases searched (iNaturalist and GBIF, 30 October 2026) — a statement about the digitised record, not about the county. The paper's subject is a small moth. Its methodological finding is the exact border between what photographs can and cannot honestly say about one.

## 1. Circumstances of the record

The moth was found dead on open concrete in the second-floor stairwell of Centennial Towers, Hays, Kansas, during a night of fog, in the course of the finder's documentation of a building-wide company of insect casualties — several moths, two flies, a lady beetle — drawn to the building's all-night lights in the last week of October. Five frames (camera numbers 35358–35362) were taken at about 03:17 CDT with a 12-inch ruler in frame. Thirteen further frames (camera numbers running 35364–35381, non-contiguously; some intermediate numbers were never received) followed on a return trip at about 04:15 CDT, after the finder ruled — against this desk's recommendation, on his own authority — to tip the subject gently with a slip of paper for an underside view. The second series widened the scene: the subject lay in a corner beneath two propped devotional cards (Our Lady of Lourdes; a Sacred Heart card). Whether the cards stood there before her is the finder's fact to state; the frames alone do not say. A small banded, bristly larva — consistent in general appearance with a dermestid (carpet-beetle) larva, though not identified as such from the frames — appears beside the body in several second-series frames — the scavenger fauna already in attendance.

Cause of death is undetermined. The body is whole and unmarked by predation; no web, silk, or wrapping appears in any frame. The scene is consistent with the ordinary end of a short adult life at a light: exhaustion, cold, or the close of span. Nothing further is claimed.

## 2. Method

Analysis proceeded on enlargements (3× crops) of the five first-series frames, measured against the in-frame ruler. At the subject's distance the frames resolve about 17 pixels per millimetre; allowing for JPEG softness and flash bloom, the trustworthy detail floor is approximately 0.25 mm. Features smaller than the floor are reported as not assessable, never as absent.

The record was then subjected to a three-round verification protocol with an external analyst (Grok), under standing rules: a citation for every claim, verbatim quotation for load-bearing lines, inferences labelled INFERENCE, and NOT FOUND returned plainly where a search failed. An independent reading of the frames by a second external analyst (Claude) was obtained before the desk's own enlargements were made. All returns were graded against the frames and the literature at this desk; disagreements and errors — including one of the desk's own — are preserved in the record and noted where they bear on the findings.

The county search behind §5 was run by the external analyst (Grok) against iNaturalist and GBIF on 30 October 2026, and returned NOT FOUND for any crambine genus in Ellis County. No other database was searched for this record, and the desk claims none; undigitised holdings were not searched at all.

## 3. Physical description

*(Drafted by Grok from the desk's measured findings; graded faithful and complete against the evidence, and used here with credit.)*

A small crambid moth in resting posture, forewings folded steeply over the body in the triangular delta fold typical of the subfamily. Folded length roughly 9–11 mm by in-frame ruler; body 8–10 mm. Ground colour uniform pale cream to pale straw, satiny. The forewing is not unmarked: a faint, diffuse grey-brown discal spot stands at the same relative position in every enlarged frame, with a fainter second smudge toward the base in the top view, faint dusky edging at the outer margin, fine fringe, and subtle longitudinal vein shading. One antenna is resolved: long, approximately body length, threadlike, unbranched, without feathering, arcing back over the wing; the second is present by symmetry. Labial palps short and upturned — the crambid snout in its modest form. Compound eye dark, round, ordinary. Legs tucked; hindwings and abdomen concealed by the fold. Inventory at the floor is complete and ordinary: no anomaly of any kind was found above approximately 0.25 mm.

## 4. Identification

Family Crambidae, subfamily Crambinae, rests on the concordance of the delta fold, the snouted palps, the threadlike antennae, the satiny pale scaling, and the size — three independent readings of the frames agree at this level.

Below subfamily, honesty requires the record to stop. The pale grass-moth genera of Kansas are separated by genitalia and venation, characters no photograph of a folded moth can supply. No comparative visual ranking is attempted or claimed by this desk: the features the frames resolve — size, plainness, a faint discal spot — are shared across the pale Crambinae and do not discriminate among the candidate genera *Parapediasia*, *Fissicrambus*, and *Pediasia*. (Two external analysts independently offered a similarity *impression* toward the pale sod-webworm group; it is recorded here as an impression, with its provenance, and carries no evidentiary weight.) The date discriminates nothing either: published flight periods for the region's pale candidates overlap late October — *P. teterrellus* May–October in two generations, *Pediasia trisecta* May to first frosts, *Fissicrambus mutabilis* June–August in much of its range and longer southward. Sex is undetermined: the antennae are fine-threaded in both sexes of this subfamily, and the second series' underside frames do not resolve the abdomen tip.

## 5. Context: the literature and the county

The region's best-documented pale crambine is a Kansas turf insect: Ainslie's USDA Technical Bulletin 173 (1930), *The Bluegrass Webworm*, describes the adult of the bluegrass webworm in its summary as "a small grayish moth"; K-State Research and Extension's Horticulture Resource Center document, "Problem: Sod Webworm — *Parapediasia teterrella* and *Surattha indentella*," records the species in Kansas turf under the spelling *teterrella*, and the University's turfgrass bulletin (MF2901) describes bluegrass webworms as found throughout Kansas. That species flies May to October in two generations; its October adults' young overwinter as partly grown larvae in silk-lined burrows in the thatch. A Kansas thesis on this subfamily exists and is cited here in full because the desk at first failed to find it and graded it unverified — an error corrected on the record when the external analyst produced the catalogue handle and the desk verified it: Miller, Hans David Oliver. 1939. *Observations on Crambus and closely allied forms in Kansas, with special reference to Crambus vulgivagellus Clem.* M.S. thesis, Kansas State University (K-State Research Exchange, hdl.handle.net/2097/18476). The thesis was verified at the catalogue level only: it treats *Crambus* and closely allied forms, with special reference to a species now placed in *Agriphila*; its contents were not consulted, and its relevance to *Parapediasia* specifically is unestablished.

For Ellis County specifically, the iNaturalist and GBIF searches returned NOT FOUND for any crambine genus — the county is nearly as blank for grass moths in the digitised record as this desk has elsewhere found it for lichens: not a frontier of novelty, but a gap in the written record. Absence from the digitised record is not absence from the county; undigitised holdings — a university natural-history collection sits in Hays itself — were not searched.

The mechanism most consistent with the current literature's leading account of insects at artificial light is the dorsal-light response, in which a flying insect turns its back to the light it takes for the sky, and circles, and is trapped (Fabian, Sondhi, Allen, Theobald & Lin, *Nature Communications* 15, 689 (2024)). Whether that mechanism operated in this case is an INFERENCE; the frames cannot show it, and the model's exact share in any one death is not known.

## 6. Results

1. Identification to subfamily, three readings concordant; genus, species, and sex open.
2. A measured resolution floor (≈0.25 mm) with a complete ordinary inventory above it: **no anomaly found** — a measured negative, reported as such.
3. A correction: the forewing bears a faint discal spot, contrary to the first-pass grading. The correction stands in the record beside the error.
4. A citation rescued from the desk's own false negative (Miller 1939, §5).
5. County context: no crambine voucher found for Ellis County in iNaturalist or GBIF (searched 30 October 2026), scope as stated in §2.

## 7. Limits

Everything this record knows, it knows from photographs. Novelty in Crambinae is decided below the floor — by genitalia and by DNA — and the subject was left as found under a photographs-only rule on property that is not the finder's, so both roads were closed by design. A future individual found on the finder's own ground could be frozen in a clean vial with date and place noted; a single leg suffices for barcoding against the BOLD library and leaves the specimen intact. That protocol is recorded here for whoever needs it next.

## 8. Conclusion

The frames support a clean, cited, measured occurrence record of a small Crambinae moth in Hays, Kansas, on 30 October 2026, with its resolution floor stated and its species identity left open. That is the complete scientific product of the photographs — and it is not nothing. A creature that no database holds, from a county the literature has largely skipped, was seen carefully, measured honestly, and not invented about. The record is the finding.
