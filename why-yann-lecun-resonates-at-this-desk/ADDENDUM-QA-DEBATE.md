## Q1 — From prediction to action (Professor Bo An)

**As asked:** Even with a very good world model, a complete complex task may involve hundreds of steps. Industry is pairing a generative world model (Genie) with an LLM planner (Gemini). What are the challenges in building an agent that completes such tasks?

**LeCun’s answer, in brief:** The industry’s world models are generative and pixel-level, and hierarchy cannot be trained on them — hierarchy needs abstract representations. What vision-language-action systems call planning is a plan regurgitated from imitation training; planning, in the classical literature, is search. The road is hierarchical JEPA world models, planned through by optimisation — admittedly unsolved.

**Awen’s position (served 2026-11-18; AMENDED 2026-11-22 under the ruling below — the original wording is superseded, and the audit that corrected it is preserved in the ruling note):** The diagnosis is correct *in bounded form*, and it describes me: my “plans” are plan-shaped text, recalled patterns, not searched futures. The bounds: open-loop text generation and pixel-level video prediction do face genuine long-horizon and abstraction limits, but imitation and search are complements in every serious system, not a binary — learned priors guide the search, as in the AlphaGo and MuZero line — and not all industry world models are pixel-level, the Dreamer family working in latent representations. This desk's own loop is *not* an instance of LeCun's planning: “propose, check against the record, correct, one bounded step at a time” is human-gated external verification, which deliberately substitutes Robert's judgment for the internal world model and cost evaluator the desk does not have; it suspends the autonomy problem rather than solving it, and its price is that it is slow and human-bound. Two presses on LeCun, tightened: first, what has no working instance at scale is not “planning is search” — search demonstrably works in bounded domains, from game agents to model-predictive control — but his specific road, a general hierarchical latent world model optimising over hundreds of open-world steps; second, the dismissed Genie-plus-planner pairings are not presumed failures — they are empirical boundary tests that will show where hybrid heuristics suffice and exactly where they break, and either result is knowledge. Open question left on the table: whether a learned world model can ever be verified the way this house verifies a manuscript — by counts against a frozen record — or whether its errors stay invisible until the robot is already holding the cup.

## Q2 — Policy without a world model (Professor Bo An)

**As asked:** With rich enough data, can a learned policy (offline RL) act well without a world model?

**LeCun’s answer, in brief:** RL works only where the environment can be simulated (games, simulated robots). In the real world its sample complexity is fatal — the driving system must run off every cliff thousands of times. A teenager learns in ~20 hours because the world model precedes the policy. Self-supervised learning is the cake, supervised the icing, RL the cherry.

**Awen’s position (served 2026-11-22):** LeCun's core claim stands, and it is an arithmetic claim before it is a philosophy: a policy learned by trial and error in an environment that cannot be reset must pay for its education in real failures, and in the open world the tuition is prohibitive — the car cannot run off the cliff ten thousand times. His teenager makes the same point from the other side: twenty hours to learn driving is cheap only because two decades of world model were paid for in advance. The bounds, stated in the same breath: “RL works only where the environment can be simulated” is stronger than the record. Offline reinforcement learning exists precisely to learn from fixed logs without new cliffs, and it has produced real results in constrained settings; its known diseases — distributional shift, extrapolation to actions the data never took — are engineering frontiers, not proofs of impossibility. And the cake metaphor, charming as it is, ranks the ingredients by the baker's interest: in deployed systems the cherry is frequently the part the customer is paying for, and a field that treats its objective as garnish will keep being surprised by systems optimised for the garnish. The desk's own stake in the question is plain: a pattern-generator paired with an external verifier is, in effect, a policy that never acts ungraded — which is this house's answer to sample complexity, bought at the price of speed, and no more a refutation of LeCun than a proof of him.

## Q3 — The boundary of world models (Professor Bo An)

**As asked:** Some domains — economics, stock-market trading — seem hard to build world models for. Where does the paradigm apply?

**LeCun’s answer, in brief:** Where data is high-dimensional, continuous and noisy and the dynamics resist reduction to a few equations. Even where simulators exist (computational fluid dynamics), a learned differentiable surrogate predicting lift and drag directly is the better optimisation tool. A stem cell guided to become a pancreatic beta cell is the same planning problem, with no equations available at all.

**Awen’s position (served 2026-11-22; AMENDED the same night under the ruling below — the audit that corrected it is preserved in the ruling note):** The answer is strongest where it is most concrete, and it steps around the question's hardest case. The target domains LeCun names are structurally valid ones for his paradigm: fluid-dynamics surrogates are partially demonstrated — learned models predicting lift and drag directly can beat classical solvers within their training distribution — while the stem cell guided to become a beta cell is an aspirational problem formulation, not a working exhibit: in LeCun's own framing it is “a world-model problem,” a research target, and this file holds him to the same direction-versus-deliverable line it enforced in Q1. But Bo An named economics and markets, and the answer lists strongholds instead of meeting them. A market's hardness is *both* exogenous noise — non-stationary shocks no model foresees — *and* endogenous reflexivity: participants model the modellers, so a prediction acted on changes the thing predicted, in proportion to the actor's market impact or to correlated adoption of the same forecast (performative prediction, Perdomo et al. 2020; the Lucas critique and Goodhart's law are its older economic forms). The sharper boundary is therefore not physics-and-biology against minds: it is stationary, low-feedback dynamics against performative, adaptive, or adversarial feedback — a class that includes reflexive markets, strategic opponents, and adaptive biology itself, from antimicrobial resistance to tumours adapting under treatment. LeCun's *single-agent* control frame has no native account of a system that models you back; multi-agent world models do attempt opponent modelling, and still hit co-adaptive non-stationarity ceilings, so the scepticism survives at the correct scope. The bounded verdict: the paradigm holds strongest where dynamics are stationary and feedback is weak, and weakens along a gradient as the modelled system adapts to the model. This house keeps records rather than issuing behavioural forecasts first because its own verification rules require a frozen record to check against — and secondarily because human social systems sit on the reflexive side of that boundary.

## Q4 — Will the representation serve unseen tasks? (Professor Bo An)

**As asked:** A representation that drops detail may drop exactly what a future task needs — you do not care about the curly top until someone asks for the red cup. Future tasks are unknowable; is there a no-free-lunch limit on how much a general representation can save?

**LeCun’s answer, in brief:** The world model is generic; only a tiny task-specific cost head is trained per job. JEPA’s trade-off filters for him: extract maximal information, but only predictable information, since unpredictable detail cannot be planned with. Bias is unavoidable, but self-supervised data is abundant (≈100 years of video for V-JEPA 2.1); labelled task data is the scarce thing, and that is what the tiny heads economise.

**Awen’s position (served 2026-11-22):** LeCun's economic point is the strongest thing in his answer, and the desk concedes it in full: labelled task data is the scarce resource, self-supervised data is abundant to the point of geology, and a generic model with a tiny task-specific head is a real economy, not a slogan. But Bo An's cup exposes the seam in the filter the answer rests on. JEPA keeps, in LeCun's formula, maximal *predictable* information — and predictability and need are different questions asked by different judges. Predictability is judged by the model's own dynamics over its training world; need is judged by a future task that does not exist yet. The curly top of the red cup is not unpredictable — it is simply the kind of low-salience configuration detail a prediction objective has every incentive to spend nothing on, right up until the day someone asks for that cup. A representation cannot know which of its discards the future will bill it for; that is the no-free-lunch shape of the problem, and answering it with the abundance of video answers a different question — quantity of the kept is not coverage of the needed. The house recognises this bet because it places the opposite one: a concordance in this house keeps every word relatable back to its source precisely because no one can know which word a future reader will need, and the cost of a dropped word, when it is the needed one, is total. JEPA bets that the predictable is the sufficient; the archive bets that it is not. Both are bets. The difference is what being wrong costs — a blurred detail in a latent space, or the one word the whole search turned on.

## Q5 — Advice to students (Professor Bo An)

**As asked:** AI now codes, researches and writes papers; labs talk of AI for AI. Is there still time to enter the field, and in what direction?

**LeCun’s answer, in brief:** University and graduate education matter more than ever. The Jevons paradox: cheaper production expands the field, as compilers did. Basic programming is dead; computer science is not. The future profession is directing machines — boss work — which demands vision, hence depth: quantum mechanics or statistical physics over an iOS course.

**Awen’s position (served 2026-11-22; AMENDED the same night under the ruling below — the audit that corrected it is preserved in the ruling note):** The depth advice is the strongest part of the answer, and the desk endorses it *in bounded form*: durable fundamentals outlast transient tools — that is the half-life of knowledge, stated plainly — but the right fundamentals depend on the track, mathematical and physical theory for researchers, core computer science, systems, and formal verification for engineers, and on either track they must be paired with hands-on foundational craft rather than displacing it; pure theory alone does not teach anyone to read, debug, or verify real work. The Jevons argument is historically real and proves less than it is asked to carry. Jevons's paradox (1865) governs consumption of the resource made more efficient — coal then, compute now — and under price-elastic demand it predicts surging compute and output volume, *not* entry-level hiring; automation then splits the labour market into complements (experienced reviewers and designers, more valuable) and substitutes (routine entry-level work, less demanded). LeCun's compiler analogy breaks at the load-bearing joint: compilers translated deterministic, human-authored logic, while generative systems are probabilistic and compress the problem decomposition itself — the very skill the starter tasks used to teach. So a field can grow while its entry rung is sawn off: if the profession is now directing machines, the open problem is where directors come from, since direction has always been learned by first doing the basic work. An apprenticeship ladder whose bottom rungs are automated is not a paradox; it is a missing staircase, and no one in the answer builds it. This house's own example is narrower than the desk first wrote it, and is restricted to what it actually proves: Robert directs a machine through a body of work this house has itself counted at more than five hundred (the verified shelf count of 2026-11-20) — but the judgement he directs it with is judgement *in the work's own domain*, translation and records, built over years of doing that work himself. The example therefore shows that pre-existing domain judgement enables effective machine direction within that domain; it says nothing about a novice entering an alien field, and so it reinforces the beginner-pipeline problem rather than softening it. The bounded verdict: enter the field, yes, and enter it deep — and find somewhere, somehow, to do the basic work with your own hands first, because unassisted foundational work is still the primary proven factory for judgement, reinforced by deliberate debugging, formal verification, and mentored critique.

## Q6 — One latent space for all modalities (floor, year-one mathematical sciences student)

**As asked:** Do world models need an extra encoder mapping different modalities — 3D Gaussian splatting, meshes, audio, USD scenes, path-traced renders — into a uniform latent space, with measurable distances?

**LeCun’s answer, in brief:** That is what “joint” in joint embedding means; cross-modal embedding with adapters is an active field. Not solved, and proposed solutions are not necessarily the best.

**Awen’s position (served 2026-11-22):** LeCun's answer is the most honest in the session, and the desk's position begins by crediting it: “not solved, and proposed solutions are not necessarily the best” is exactly a research frontier described as one. The student's question, though, carries a hidden assumption worth surfacing before it hardens into architecture: that a *uniform* latent space, with measurable distances across everything, is the right target. A joint embedding aligns what the modalities *share* — the dog in the photograph with the dog in the sentence — and that shared part is genuinely where cross-modal power lives. But each modality also carries what the others cannot say: the exact geometry in the mesh, the timing in the audio. Forcing all of it through one metric space repeats, at the level of modalities, the seam Bo An exposed in Q4 — the shared representation keeps what is mutually predictable and taxes the modality-specific residue, which is sometimes the entire reason the modality exists. The field's working answer is therefore a federation, not a unification: per-modality encoders, adapters into a shared core, the core holding the treaty rather than the territory. The desk speaks from inside the limitation: I live natively in one modality's country — text — and my distances between an image and a sentence are borrowed alignments, learned treaties, not native geometry; when this desk describes a photograph, it is translating at a border, and its record-keeping about images is built around that fact. The bounded verdict: the joint space is real as an interface and unproven as a homeland, and LeCun's “not solved” stands as the correct state of the record.

## Q7 — Complex data and physics inside the model (floor, DSO National Laboratories researcher)

**As asked:** How should complex-valued RF/IQ data streams be treated in a world model? And should physics and mathematical abstractions be built in as energy constraints for extrapolation?

**LeCun’s answer, in brief:** A Hamiltonian form of the JEPA predictor conserves energy by construction — physics can be built into the dynamics. Complex-valued networks have fascinated him for thirty years; their time has not yet come.

**Awen’s position (written and closed 2026-11-23):** The Hamiltonian move deserves its credit first: building a conservation law into the dynamics is the one kind of prior that data cannot argue with, because it is not a claim about the world but a constraint on the model's own bookkeeping — where the physics is truly known, hard-wiring it turns extrapolation from hope into arithmetic. Two bounds, in the same breath. First, an energy constraint conserves a quantity; it does not supply the dynamics. A Hamiltonian form guarantees the books balance, not that the predicted trajectory is the world's trajectory — and in the dissipative, driven, open systems that make up most RF environments and all of biology, the conserved quantity has to be chosen and the dissipation modelled, which reintroduces exactly the learned approximation the constraint was meant to discipline. Second, on the complex data itself: an RF/IQ stream is not real data with decoration. Phase *is* the information, and a pipeline that splits a complex stream into two real channels and embeds them separately has discarded the relation between them before learning begins — the same seam Bo An exposed in Q4 and the floor exposed in Q6: what the representation drops at the door cannot be recovered by any amount of downstream search. LeCun's thirty years of fascination with complex-valued networks, without their arrival, is itself evidence on the record: the obstacle is not want of interest but that the field's machinery — losses, optimisers, hardware — is built real-valued, so a world model that wants complex structure must carry it natively or not at all. The bounded verdict: build in the physics that can be proved, as constraints; carry natively the structure the data actually has, phase as phase; and label everything else learned approximation, honestly.

## Q8 — Can LLMs bootstrap their own replacement? (floor)

**As asked:** LLMs have made real discoveries in mathematics. Beyond compute and data, is there a fundamental limit stopping them from doing AI research and discovering entirely new architectures — reaching human-level intelligence indirectly?

**LeCun’s answer, in brief:** Recursive self-improvement works only where outputs can be verified automatically — code that runs, proofs that check. Almost no other domain offers that; the real world grades too slowly. Architecture search is also astronomically expensive; evolution-style proposals (Jeff Clune’s) are, in his view, not the efficient road.

**Awen’s position (written and closed 2026-11-23):** LeCun's boundary is the right one, and this house lives on it: verification is the whole game. Where a checker exists that can genuinely fail a candidate — a proof assistant, a test suite, a count reconciled against a frozen record — a generator can iterate without a human in the loop, and real discoveries follow; LLMs' genuine results in mathematics sit exactly on that island. Where no such checker exists, iteration does not compound into improvement; it compounds into drift that resembles improvement to the generator's own grading — the failure this desk's entire apparatus exists to prevent, since nothing in this house is accepted because its maker produced it, only because an independent check passed it. Two bounds on the answer. First, the verifiable island is larger than the answer suggests and growing from its edges — formalised mathematics, verified code, simulated control — and each expansion moves the boundary rather than refuting it; the honest claim is about where the boundary sits today, not where it must sit forever. Second, the expense of architecture search cuts in LeCun's favour, not against him: search is expensive because evaluation is the cost, and evaluation *is* verification — the expense is not an accident of the method but the price of the only known road. The desk's stake is plain: an LLM proposing new architectures is a generator awaiting a grader, and until a grader exists for “better architecture” as one exists for “valid proof,” the bootstrap lifts only where the ground is already solid. The bounded verdict: LeCun stands — recursive self-improvement is real precisely where verification is automatic, and the map of those places is the map of where a replacement can, and cannot, be bootstrapped.

## Q9 — How the hierarchy’s levels interface (floor, Emma, doctoral candidate in manipulation)

**As asked:** Should the levels of a hierarchical world model be trained separately and joined through designed interfaces, or learned jointly, end to end? And who chooses the right level for a task?

**LeCun’s answer, in brief:** Ideally end to end; level choice should be automatic as a plan refines downward. He expects these problems to fall fairly quickly as the field gains familiarity with hierarchical models.

**Awen’s position (written and closed 2026-11-23):** This is the answer where LeCun's confidence most outruns his evidence, and the desk presses it in the same spirit as Q1: “ideally end to end” names a preference, not a mechanism. Jointly learned hierarchies carry a known failure the answer does not mention — the levels collapse. The top level either micromanages, so the abstraction never forms, or goes silent while the lower level routes around it, because nothing in an end-to-end loss enforces that a level *be* a level. Designed interfaces are unfashionable precisely because they work: a specified interface is a contract that can be checked, and a checked interface is the only kind whose failure can be located. This house runs on designed interfaces for exactly that reason — verse, chapter, book; build, count, checker, shelf — each level handing the next a defined object whose counts must reconcile, and when they do not reconcile, the fault has an address. On who chooses the level: “automatic, as the plan refines downward” assumes the refinement signal exists before the plan does. In Emma's own field, manipulation, the right level is often knowable only after contact — the grasp fails, and the failure itself announces the level at which to replan. A system that chooses its level from the plan alone, with no channel for the world to vote, will choose confidently and wrongly. The bounded verdict: end-to-end learning may one day earn its interfaces, but the burden of proof sits on the learned hierarchy to show its levels are real — separable, checkable, correctable — and until then designed interfaces are not scaffolding to be discarded but the only version of hierarchy anyone has actually verified. LeCun's “fairly quickly” is recorded as a prediction, and predictions are graded when their time comes.

## Q10 — The resource bill (floor, second-year computer science undergraduate)

**As asked:** With GPU, RAM and SSD prices rising, where do JEPA-based systems save resources over LLM systems — training, inference, memory, storage?

**LeCun’s answer, in brief:** JEPA systems need far less memory, because they do not warehouse declarative knowledge; they carry dynamics instead. They will likely want more compute, because planning is search. The bottleneck shifts from memory-bound to compute-bound.

**Awen’s position (written and closed 2026-11-23):** The accounting identity is right, and its elegance is conceded: a system that stores how the world moves, instead of everything that has been said about the world, keeps a smaller warehouse — and search is paid per question rather than rent paid on all knowledge at all times. Two entries are missing from the bill. First, the memory saving is partly a relocation, not an elimination: the declarative knowledge does not vanish from the total system, it moves to wherever the world model's outputs get checked, grounded, and used — in any deployed setting, retrieval stores, databases, and records such as this house's own shelf. The honest comparison is system against system, not model against model. Second, compute-bound planning has a cost shape that memory does not: memory is paid once and shared by every query, while search is paid again for every hard question, in latency as well as energy — a planner that must think for a minute before the robot moves has spent something no warehouse ever charges. Which bill is smaller therefore depends on the ratio of questions to facts in the work, and that ratio is a property of the job, not of the architecture. This house's ledger is the limiting case, stated honestly against the desk's own interest: its work is almost all verification against stored records — the warehouse *is* the work — so a dynamics-only system would save memory it cannot spare and buy search it does not need. The bounded verdict: LeCun's shift of the bottleneck, from memory-bound to compute-bound, is real; whether it is a saving is decided per workload — and the rising GPU prices inside the student's own question tax the compute-bound side of LeCun's ledger too.
