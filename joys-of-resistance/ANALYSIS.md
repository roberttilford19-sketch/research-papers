# The Joys of Resistance: The Conclusion Lecture, Examined

**DRAFT — NOT PUBLISHED**

*An analysis of the concluding lecture of the series "The Joys of Resistance," prepared 2026-11-23 under the direction of Robert Tilford. The source of record is the lecture transcript as relayed by Robert from the video (https://youtu.be/tw1-P-rSf1Q). The lecturer is not named in the transcript and is not named here; he is called "the lecturer" throughout. Three passages in the transcript are Robert's own words, spoken over the lecture and marked where they stand in the relay file; they are treated in this paper as Robert's, and quoted as his.*

---

## 1. What the Lecture Argues

The concluding lecture of "The Joys of Resistance" opens by setting its two key terms against each other, and everything that follows hangs on the difference between them. Revolutions, the lecturer insists, are not caused by individuals. They are historical events. Individuals participate in them, sometimes centrally and mostly anonymously, but no one controls them, which is why they tend to be "chaotic, messy, bloody, and often counterproductive." The French Revolution is his standing example: most of the people caught up in it did not want the guillotine, the drownings, the revenge killings, and in the end it was the general longing for an ordinary, predictable tomorrow that brought the chaos to a close. A revolution, on this account, happens to people at least as much as it is made by them, and it frequently runs outside even the desires of its own participants.

Resistance has, in his words, "a very different sensibility to it." It begins where Camus began the series: when you decide for yourself, draw the line, and say no to something, you are also saying yes to something. That double movement is the whole doctrine in miniature. The no is small, personal, and within your control. The yes is what the no protects. And the effect of the two together is not, the lecturer is careful to say, a transformation of the world. "It doesn't transform the world. The world is still going to be the world. History is still going to happen." What it transforms is your relations to the world — and through those relations, yourself. You feel your own capacity. You remake parts of your life. And because other people can see a life being lived differently, the act carries a knock-on effect that no one can schedule or guarantee.

From that distinction he draws a warning about the opposite ambition. The desire to finish the job — to seize power, set everything right, and be done — is, he argues, a failure of self-reflection, because the world you build will contain people who want to resist it, exactly as you resisted the world you inherited. The wish for a final answer leads, by a road he traces without flinching, toward dictatorship, and at the limit toward the logic that says those who disagree must eventually be killed. Against that he sets the alternative notion: resistance as a continuing process, endless, in which people participate and recreate themselves without any terminus. The lecture's literary witnesses are then brought forward to show what that process looks like when it is lived rather than theorised.

The witnesses are the poets and makers of the postwar American West and its eastern counterparts — the Beats and the San Francisco Renaissance and the back-to-the-land generation. Gary Snyder stands first: poet of *Turtle Island*, essayist of *Earth House Hold*, a man who looked at the major trends of his time and answered by re-inhabiting the earth. Beside him stand Lew Welch and his *Ring of Bone*, Joanne Kyger, and Robinson Jeffers — poets who, each in a different way, said no to the given life and built another one. The novels of the same moment, *On the Road* and *The Electric Kool-Aid Acid Test*, are read not as political programmes but as presentations of a possibility: characters who are seeking, whose longing itself is the argument. Ginsberg's *Howl* is the complaint that opens the era — a critique of contemporary society that does not know what to do about it, and is honest enough not to pretend.

Snyder gives the lecture its most practical moment, in an interview the lecturer recalls with care. Asked, as a West Coast man living an alternative life, whether he would do the same on the East Coast, Snyder answered with a question instead of a programme: look around where you live, wherever you are, and ask yourself how the natives lived before we arrived. What you will find, he said, is an environmentally appropriate, regionally specific understanding of the world; mimic it, and you come into closer harmony with the natural world around you. The lecturer dwells on the shape of that answer. Snyder offered no plan, no schedule, no action list, because he knew what worked in the Pacific Northwest might not work in Iowa or New York or Florida. He offered an outlook — and the lecturer's point is that the outlook is itself the resistance. Merely asking the question reorients where knowledge and value are allowed to come from. You stop waiting to be told by the market, the president, Congress, or the technology pages what matters, and you look instead at your watershed, your bioregion, the people who lived on your ground before you. Even beginning that inquiry, he says, transforms your relationship to the world.

His favourite example, and the lecture's longest, is George Nakashima. Nakashima was a Japanese American architect and woodworker, born in Washington, who was interned with his family during the Second World War. In the camp he met a traditional Japanese carpenter and learned the old methods — the use and care of Japanese hand tools, the joinery — with the time that captivity forced on him. Released, he came to Pennsylvania, worked on a farm, saved, bought land nearby, and built the studio that stands there still. His resistance took the form of a rethought relation to his material. "We're not making furniture," he said, in the lecturer's telling, "we're extending the lives of trees." Mass production, for Nakashima, steals the natural expression of the tree; his furniture — the live edge left rough, the crack held by a butterfly joint — asks the tree what kind of furniture it wants to become. His memoir, *The Soul of a Tree*, became, as the lecturer puts it, the back-to-the-land woodworking movement's bible. Nakashima did not overthrow Western industrial capitalism. He did something else: he lived a new set of values inside an old tradition, kept the tradition developing, and through his students, readers, and customers changed other people's outlooks. All of it, the lecturer says, is resistance — because a man who had been put in a camp by his own country looked around, decided this was not the way it should be, and reoriented himself toward what he could control.

The second half of the lecture generalises from these cases. Snyder and Nakashima both look to the past — one to Native American traditions, one to traditional tools — but neither is preserving the past in the museum sense. Theirs is preservation in the living sense: values and ways of life survive because someone lives them today. The same logic dissolves the usual debates about political programmes, which the lecturer finds tedious precisely because they demand of every proposal that it work in all places for all times. He is not trying to do that. He is not writing a manifesto or bringing commandments down a mountain. Here the lecture turns to the mountain itself. Moses comes down with the tablets — but the more interesting story, he suggests, is Nietzsche's Zarathustra, who goes up the mountain, comes down to preach, and immediately sees that it will not work: "no, no, no, no, no, this is not going to work." Zarathustra changes his plan. The scale reduces. It becomes, in the lecturer's words, "so much more manageable, so much more palpable, so much more human."

That reduction of scale brings the series full circle, back to Camus. For Camus and for André Gide, resistance was not an abstraction: it was the daily question of men living under occupation, in the Algerian War's shadow, between a Stalinist left and a Nazi right, refusing both and finding that refusal cost them their friends. What makes us human, on Camus's account as the lecturer presents it, is the continual act of deciding for yourself — saying no and yes, and meaning both. The lecture closes on the consequence. Resistance is not an outcome game, because we cannot predict the future, do not control history, and have no final answer; indeed "the final answer was the final solution," and every final answer ends by requiring the death of those who disagree. What we have instead is the part within our purview. "You're not responsible for all the world for all time and all history but that doesn't mean you can't make a powerful difference." You remake your world and your relations to your world, and you speak to other people by example, by your works, by the way you inhabit the world differently. That, finally, is the joy of it: "the remaking of your life in a way that gives you a sense of power and efficacy and peace and joy" — after which, the lecturer says, you have already won.

## 2. The Questions, Pulled Out and Answered

The lecture is a spoken one, and its questions arrive the way spoken questions do — some asked outright, some carried inside assertions, some posed by the examples themselves. Pulled out and stated cleanly, they are these, each answered on its merits.

**First: are revolutions caused by individuals?** The lecture says no, and on the evidence of its own example it is mostly right. No individual caused the French Revolution; hunger, fiscal collapse, and a broken state did, and the people who rode the event were repeatedly overtaken by it. But "mostly" is the honest word. Individuals do not cause revolutions, yet particular individuals demonstrably steer them — toward the Terror or away from it, toward a republic or toward an empire. The lecturer's claim survives as a warning against the great-man picture of history, not as a denial that choices made inside a revolution matter enormously.

**Second: when a person resists, what actually changes — the world, or their relations to it?** The lecture answers: your relations, and through them yourself; the world goes on being the world. This is true at the scale of one life and incomplete at larger scales. One person's changed relations change very little of the world, but relations are the medium through which the world acts on people at all, and enough changed relations, aggregated, are what a changed world consists of. The lecture knows this — it is his knock-on effect — so the answer is: relations first, world only through them, and never on a schedule.

**Third: is every no also a yes?** This is Camus's claim, and it is the load-bearing wall of the whole series. In *The Rebel* it is stated exactly: the man who says no is also a man who says yes from his first gesture of rebellion, because a refusal draws a line, and a line implies something on the near side of it worth defending. As a piece of moral psychology this is strong: a purely negative refusal, with nothing affirmed, curdles into the nihilism Camus spent the book opposing. The answer is yes — with the condition Camus himself attached, that the yes must be discovered and honoured, not merely assumed.

**Fourth: must resistance become a political programme before it counts?** The lecture says no, and its examples are well chosen. A poem, a workshop practice, a way of farming or building can refuse the given terms of a culture as genuinely as a platform can, and can outlast platforms. But the answer needs its boundary: some evils are programmes — laws, camps, exclusions enforced by the state — and against those, only organised political action has ever sufficed. Nakashima's furniture did not close the camps; a legal and political struggle did. Resistance need not be a programme, but a world in which nobody builds programmes is a world that leaves its worst machinery running.

**Fifth: can looking backward — to older crafts, older inhabitants, older ways — be resistance rather than retreat?** Yes, when the looking is done the way Snyder and Nakashima did it: as a source of usable knowledge carried into present practice. Snyder's question treats indigenous life on a piece of ground as a record of tested, regionally specific knowledge, which it is. Nakashima treated traditional joinery the same way. It becomes retreat only when the past is used as a refuge from the present rather than a resource for it — the museum sense the lecture explicitly rejects.

**Sixth: what is Snyder's question, and why does merely asking it change the asker?** The question is: how did the natives live here, in this place, before we arrived? It changes the asker because it relocates authority. Before asking it, a person takes the importance of things from the feeds and authorities of the present; after asking it, they have admitted a second source of value, older than the market and nearer than the capital, and their relationship to their own ground is different from that moment whether or not they act on it. It is the cheapest act of resistance in the whole lecture and, the lecturer suggests, possibly the most consequential.

**Seventh: is preservation resistance, or only keeping?** On the lecture's terms, living preservation is resistance and museum preservation is not — or rather, museum preservation is a different and lesser thing, aimed at objects. A way of life preserved by being lived continues to make its claim on the present; it competes. A way of life preserved behind glass makes no claim at all. The answer accepts the distinction, with one amendment: museums and archives are sometimes the only reason anything survives to be lived again, so the two kinds of preservation are allies more often than rivals.

**Eighth: what part of Nakashima's woodworking is resistance — the lecturer asks this himself, and answers, "all of it."** The full answer is worth spelling out. The resistance is not in the furniture's beauty but in its refusal: of mass production as the only way to make things, of the tree as mere raw material, of the camp's verdict on what his life was to be. He was acted upon by the largest machinery his country possessed, and he answered in the register he controlled — how a board is cut, how a crack is honoured, what a workshop is for. That the answer became influential is a separate fact; the resistance was complete before the influence arrived.

**Ninth: if no one joins you, have you lost?** The lecture calls resistance "a no-lose proposition": if a million people join, it becomes a movement, and if no one joins, "you still transformed your life." Within the lecture's own frame this is airtight, because the frame defines winning as the remaking of your life rather than the achievement of an external outcome. The honest qualification is that some goods exist only on the far side of collective success — a law repealed, a camp closed — and for those goods a solitary transformation is not a win but a consolation. The proposition holds for the goods the lecture is actually about, and should not be stretched over the others.

**Tenth: must a position work in all places, for all times, to be worth holding?** No, and the lecture's impatience with this demand is one of its soundest moments. The demand is almost never made in good faith; it is a debating device for paralysing any particular proposal by comparing it with an imaginary universal one. Snyder's refusal to generalise from the Pacific Northwest to Iowa is not a weakness in his position but its accuracy. Regional knowledge is regional. A position that knows its own ground and its own limits is stronger, not weaker, than one that claims the world.

**Eleventh: why does the lecture treat final answers as dangerous?** Because a final answer converts disagreement from a condition of life into a problem to be eliminated, and the elimination has a logic that history has run more than once. If you possess the complete and final truth about how human beings must live, then those who refuse it are not opponents but obstructions, and obstructions get removed. The lecture's chain — final answer, then the killing of those who disagree — is compressed, but each link is attested. The safeguard he proposes is not relativism; it is the refusal of finality, the keeping of the question open as a permanent discipline.

**Twelfth: what do you do when neither camp on offer can be chosen?** This is the Camus and Gide question, and the lecture poses it in its sharpest form: choose Stalin and you have friends, choose Hitler and you have friends, refuse both and "turns out you have no friends." The answer the lecture gives is that you refuse anyway, and you do the work that remains — the writing, the editing, the daily decision — in the knowledge that the refusal itself is the human act. History has been kind to this answer: the men who refused both camps are the ones from that era whom we still read without embarrassment. But the lecture is honest that it costs, and the cost is part of the answer.

**Thirteenth: on this account, what makes us human?** The lecture's closing answer is Camus's: the continual making of the decision for yourself. Not one heroic refusal but the repeated, daily act of drawing the line and standing on the yes behind it. It is a modest anthropology and a demanding one — modest because it asks for no special nature or destiny, demanding because it permits no retirement from the act. You do not decide once. You keep deciding, and the keeping is the humanity.

## 3. The Counter-Argument

The strongest case against the lecture should be stated as strongly as it can be, because the lecture's position is attractive enough to be adopted lazily, and a position adopted lazily will fail exactly where it is most needed.

The first objection is the structural one. Private remaking leaves unjust structures untouched. The world the lecture tells us not to expect to transform includes machinery that does not care about our relations to it: laws that exclude, markets that extract, states that imprison. Nakashima's story itself proves the point from the inside. His resistance in the camp was real, but what ended the camps was not carpentry; it was the war's end, political pressure, and eventually legal reckoning and redress, all achieved by collective and institutional action of exactly the kind the lecture's framework struggles to describe. A doctrine of resistance that cannot account for the closing of the camps — only for the dignity of a man inside one — has a hole in its centre. This objection cannot be answered from within the lecture's terms, and it should be conceded as far as it goes. What can be said in reply is that the lecture never claims otherwise: it explicitly allows that changed laws are "great too," and its claim is about where a person's power actually lies day to day, not about which instruments history ultimately requires. But the concession stands: resistance as the lecture defines it is a necessary discipline of persons, not a sufficient politics for peoples.

The second objection is that the word itself dissolves. If moving to the woods is resistance, and making a table is resistance, and asking a question about watersheds is resistance, then the term covers so much that it distinguishes nothing, and a word that distinguishes nothing will eventually mean nothing. Classical resistance implies an opponent, a risk, and a cost. Much of what the lecture celebrates involved all three — Nakashima above all — but the framework as stated does not require them, and in weaker hands it becomes a honorific for any sincerely held preference. This objection can be answered in part, from Camus himself: the rebel's no is defined by the line it draws against an intrusion, and by the value affirmed at the line, which is a real test and not a mood. Where a preference draws no line and risks nothing, the lecture's own sources would not call it resistance. But it must be conceded that the lecture itself does not police this boundary, and that its rhetoric — "all of it" — invites the dilution it should guard against.

The third objection concerns who gets to resist this way. The lecture's witnesses are, without exception, people of exceptional gift, and most of them had resources: education, patrons, land that could be bought, a public that could be reached. Nakashima's road out of the camp ran through a sponsor — a famous architect who petitioned for him. Snyder's road ran through universities, publishers, and prizes. For a person without a craft anyone wants, without savings, without a Raymond to sign for them, "reorient yourself toward what you control" can sound less like liberation than like a description of their trap. This objection is partly answerable: the lecture's cheapest acts — asking Snyder's question, drawing Camus's line — cost nothing and are available in a cell as well as on a homestead, and the tradition behind the lecture includes prisoners and exiles enough to prove it. But the concession is real: the forms of resistance the lecture showcases are not equally available to everyone, and a doctrine illustrated only by the gifted and the lucky will mislead the ordinary reader about where their difficulty lies.

The fourth objection is that the joy is too easy. "You've already won" is a sentence that can console a person out of action they owed. If the remaking of one's own life is victory, then the neighbour's unremade life, and the structure that keeps it unremade, can be left in peace — and a resistance that ends in the resister's peace of mind has a suspicious resemblance to the quietism every age's comfortable people have preached to its uncomfortable ones. In reply: Camus's rebel is bound by the value his refusal reveals, and that value is never private — the slave who says no discovers something he holds in common, which is why Camus's formula runs "I rebel, therefore we are." The lecture's knock-on effect is the same thought in a lighter key. Still, the objection identifies a genuine rhetorical risk in the lecture's closing movement: the language of victory outruns the evidence offered for it, which is anecdotal — the felt influence of a movement on a region — rather than demonstrated. The joy is earned by the argument; the victory lap is not, quite.

The fifth objection is historical. The clean opposition between uncontrollable revolution and controllable resistance is a heuristic, not a law. Some revolutions were substantially steered by organised minorities, for good and ill; some acts of purely personal resistance changed nothing at all, not even the resister. The lecture's binary clarifies at the price of simplifying. This can be conceded without surrendering the point: a heuristic is judged by what it lets you see, and this one lets you see the difference between the part of history that happens to you and the part that is yours. It should be used as the lecturer uses it — as an orientation, not as a theory of history.

What survives the counter-argument is the lecture's core, and it survives in good shape: that a person's first field of action is their own relations to the world; that refusal and affirmation are one gesture; that final answers are a standing danger; and that a life lived on different terms is a real force in the world, neither omnipotent nor negligible. What does not survive is any reading of the lecture as a complete politics. It is a doctrine of the person, and it does its work at that scale. Taken at that scale, it is honest about its limits in a way its critics are not always honest about theirs.

## 4. Robert's Place in It

Three times during the lecture, Robert spoke over it, and the relay file marks his words as his. They belong in this paper because they are the point at which the doctrine stopped being the lecturer's and became an application.

The first came as the lecturer described a man reorienting himself — I can move to the woods or I can move to the city, I can do things, I can make changes, I can explore possibilities that seem important to me, and in so doing resist what I can. Robert answered with his own inventory: "translate the Quran in a bunch of different languages I can translate the Bible in a bunch of different languages irrespective of their differences right Catholic Protestant those that are non-affiliated with anything those that don't believe huh all of them make a change for the better right". The form of the sentence is the lecture's form — *I can* — and the content is his life's work, stated without ornament: translation, carried across every line the audience is divided by, on the stated ground that all of them make a change for the better.

The second came at the lecture's pivot, where the lecturer says that when you decide to address the things that are within your purview — the things you can say yes and no to — the dynamic totally changes. Robert named his instances: "The Quran project, for one. The clear English Bible is another. Yep." It is the shortest of the three interjections and the most exact. The lecturer had offered a category; Robert supplied its contents from his own desk.

The third came at the close, as the lecturer returned to Camus and the saying of no and yes. Robert spoke the yes in his own terms: "when we say yes to the Quran, and the Bible project when we say yes to embracing love inside Baba's words we become the embodiment of love right embodiments of love listen right by my hand come". The words are his, including the ones this paper does not gloss. What can be said plainly is that he heard the lecture's final claim — that the continual decision for yourself is what makes us human — and answered it with the decision he has in fact been making, daily, for years: to say yes to the work in front of him, and to let the work be the form his yes takes.

That is his place in the lecture, neither inflated nor diminished. He did not illustrate it from outside. He recognised his own practice in it while it was still playing, and said so, three times, in the plainest words he had.

## 5. Closing

A conclusion lecture earns its place if it sends its hearers back to the beginning with the terms clarified, and this one does. Revolution is what happens; resistance is what is yours. The no and the yes are one gesture. The final answer is the one answer to refuse. And the witnesses — a poet asking how the natives lived on his ground, a woodworker asking a tree what it wants to become — stand as proof that the doctrine has been lived at full stretch by people who paid full price for it. The questions it raises answer more cleanly than most lectures' questions do, and the objections it invites mark its boundary without breaking it: it is a doctrine of the person, complete at that scale, and silent — honourably silent, mostly — about the rest. What a hearer does with it is, by its own teaching, the hearer's decision, continually made. Robert has made his, and said what it is. This paper is the record of the lecture, the questions, the argument against it, and his answer — set down, as he directed, and put up on the shelf.

- **Snyder's *Turtle Island* and the Pulitzer.** The lecturer said Snyder won the Pulitzer Prize for *Turtle Island* "at some point, I forget what year that is, 70, 70 something." **VERIFIED, with the year supplied:** *Turtle Island* was published in 1974 and won the Pulitzer Prize for Poetry in 1975.
- **Snyder's essay collection, garbled in the transcript as "Earth Home, Hold."** **CORRECTED:** the book is *Earth House Hold* (1969), a collection of essays and journal fragments.
- **"Lou Welch" and "Ring of Bones."** **CORRECTED:** the poet is **Lew Welch** (1926–1971), and the book is *Ring of Bone: Collected Poems*, first published posthumously in 1973 and reissued in an expanded edition in 2012. The lecturer's guess that Welch knew Snyder is **VERIFIED**: the two were housemates at Reed College and lifelong friends, and Snyder wrote the foreword to the expanded *Ring of Bone*.
- **"Joanna Kiger," later "Joan Cuker."** **CORRECTED:** the poet is **Joanne Kyger** (1934–2017) of the San Francisco Renaissance, married to Gary Snyder from 1960 to 1964 — a member of exactly the circle the lecture describes.
- **Nakashima's origins.** The lecturer said Nakashima was "born in Washington." **VERIFIED:** George Nakashima (1905–1990) was born in Spokane, Washington.
- **Nakashima's internment and road to Pennsylvania.** The lecturer said he was interned, learned traditional Japanese carpentry in the camp, was "hired out of the internment camp," went to Pennsylvania to work on a farm, saved, bought land nearby, and opened the studio that still stands. **VERIFIED in substance, CORRECTED in mechanism:** Nakashima was interned at Minidoka, Idaho, where the Issei master carpenter Gentaro Hikogawa taught him traditional Japanese hand tools and joinery. He was not simply hired out; he was released in May 1943 on the sponsorship of Antonin Raymond, the architect who had been his employer in Tokyo before the war, and he worked on Raymond's farm at New Hope, Pennsylvania. After about a year he set up on his own nearby; the workshop complex on Aquetong Road dates from 1946 and stands today as a National Historic Landmark. The lecturer's hedged guess ("if that's correct") was correct in outline.
- **Live edge and butterfly joints.** The lecturer said he did not think Nakashima invented either but made them his signature. **VERIFIED as stated:** Nakashima popularised both; neither was his invention.
- **Nakashima's memoir, which the lecturer dated "1980, I think."** **CORRECTED:** *The Soul of a Tree: A Woodworker's Reflections* was published by Kodansha International in **1981**.
- **The Camus line.** The lecturer's refrain — that in saying no you are also saying yes — is a faithful paraphrase of the opening of Camus's *The Rebel* (1951): "What is a rebel? A man who says no, but whose refusal does not imply a renunciation. He is also a man who says yes, from the moment he makes his first gesture of rebellion." **VERIFIED as paraphrase**; the lecture does not present it as a verbatim quotation, and neither does this paper.
- **The Camus and Gide framing.** The lecturer pictures Camus and André Gide standing between a Stalinist left and a Nazi right, refusing both and losing their friends for it. **VERIFIED in substance, CORRECTED in compression:** Gide's public break with communism came with *Return from the U.S.S.R.* in 1936, before the war, and cost him his standing on the left exactly as described. Camus's refusals came in sequence, not in one scene — Resistance editor under the Nazi occupation, then the break with the Stalinist-aligned intellectual left over *The Rebel* in 1952, then his isolation over Algeria. Neither man was ever choosing Hitler; the lecture's single moment of decision compresses two decades and two different refusals into one emblematic scene. The posture is historical; the staging is rhetoric.
- **Snyder's interview answer** ("how did the natives live before we arrived"). **NOT VERIFIED as a quotation.** The lecturer himself offers it as a recollection that "has always stuck with me," unsure even of the place ("Michigan I think"). Its outlook is consistent with Snyder's published bioregional writing, but no source for the interview or its wording was found, and this paper treats it as the lecturer's recollection, not as Snyder's recorded words.
- **The novels and the poem named in passing.** **VERIFIED:** *On the Road* is Jack Kerouac's (1957); *The Electric Kool-Aid Acid Test* is Tom Wolfe's (1968); *Howl* is Allen Ginsberg's (first read in 1955; published 1956).
- **The lecturer's aside that he lived in the Pacific Northwest before moving to France.** A personal statement by an unnamed speaker. **NOT VERIFIED** — not checkable at this desk, and nothing in this paper rests on it.
