# The Varieties of Religious Experience: A Question Textbook

**Book:** *The Varieties of Religious Experience: A Study in Human Nature*
**Author:** William James (1842–1910)
**Source of the text used:** The full text of the first edition (Longmans, Green, and Co., 1902), as scanned and made freely available by the Internet Archive (the University of Toronto copy, identifier `varietiesofrelig00jameuoft`); the same text is carried by Project Gutenberg as eBook #621. The book is in the public domain.

**What this book is.** In 1901 and 1902 William James, the Harvard psychologist and philosopher, delivered the Gifford Lectures on Natural Religion at the University of Edinburgh, and this book is those twenty lectures. Instead of studying churches, creeds, or rituals, James studies individual religious experience from the inside, working from hundreds of first-hand documents — autobiographies, journals, letters, and testimonies — in which men and women describe melancholy, conversion, saintliness, and mystical states that in many cases completely transformed their lives. His method throughout is to let the cases speak first, and to judge religious experience the way he says all experience must finally be judged: not by its roots, but by its fruits.

**How to use this textbook.** Each chapter below treats one lecture. A short orientation tells you what James is doing in that lecture; the study questions then work the actual material — the cases he quotes, the distinctions he draws, and the turns his argument takes. Read the lecture first, then sit with the questions. There are no answer keys: James himself believed the questions matter more than any system built on top of them.

## Lecture I — Religion and Neurology

James opens by setting his method and disarming his critics in advance. He announces that he will study religion psychologically, through the personal documents of religious "geniuses," and he confronts the school he calls "medical materialism," which dismisses saints and prophets as epileptics, hysterics, and degenerates. His reply is the distinction on which the whole book rests: a judgment about a thing's origin is not a judgment about its value.

1. Why does James insist at the outset that he is neither a theologian, nor a historian of religions, nor an anthropologist, but only a psychologist — and what does that choice make the subject of the lectures?
2. James says the inquiry must concern "not religious institutions, but rather religious feelings and religious impulses." What is gained, and what is set aside, by that decision?
3. Why does James prefer the documents of those "most accomplished in the religious life" — religion as "an acute fever" rather than "a dull habit" — over the religion of the ordinary second-hand believer?
4. What is the difference between an *existential judgment* and a *spiritual judgment* (a *Werthurtheil*)? Give James's own illustration of the two using the higher criticism of the Bible.
5. Why can neither kind of judgment be deduced from the other, and what happens when people confuse them?
6. James describes George Fox, founder of the Quakers, as "a psychopath or détraqué of the deepest dye" and in the same breath as a mind of unsurpassed spiritual sagacity. What is James asking us to hold together in this portrait?
7. In the long passage from Fox's *Journal*, Fox walks barefoot into Lichfield crying "Wo to the bloody city of Lichfield!" What features of this episode would a medical materialist seize on, and what does Fox himself understand the episode to mean?
8. What is "medical materialism," and how does it "finish up" Saint Paul, Saint Teresa, Saint Francis of Assisi, George Fox, and Carlyle?
9. James grants that the medical materialists may be right about the bodily facts in every case. Why does he say their conclusion still does not follow?
10. "Scientific theories are organically conditioned just as much as religious emotions are." How does this sentence turn the medical materialist's weapon back on itself?
11. What does the case of "Philip drunk and Philip sober" show about the two criteria by which we judge states of mind — immediate delight, and good consequential fruits for life?
12. James quotes Moreau ("Genius is but one of the many branches of the neuropathic tree"), Lombroso, and Nisbet on genius as disease. Why do even these authors refuse to condemn the works of genius — and what does their refusal prove?
13. What are the only three criteria James finally accepts for judging a religious opinion or state of mind? (Look for "immediate luminousness," "philosophical reasonableness," and "moral helpfulness.")
14. Dr. Maudsley, the cleverest of James's opponents, ends by admitting that "not its origin, but the way in which it works on the whole, is… the final test of a belief." Why does James treat this concession as decisive?
15. "By their fruits ye shall know them, not by their roots." How do Jonathan Edwards's *Treatise on Religious Affections* and Saint Teresa's defense of her own visions both work out this same empiricist criterion?
16. Why, then, does James still insist on studying the pathological aspects of religion at all? What do insane conditions let us see that healthy conditions hide, and why does he compare them to the scalpel and the microscope?
17. In the closing pages James argues that the psychopathic temperament, joined to a superior intellect, may be "the best possible condition" for effective genius — quoting Annie Besant's contrast between "Some one ought to do it, but why should I?" and "Some one ought to do it, so why not I?" What qualities does this temperament contribute that the "robust Philistine type of nervous system" lacks?
18. Sit with this: is there a belief or experience of your own that you have dismissed — in yourself or in another person — purely because of where it came from? What would change if you judged it by James's three criteria instead?

## Lecture II — Circumscription of the Topic

Having cleared the ground, James now draws the boundary of what he will study. He refuses to define the *essence* of religion — "religion" is a collective name, he argues, and there is no single "religious emotion" — so instead he arbitrarily circumscribes a field: personal religion, the individual's solitary relation to whatever he considers the divine, where "divine" must be taken broadly enough to include godless systems, and where the response is always solemn and grave.

1. Why does James refuse to begin, as most books on the philosophy of religion do, with a precise definition of religion's essence? What does his comparison with the word "government" show?
2. James says there is no single elementary "religious emotion": religious love is ordinary love directed to a religious object, religious fear ordinary fear, religious awe the same thrill we feel in a forest at twilight. If that is so, what makes an emotion *religious* at all?
3. What is the great partition James draws across the religious field — institutional religion on one side, personal religion on the other — and which side does he choose for these lectures? Why?
4. James claims personal religion is "more fundamental than either theology or ecclesiasticism," because the founders of every church — Christ, the Buddha, Mahomet, and the originators of sects — owed their power to direct personal communion with the divine. Do you find that argument convincing? What does it leave out?
5. Write out James's working definition of religion for these lectures, word for word. Then take it apart: why "in their solitude"? Why "whatever they may consider the divine"?
6. Why must "divine" be interpreted broadly enough to cover Buddhism, which James calls atheistic in strictness, and Emerson's transcendentalism? What do the experience and response in such godless or quasi-godless creeds share with the Christian appeal and response?
7. In the long passage James quotes from Emerson's 1838 Divinity School Address, what is worshipped, if not a personal God? Why did the address cause a scandal?
8. James tests an extremely broad definition — religion as a man's "total reaction upon life" — and quotes his colleague on the fervent atheist: "He believes in No-God, and he worships him." Why does James finally reject this broad definition as inconvenient?
9. Why are Voltaire's laughing defiance at seventy-three and Renan's systematic irony (*je m'en fichisme*, "all is vanity") *not* religion for James, even though they are total reactions upon life? What single phrase does he offer as religion's universal message instead?
10. James narrows the definition once more: the divine must be a primal reality "the individual feels impelled to respond to solemnly and gravely, and neither by a curse nor a jest." Why do Schopenhauer and Nietzsche also fail this test, in his judgment?
11. James admits the boundaries of his field are "always misty" — things are only *more or less* divine, religious, total. Why does he say hesitation about whether a state of mind is religious arises only when the state is "weakly characterized"?
12. Frederick Locker-Lampson's gentle, resigned *Confidences* passage is offered as a borderline case. Why does James set it aside as "too insignificant for our instruction," and what principle of study does this repeat from Lecture I?
13. "I accept the universe," Margaret Fuller is reported to have said; Carlyle's comment was, "Gad! she'd better!" What is the real subject of both morality and religion, according to James, and how does the *manner* of our acceptance divide them?
14. Compare the acceptances James sets side by side: Marcus Aurelius, the author of the *Theologia Germanica*, and the *Imitation of Christ*. What emotional difference — "like that between an arctic climate and the tropics" — does James find between the Stoic's agreement and the Christian's?
15. The moralist's "athletic attitude" — holding his breath, keeping his muscles tense — must eventually break down. What does the helpless person actually crave, and what state of mind does religion supply in which fear is "not held in abeyance… it is positively expunged and washed away"?
16. James corrects Havelock Ellis, who identified religion with every form of happiness: why is religious happiness not a mere "relief" or escape from evil? What does Guido Reni's St. Michael, with his foot on Satan's neck, illustrate about the religious consciousness?
17. James ends by foreshadowing his conclusion: the religious attitude will prove to be "both a helpless and a sacrificial attitude," and "religion thus makes easy and felicitous what in any case is necessary." What does the Goethe couplet he quotes — "Entbehren sollst du! sollst entbehren!" ("Thou shalt renounce!") — add to this?
18. Sit with this: which of the attitudes in this lecture is closest to your own total reaction upon life — the moralist's tense effort, Voltaire's laughter, Renan's irony, the Stoic's cold consent, or the saint's glad surrender? What would have to change for it to become, in James's sense, religious?

## Lecture III — The Reality of the Unseen

James now turns to the object of religion: an unseen order, believed in though it cannot be seen. His psychological claim is that human beings possess a "sense of reality" — a feeling of objective presence, of "something there" — deeper and more general than any of the five senses, and that this feeling can attach itself to abstract ideas as powerfully as to sensible things. The lecture closes with his case against rationalism: articulate reasons convince us only when our inarticulate feelings of reality have already been won.

1. How does James characterize the life of religion "in the broadest and most general terms possible" at the head of this lecture? What two things make up the religious attitude in the soul?
2. Why can ideas of things not present to the senses move us *more* strongly than sensible presences? What homely examples does James give (the remembered insult, the blunder we are ashamed of afterwards)?
3. Kant held that words like "God," "soul," and "immortality" have no distinctive sense-content and are theoretically "devoid of any significance," yet make a genuine difference in practice. Why does James call this "a mind believing with all its strength in the real presence of a set of things of no one of which it can form any notion whatsoever" — and why does he find the phenomenon instructive rather than absurd?
4. What does the image of the bar of iron endowed with "an inner capacity for magnetic feeling" show about the sentiment of reality?
5. How do Plato's doctrine of Ideas and Emerson's worship of abstract divineness extend the same point? Why can "Science" itself, or the ethical societies' moral law, become the object of a genuinely religious attitude?
6. State James's central hypothesis in this lecture: "It is as if there were in the human consciousness a sense of reality, a feeling of objective presence, a perception of what we may call 'something there,' more deep and more general than any of the special 'senses.'" What follows from it for how religious conceptions get believed "in spite of criticism"?
7. James's intimate friend describes the "consciousness of a presence" that came into his college rooms on successive nights in September 1884 — first as horror and abhorrence, "more surely" known than any fleshly creature. What makes this case a "curious proof" of the undifferentiated sense of reality rather than a perception by any ordinary sense?
8. The same friend later had the sense of presence "filled with a quality of joy" — "the sure knowledge of the close presence of a sort of mighty person." Why does James note, with interest, that his friend does *not* interpret these experiences theistically? What does that show about the feeling itself, apart from any interpretation laid upon it?
9. What do the shorter cases add — the narrator in the *Journal of the Society for Psychical Research* who knew his friend A. H. stood at his elbow; the man wakened by a "spiritual presence"; Professor Flournoy's lady whose automatic writing came with a feeling of foreign presence; and the blind man who "saw" a gray-bearded figure in a pepper-and-salt suit squeeze under his door?
10. Against this sense of reality James sets its negative counterpart, the feeling of unreality, quoting Madame Ackermann ("I have been dreaming"). Why does he promise that in morbid melancholy this feeling can become "a carking pain"?
11. The scientific man of James's acquaintance describes his lifelong relation to a "fundamental cosmical It" — an "unfailing fountain of living justice, truth, and strength" — and its gradual loss by the age of nearly fifty: "there was no electric current. A blank was there instead of It." Why does James call this a *negative* example, and what does it prove about faith being "something more like a sensation than an intellectual operation"?
12. Contrast the two vivid testimonies that follow: James Russell Lowell's memorandum ("The whole room seemed to me full of God") and the clergyman's hilltop experience from Starbuck's collection ("I could not any more have doubted that He was there than that I was. Indeed, I felt myself to be, if possible, the less real of the two"). What is common to both, and why can no later argument shake faith founded this way?
13. The Swiss tramp on the Buet describes four or five minutes of ecstasy in perfect health and "a state of equilibrium," insisting that "God had neither form, color, odor, nor taste." Why does James include a case with every pathological explanation carefully excluded by the witness himself?
14. Rationalism demands four things of any belief: statable abstract principles, definite facts of sensation, definite hypotheses, and definite inferences logically drawn. Why does James say the part of our mental life rationalism can account for is "relatively superficial," and that it "will fail to convince or convert you all the same, if your dumb intuitions are opposed to its conclusions"?
15. "In the metaphysical and religious sphere, articulate reasons are cogent for us only when our inarticulate feelings of reality have already been impressed in favor of the same conclusion." What does James mean by "Instinct leads, intelligence does but follow"? And why is he careful to add that he is pointing out a fact, not yet saying it is *better* that the non-rational should hold primacy?
16. James closes by opposing the sombre religious temper (the Book of Job; Mark Rutherford: lie low, "for you are in the hands of a living God") to the sanguine one (Professor Seeley's "habitual and regulated admiration"; Havelock Ellis's laughter). Why does he refuse to leave out either the sadness or the gladness, and why does he make religious optimism — healthy-mindedness — the theme of the next two lectures?
17. Sit with this: have you ever *known* something was present, or true, before you could give any reason for it? Did reasons later confirm it, overturn it, or simply fail to touch it either way?

## Lecture IV — The Religion of Healthy-Mindedness (I)

James turns to religious optimism. Healthy-mindedness is the tendency to look on all things and see that they are good; in its involuntary form it is a congenital temperament, in its systematic form a deliberate policy of excluding evil from one's field of vision. This lecture portrays the *once-born* — souls whose religion is union with the divine from the outset, with no antecedent burden to be delivered from — and follows healthy-mindedness into liberal Christianity and the religion of evolutionary science.

1. "How to gain, how to keep, how to recover happiness, is in fact for most men at all times the secret motive of all they do." How does this lead ordinary men to treat the happiness a creed affords as a proof of its truth — "Such a belief ought to be true; therefore it is true"? What does James call this inference?
2. What marks the person in whom happiness is "congenital and irreclaimable"? Why does James quote St. Augustine's maxim *Dilige et quod vis fac* — "if you but love [God], you may do as you incline" — in describing this company of spirits?
3. Francis W. Newman says God has "two families of children on this earth, the once-born and the twice-born." From Newman's description, what do the once-born see when they look at God, at themselves, and at human sin? Why are they *not* self-righteous, though never distressed by their own imperfections?
4. Theodore Parker's letters show the type inside Protestantism: the heathen classics show no "consciousness of sin" — "God be thanked for it." What does Parker put in the place of the consciousness of sin?
5. Dr. Edward Everett Hale, answering one of Dr. Starbuck's circulars, says he never knew "for an hour" what religious struggles are. What does Hale claim a child gains who is early taught that he is God's child rather than "born the child of wrath"?
6. Why does James say that in some individuals optimism becomes "quasi-pathological," with the capacity for even transient sadness "cut off from them as by a kind of congenital anaesthesia"?
7. Dr. Bucke's portrait of Walt Whitman shows a man who, in Bucke's long observation, never complained, never swore, never exhibited fear, and "never spoke deprecatingly of any nationality or class of men... nor any of the results of those laws, such as illness, deformity, and death." What does James say Whitman *owes his importance in literature* to, and how does his first-person voice differ from mere monstrous conceit?
8. Why does James deny that Whitman is a "pagan" in either sense of the word? Use the passage from *Song of Myself* ("They do not sweat and whine about their condition") and Achilles' speech to Lycaon in the *Iliad*: what did the Greeks keep unmingled that Whitman refuses to adopt, and why does James call Whitman's optimism "too voluntary and defiant"?
9. Distinguish involuntary from systematic healthy-mindedness. In its systematic form, good is taken as "the essential and universal aspect of being" and evil is deliberately excluded from the field of vision. Why is this less intellectually dishonest than it first sounds?
10. "Refuse to admit their badness; despise their power; ignore their presence; turn your attention the other way." Much evil, James argues, is due to the way men take the phenomenon, and can be converted into "a bracing and tonic good" by a change of inner attitude from fear to fight. How far does this argument honestly run, and where would you expect it to break down?
11. Why does James call the advance of liberalism in Christianity over the past fifty years "a victory of healthy-mindedness within the church over the morbidness with which the old hell-fire theology was more harmoniously related"? What changes in preaching does he point to?
12. The anonymous respondent to Starbuck's circular, aged sixty-seven, declares religion "means nothing" to him, calls sin "a condition, a disease," and expects that a million years hence no one will have any idea of evil or sin. Why does James present this coarse, self-satisfied evolutionist as an excellent example of the optimism encouraged by popular science — and what does he mean by saying the man's contentment "incases him like a lobster-shell"?
13. Sit with this: which parts of your own outlook are temperament — things you could not feel otherwise if you tried — and which parts are policy, deliberately adopted? Does the distinction change how you judge either?

## Lecture V — The Religion of Healthy-Mindedness (II): Mind-Cure

The second healthy-mindedness lecture studies the mind-cure movement — the "New Thought" — which James calls a deliberately optimistic scheme of life and a genuine religious power. Its pillar is man's dual nature: a shallower self ruled by fear, and a profounder self already one with the Divine through the subconscious. James closes by comparing mind-cure's claim to verification with science's own: both, he argues, are genuine keys that unlock different parts of the world's treasure-house.

1. James lists the doctrinal sources of mind-cure: the four Gospels, Emersonianism, Berkeleyan idealism, spiritism, optimistic popular-science evolutionism, and Hinduism. But what does he call its *most characteristic* feature — the direct inspiration its leaders actually had?
2. What practical evidence does James marshal for taking the movement seriously — the blind made to see, lifelong invalids restored, regeneration of character — and why does he say we can afford to overlook its failures, self-deceptions, and "moonstruck" verbiage?
3. "The plain fact remains that the spread of the movement has been due to practical fruits." Why does James call mind-cure America's "only decidedly original contribution to the systematic philosophy of life," and why does Dr. Goddard's hostile-critical study of faith cures end up supporting rather than refuting him?
4. State the movement's foundation: man's dual nature, a shallower sphere of fleshly sensation and egotism and a profounder sphere. Where Christianity has called the lower nature's essential vice *frowardness*, what do the mind-curers call its mark of the beast — and how does Horace Fletcher's coinage "fearthought" (with "worry" as fearthought, and fearthought as "the self-imposed or self-permitted suggestion of inferiority") sharpen the doctrine?
5. Dresser's catalogue runs from expected children's diseases and the fear of death down to the dread of the east wind and of hay-fever "upon the 14th of August in the middle of the day." What is the writers' point in piling up so long a list of anticipations? What does Henry Wood add about the ancestors who lived under this "perpetual nightmare"?
6. How does the mind-cure doctrine of man's higher nature differ from the ordinary Christian one? In Trine's words, what is "the great central fact of the universe," and what is "the great central fact in human life"? What does the image of attaching "the belts of our machinery to the powerhouse of the Universe" claim?
7. The first woman correspondent had been bedridden for years with spine and lower limbs paralyzed, and afterwards worked as a healer fourteen years without a vacation, asserting she never knew "a moment of fatigue or pain." What single fact does she name as "the first underlying cause of all sickness, weakness, or depression," and what as its cure?
8. The second correspondent recovered from nervous prostration and insomnia only when "this New Thought took possession of me." Why does she insist that bodily health "cannot be found by any special mental act or desire to have it," but comes only as an incidental result — "added unto you"? What does she say we commonly make the *object* of life that should instead be a *result*?
9. The third case is the most concrete: a sufferer from childhood to her fortieth year hears, one October afternoon, the words "You will be healed and do a work you never dreamed of," and on January 7, 1881, visits a mental healer. Trace her five stages — from "I am Soul, therefore it is well with me" through the visions of the beast with her own face, which she refuses to acknowledge, to the conviction "that I was perfectly well and always had been." What is the "perfect and completed separation" she reaches, and why did full expression of health still take "two years of hard work"?
10. Why does James say it is "impossible not to class mind-cure as primarily a religious movement," and how does its doctrine of oneness compare with the interpretation of Christ's message defended by Scottish philosophers like the Cairds?
11. Mind-curers, James notes, offer no speculative explanation of evil at all: "Don't reason about it, as Dante says, but give a glance and pass beyond!" — evil is *Avidya*, ignorance, to be outgrown. Christian Science under Mrs. Eddy goes furthest: "evil is simply a lie." Why does James call this "a bad speculative omission" yet "intimately linked with the practical merits of the system"?
12. "Thoughts are things." What doctrine about thought-forces and the law that like attracts like do the mind-cure writers build on the slogans "Pessimism leads to weakness. Optimism leads to power"?
13. James draws a striking parallel: to the anxious question "What shall I do to be saved?" Luther and Wesley answered, "You are saved now, if you would but believe it." What is the mind-curers' precisely similar word of emancipation, and why does James think the *adequacy of the message to the mental needs* of its hearers explains the force of all three movements?
14. Against official moralism — "hold your will like a bow always bent" — James sets the anti-moralistic method: "Passivity, not activity; relaxation, not intentness, should be now the rule." Why does conscious effort make some people "twofold more the children of hell they were before," and what must happen inside — "something must give way, a native hardness must break down and liquefy" — for the surrender to work?
15. What is the revivalist preachers' story of the man clinging to the branch over the precipice meant to prove? He fell "just six inches." How does James use it to state the psychology of "the everlasting arms"?
16. James insists the regenerative phenomena of letting go "remain firm facts of human nature" under three rival explanations — theistic grace, pantheistic merging of the private self into the greater Self, and medical materialism. What are the three explanations, and why does he refuse to choose between them here?
17. Why does James call "suggestion" a wet blanket on investigation? An idea, to be suggestive, "must come to the individual with the force of a revelation." What made mind-cure a revelation to people "whose hearts the church Christianity had left hardened," and what does James predict will happen to its suggestive efficacy if mind-cure ever becomes "official, respectable, and intrenched"?
18. Mind-cure made "an unprecedented great use of the subconscious life" — silent meditation, "entering the silence," the office worker who drew "the curtains of privacy" about him in a noisy room. Why does James ask wherein this differs from the Catholic practice of recollection, the practice of the presence of God as Alvarez de Paz defines it?
19. The closing argument: the positivist says science supplanted primitive personal thought by experimental verification. Mind-cure "sets up an exactly identical claim": "Live as if I were true, she says, and every day will practically prove you right." Using the two short appendix cases (the sprained ankle walked on for two miles; the woman who "lay down in the stream of life" and woke well), explain why James concludes that science and religion are "both of them genuine keys for unlocking the world's treasure-house," neither exhaustive nor exclusive of the other. What does he mean by calling the claims of the sectarian scientist "premature"?
20. Sit with this: is there a fear in your own life that functions the way Fletcher says fearthought does — a "self-imposed or self-permitted suggestion of inferiority"? What would the mind-cure prescription actually ask you to *do* about it, and what part of that prescription seems to you sound, and what part evasive?

## Lecture VI — The Sick Soul (I)

James now sets against healthy-mindedness its radical opposite: the view that the evil aspects of our life are of its very essence, and that the world's meaning comes home to us when we lay them most to heart. Before descending into pathological melancholy, he pauses on the philosophy of evil — monism cannot house evil without making God its author, while pluralism can treat evil as an alien element to be sloughed off — and then begins the evidence of the sick soul: precarious happiness, failure, and the perishing of all natural goods.

1. How does James summarize the healthy-minded settlement with evil at the head of this lecture — and what does Spinoza add to it? Why does Spinoza "categorically condemn repentance," and what does he say we can always do better than by "worry of conscience and remorse"?
2. James finds a healthy-minded doctrine of repentance even inside Christianity. How do Catholic confession and absolution function, in one of their aspects, as "a systematic method of keeping healthy-mindedness on top"? And how does Luther — no healthy-minded man — come to a healthy-minded view of repentance through Staupitz's confession, "I have vowed unto God above a thousand times that I would become a better man: but I never performed that which I vowed"? What does Luther call this "a godly and a holy desperation"?
3. Molinos, the Quietist founder condemned partly for his opinion of repentance, advises the soul that falls to get up at once like the runner fallen at tournament. What are "the weapons" he gives — and why does he say the enemy's whole strategy is to make a giant of your misery?
4. State the philosophical difficulty James now raises: if theism hardens into a monistic, pantheistic system in which God is All-in-All, what must be true of evil? Why is there "no clear or easy issue" from this puzzle — and what is the "only obvious escape" James names?
5. Why does absolute idealism (Hegel: "everything actual is rational") have to pin evil in, consecrate it, and award it a function in the final system? Contrast the healthy-minded vote: what does it call evil, and what does it say the ideal is, relative to the whole actual?
6. James asks us not to forget the notion of elements of the universe that make "no rational whole in conjunction with the other elements" — "so much 'dirt,' as it were, and matter out of place." Why does he predict "we shall have to admit it ourselves in the end as containing an element of truth"?
7. James distinguishes two levels of evil for the morbid mind: evil as mere mal-adjustment of the self with its environment, curable on the natural plane; and evil as "a wrongness or vice in his essential nature," requiring a supernatural remedy. How does he distribute these between the Latin and Germanic races — sins in the plural versus Sin with a capital S — and which way of feeling does he call the more instructive for our study?
8. Explain the threshold metaphor: pain-threshold, fear-threshold, misery-threshold. What does James mean by men who "seem to have started in life with a bottle or two of champagne inscribed to their credit," while others are "born close to the pain-threshold"? What question about the relativity of religions to types of need does this raise?
9. "A chain is no stronger than its weakest link, and life is after all a chain." Why is even the happiest healthy-mindedness left with "an irremediable sense of precariousness" — "a bell with a crack"? And why does the reflecting happy man find his escape "just a lucky chance and no essential difference"?
10. James claims that in nine cases out of ten the most envied man's inmost consciousness is one of failure. How do Goethe's words of 1824 ("during the whole of my 75 years, I have not had four weeks of genuine well-being") and the aged Luther's ("rather than live forty years more, I would give up my chance of Paradise") serve as his star witnesses? What does Stevenson add: "we are not intended to succeed; failure is the fate allotted"?
11. In James's footnote, why does the experience of failure generate the very *need of a God*? What is the "residuum of worth" that the world, dealing with us *in actu* and not *in posse*, never takes account of — and why can "only by an All-knower can we finally be judged"?
12. The second stage of world-sickness spoils the successful moments themselves: "All natural goods perish. Riches take wings; fame is a breath; love is a cheat." How does Ecclesiastes voice this stage, and why does James conclude, "We need a life not correlated with death, a health not liable to illness, a kind of good that will not perish"?
13. Why is healthy-mindedness's only answer at this depth — "Cheer up, old fellow, you'll be all right erelong, if you will only drop your morbidness!" — not, in all seriousness, a rational answer? What does James call the ascription of religious value to mere happy-go-lucky contentment?
14. "The lustre of the present hour is always borrowed from the background of possibilities it goes with." Explain this through James's two pictures: the old man who learns his fate from the doctors, and mankind living on a frozen lake that is slowly melting, with cliffs on every side and no escape. Why does naturalism's background turn the thrill into "an anxious trembling"?
15. James corrects the literary myth of Greek joyousness: even Homer's reflective passages are cheerless, and systematic Greek thought became "unmitigated pessimist." What do the Epicurean and Stoic counsels respectively say, and why does James call both "in its degree a philosophy of despair in nature's boons" — expedients marking "the conclusion of what we call the once-born period"?
16. Sit with this: when your own happiness has been interrupted by the thought that it cannot last, which of James's stages were you in — precariousness, failure, or the perishing of the good itself? Did the thought feel like morbidness, or like seeing clearly?

## Lecture VII — The Sick Soul (II): Melancholy, Tolstoy, Bunyan

The second sick-soul lecture descends into pathological melancholy proper: anhedonia, active anguish, and panic fear. Its great documents are Father Gratry's youthful desolation, Tolstoy's *Confession*, Bunyan's *Grace Abounding*, Henry Alline's journal, and the case of panic fear James prints from a French correspondent — a case long recognized as drawn from James's own experience, though he presents it as another's. The lecture ends with James's verdict that morbid-mindedness "ranges over the wider scale of experience," and that the completest religions are religions of deliverance.

1. Why must the individual, for the extremity of pessimism to be reached, "in his own person become the prey of a pathological melancholy"? What is the symmetry James draws between the melancholic and the healthy-minded enthusiast — what is each forced, in spite of himself, to ignore?
2. Professor Ribot's word for passive joylessness is *anhedonia*. How do his two cases show it — the young girl with liver disease who could feel no affection and no pleasure in her doll, and Esquirol's magistrate, to whom his home and absent children appealed "as little... as a theorem of Euclid"? Why does James mention that prolonged seasickness produces the same condition temporarily?
3. Father Gratry, broken by isolation and excessive study at the Polytechnic, woke at night fearing the Panthéon was tumbling on the school — and worse, found every idea of heaven taken away: "Happiness, joy, light, affection, love — all these words were now devoid of sense." What made this second loss "my great and inconsolable grief," beyond the terrors themselves?
4. The letter from the patient in a French asylum ("fear, atrocious fear, presses me down, holds me without respite") teaches James two things. What are they — and why does *querulousness* of mind tend toward irreligion and play "no part whatever in the construction of religious systems"? What different mood must religious melancholy be cast in?
5. Before quoting Tolstoy, James argues that facts are compatible with opposite emotional comments: "Whatever of value, interest, or meaning our respective worlds may appear endued with are thus pure gifts of the spectator's mind." Use his images — love transforming Mont Blanc at sunrise, the stage-setting under alternating colored lights — to explain why value cannot be reasoned back once withdrawn. What is "the practically real world" for each of us?
6. How do the melancholiacs quoted from Dumas describe their changed world — "I see everything through a cloud"; "Persons move like shadows"; "I weep false tears, I have unreal hands"? Why does James say this strangeness sets up "an urgent wondering and questioning" in some sufferers, driving them toward a religious solution?
7. Tolstoy at about fifty began to have moments of "arrest," as if he knew not "how to live." Lay out his outward circumstances at the time — wife, children, property, fame, health, strength — and his inward state. Why does James stress that Tolstoy was "neither insane nor ill"? What does the discrepancy prove about the malady?
8. Retell Tolstoy's oriental fable: the traveler in the well, the beast above, the dragon below, the white mouse and the black mouse gnawing the bush, the drops of honey. What does each element stand for, and why could Tolstoy no longer suck the honey? "One can live only so long as one is intoxicated, drunk with life" — what does sobriety reveal?
9. Tolstoy sought an answer "in all the branches of knowledge acquired by men... like a man who is lost and seeks to save himself — and I found nothing." What four ways does he find men of his class meeting the situation, and why is suicide "the consistent course dictated by the logical intellect"? What, in his own words, kept him from the deed — "a consciousness of life," and a "thirst for God" that ran "direct contrary" to the movement of his ideas?
10. Why does James say that after disillusionment this deep "there is seldom a restitutio ad integrum" — that the happiness which returns "is not the simple ignorance of ill," but a redemption in which natural evil is "swallowed up in supernatural good," and the sufferer is saved "by what seems to him a second birth"?
11. Bunyan's trouble, unlike Tolstoy's, was not the meaning of life in general but "the condition of his own personal self." Describe his psychopathic symptoms as James lists them — the Scripture texts buffeting his mind "like a shuttlecock," the sore conscience that "durst not take a pin or stick, though but so big as a straw." Why did the people's telling him of the Promises do him no more good than bidding him "reach the Sun with my finger"?
12. Bunyan writes: "I was more loathsome in my own eyes than was a toad... I could have changed heart with anybody... If I would have given a thousand pounds for a tear, I could not shed one." What added to his sorrow most — that he could not even *desire* deliverance? And why do both Bunyan and Henry Alline envy the beasts — "gladly would I have been in the condition of the dog or horse"? What does Alline add: the earth "dressed in mourning," his sins seemingly known to everyone he met?
13. The worst form is panic fear. In the French correspondent's case, an evening visit to a dressing-room in the twilight fuses with the image of the epileptic idiot in the asylum — "That shape am I, I felt, potentially" — and "something hitherto solid within my breast gave way entirely." What was the experience *like a revelation* of, and what did the sufferer say kept him from real insanity? Why does James ask us to note that his cheerful mother's "unconsciousness of danger" seemed to him "a perfect paradox"?
14. James sorts the three documents: "One of them gives us the vanity of mortal things; another the sense of sin; and the remaining one describes the fear of the universe." Which is which? Why are these, he says, the three ways "man's original optimism and self-satisfaction get leveled with the dust"?
15. "Here is the real core of the religious problem: Help! help!" Why must deliverance "come in as strong a form as the complaint, if it is to take effect" — and why may that mean "the coarser religions, revivalistic, orgiastic, with blood and miracles and supernatural operations, may possibly never be displaced"?
16. State James's verdict on the quarrel between the two ways of viewing life: why is morbid-mindedness's survey "the one that overlaps," and healthy-mindedness "inadequate as a philosophical doctrine" even where it works? Yet what concession does he make to healthy-mindedness "within the sphere of its successful operation"? And why does he end by naming Buddhism and Christianity "essentially religions of deliverance: the man must die to an unreal life before he can be born into the real life"?
17. Sit with this: Tolstoy's questions — "Why should I live? Why should I do anything? Is there in life any purpose which the inevitable death which awaits me does not undo and destroy?" — are, he says, in the soul of every human being. Have they ever visited you in the small hours? What answered them, if anything did — an argument, a feeling, a person, or simply morning?

## Lecture VIII — The Divided Self, and the Process of Its Unification

Behind the sick soul lies a psychological fact: the divided self. Some temperaments are harmonious from the outset; others are heterogeneous, a battleground of incompatible impulses, and their normal growth is the slow straightening-out and unification of the inner life. James shows the division in Daudet, Annie Besant, Augustine, and Henry Alline, then insists that unification — conversion — is a general psychological process that can run in any direction, religious or away from religion, gradual (*lysis*) or sudden (*crisis*).

1. Contrast the two conceptions of the universe James draws at the head of this lecture: the once-born religion's "one-storied affair," whose accounts are kept in one denomination, and the twice-born religion's "double-storied mystery." Why, in the second, can peace *not* be reached "by the simple addition of pluses and elimination of minuses from life"? What is the falsity that "lurks in the very being" of natural good?
2. What is the psychological basis James assigns to the twice-born character — and how does Alphonse Daudet's "Homo duplex, homo duplex!" illustrate it? Daudet's second self, discovered at his brother Henri's deathbed when he was fourteen, could never be intoxicated, made to shed tears, or put to sleep. Why does James call this duality "horrible"?
3. Annie Besant's self-portrait is James's example of an *innocent* heterogeneity: the doughty platform combatant who at home shrank from reproving a servant and would go without what she wanted rather than ring for the waiter. What does the case show about how a person can be "a coward at heart in private while a good fighter in public" without being a hypocrite?
4. James says the extreme examples of heterogeneous personality appear in the psychopathic temperament, and instances Bunyan's obsession — "Sell Christ for this, sell him for that, sell him, sell him!" running a hundred times together until, out of breath with retorting "I will not," he impulsively said "Let him go if he will." Why did that one momentary loss of the battle keep him in despair for over a year, and with what does James say the phenomenon "connects itself"?
5. "In all of us... does the normal evolution of character chiefly consist in the straightening out and unifying of the inner self." Why is unhappiness "apt to characterize the period of order-making and struggle," and what form does it take in a person "of tender conscience and religiously quickened"? Quote St. Paul's line James uses for it.
6. Augustine's garden crisis is famous — the voice saying "Sume, lege" ("take and read") — but James quotes the *Confessions* for the analysis of the divided will that precedes it: "these two wills, one old, one new, one carnal, the other spiritual, contended with each other and disturbed my soul." What is Augustine's devastating self-observation about his "Presently; yes, presently; wait a little while"? And why was he "afraid thou wouldst hear me too soon, and heal me at once of my disease of lust, which I wished to satiate rather than to see extinguished"?
7. James comments that Augustine's higher wishes lacked "just that last acuteness, that touch of explosive intensity, of dynamogenic quality... that enables them to burst their shell." Keep this phrase in mind: how will it matter when James turns to sudden conversion?
8. Henry Alline's divided will ran on harmless sins — "frolicking and carnal mirth" — yet produced misery as real as Augustine's. Trace the cycle: esteem of young company, mirth, guilt, hours of begging forgiveness, relapse at the first music and glass of wine. Why did Alline call himself a hypocrite for feigning a merry heart in company, and what does James note about the disproportion between the smallness of the sins and the greatness of the distress?
9. James now makes a methodological turn: "to find religion is only one out of many ways of reaching unity." List the other directions a "new birth" can take — away from religion into incredulity, from scrupulosity into license, and by the irruption of love, ambition, cupidity, revenge, patriotic devotion. What is identical in all these instances, psychologically?
10. Jouffroy's "counter-conversion" — the December night when the veil concealing his own incredulity was torn — mirrors religious conversion point for point, down to the sadness at lost illusions: "in the depth of my mind nothing was left that stood erect." Why does James place this memorial beside the religious cases? What does the parallel prove about the *form* of the event, whatever its content?
11. John Foster's case of the sudden conversion to avarice: a ruined young spendthrift sits for hours above his lost estates, springs up "with a vehement, exulting emotion," shovels coals for his first pence, and dies an inveterate miser worth £60,000. Why does James find this grotesque story "illustrative enough to quote" in a lecture on religious unification?
12. In the footnotes, Tolstoy's story of S. — who at twenty-six, on a hunting expedition, heard his brother say only "Do you still keep up that thing?" and never prayed again in thirty years — is James's example of how small a stimulus suffices "when the process of preparation and incubation has proceeded far enough." What two images does James use for this (the leaning wall; the supersaturated fluid)? And how does the footnote case of the man who fell *out* of love in a single morning — burning the letters, crushing the ambrotypes under his heel "in a sort of fierce joy" — show "two different levels of personality" resolving "not gradually, but in a sudden crisis"?
13. Horace Fletcher's conversion is to healthy-mindedness itself: the words "get rid, get rid" of anger and worry work on him overnight, and he wakes asking, "If it is possible to get rid of anger and worry, why is it necessary to have them at all?" — "The baby had discovered that it could walk." Why does James say this case shows "how, when the fruit is ripe, a touch will make it fall"? Note Fletcher's evidence afterwards: ten thousand miles of travel without a single felt incivility, the missed train, the porter who refused his fee next day.
14. Medicine speaks of recovery by *lysis* (gradual) and by *crisis* (abrupt). Tolstoy's unification was gradual: explain his reasoning that he had been looking for the value of one finite term in another, getting only "0=0," and that faith must bring in the infinite — "Faith is the sense of life... the force whereby we live." What settled conviction took him two years to reach, and what did the morning in the spring forest ("To acknowledge God and to live are one and the same thing. God is what life is") complete?
15. Bunyan's recovery was slower still — peace "in and out twenty times a day," good texts holding for "two or three hours," the periods accumulating until "my chains fell off my legs indeed." What does James mean by calling Tolstoy's melancholy "logically called for by the clash between his inner character and his outer activities and aims" — "a case of heterogeneous personality tardily and slowly finding its unity and level"?
16. "But neither Bunyan nor Tolstoy could become what we have called healthy-minded. They had drunk too deeply of the cup of bitterness ever to forget its taste." How is sadness "preserved as a minor ingredient in the heart of the faith by which it was overcome" in each man — Tolstoy implacable toward fashionable life and empire to the end, Bunyan passing "a sentence of death upon everything that can properly be called a thing of this life," even his wife and his poor blind child?
17. Sit with this: where in your own life is the division — the wish that convinces but does not move, Augustine's "presently" that had no present? If unification came, would you expect it by lysis or by crisis — and which would you trust more?

## Lecture IX — Conversion (I)

James defines conversion as the process, gradual or sudden, by which a self hitherto divided, and consciously wrong, inferior, and unhappy, becomes unified and consciously right, superior, and happy, in consequence of its firmer hold upon religious realities. Psychologically, it is a shift of the habitual centre of personal energy: religious ideas, previously peripheral, become central. This lecture gives the definition its machinery — hot and cold regions of consciousness, subconscious incubation, Starbuck's two types (volitional and self-surrender) — and the great case of the drunkard S. H. Hadley.

1. Give James's definition of conversion in full. Which phrases in it are purely psychological, and which phrase — "in consequence of its firmer hold upon religious realities" — deliberately leaves the theological question open?
2. Stephen H. Bradley believed himself fully converted at fourteen — he "saw the Saviour, by faith, in human shape, for about one second," and wanted to die. Nine years later the young converts of the 1829 revival unsettled him: they said they *knew* they had religion; he could only say he hoped he had it. Why does James choose a case with *two* conversions, and what does he say it shows about "one unsuspected depth below another" in character?
3. Bradley's second crisis came on the night of November 2, 1829, after two sermons on the judgment that left his feelings "still unmoved." Describe what happened in bed: the heart beating, the stream "resembling air in feeling" entering his mouth and heart for five minutes, the memory clearing so that Romans 8:26–27 seemed set open before him "as if some candle lighted was held for me to read," the groaning that brought his brother to the door asking if he had the toothache. Why does James quote the case at such length and without abridging its strangeness?
4. What changed in Bradley by morning — he could "but just stand," felt "as completely raised above the fears of death as of going to sleep," and went round to converse with neighbours on religion, which "I could not have been hired to have done before this"? What does he say about his speech being "under the control of the Spirit," and what limit does he himself put on the Pentecost comparison?
5. James's psychology of the self uses groups of ideas gathered round aims. Why is the President camping in the wilderness — paddle, gun, and fishing-rod, presidential anxieties lapsed — an illustration of the *completest* way a self may be divided? And when does such an alternation earn the name "transformation"?
6. What is a *velleity*? Use Augustine's aspirations and the President wondering in his pride of office whether the wood-chopper's life were not wholesomer. Where do velleities live in the mind's geography, and where does the "real self" sit meanwhile?
7. James retells how, as a youth, he heard his father read the part of Lord Gifford's will founding these very lectureships, and thought it as remote "as if it related to the planet Mars" — yet "here I am, with the Gifford system part and parcel of my very self." What is this autobiographical aside meant to prove about the migration of ideas from periphery to centre?
8. Define James's key term: the *habitual centre of personal energy*. In these terms, what exactly does "to say that a man is 'converted'" mean? Why does James insist Buddhists or Humians could describe the same facts without the word "soul"?
9. Why can psychology not say, in a given case, *why* an aim that was peripheral becomes central at just that moment? "We have a thought, or we perform an act, repeatedly, but on a certain day the real meaning of the thought peals through us for the first time." What is James's mechanical image for the whole process — the undermined building that keeps upright "by dead habit" until a shock makes "the whole fabric fall together"?
10. Starbuck's statistical finding: ordinary evangelical conversion parallels the normal adolescent growth-crisis — same age (usually fourteen to seventeen), same symptoms (incompleteness, brooding, sense of sin, anxiety about the hereafter), same result (happy relief and objectivity). What is Starbuck's conclusion about what conversion *is*, and what does theology, on his account, do to the adolescent tendencies? Why are revival conversions "very much more intense" yet about one-fifth as long?
11. Why does James warn, with Jonathan Edwards's help, that commonplace conversion narratives are "insensibly strained" to fit "the scheme already established in their minds" — and why does he resolve to keep to "the more first-hand and original forms," in "sporadic adult cases"? Why is there no conviction-of-sin stage in Catholic lands?
12. Leuba defines the religious sense morally: "the feeling of unwholeness, of moral imperfection, of sin... accompanied by the yearning after the peace of unity." Test the definition against S. H. Hadley's case: the homeless, dying drunkard in the Harlem saloon who pounded the bar till the glasses rattled, had himself locked up, prayed through the horrors in the cell, and at Jerry McAuley's Mission felt "the glorious brightness of the noonday sun shine into my heart." What is present in Hadley's experience, and what doctrinal theology is absent? What does Hadley's afterwards — never wanting a drink again, rescuing drunkards — add?
13. James says Leuba makes his type "too exclusive," because it matches only the subjectively centred melancholy of Bunyan and Alline. What other element of conversion does Tolstoy's case force us to add? (Starbuck's name for the second type is "spiritual illumination.")
14. Some persons, James says, never can be converted: religious ideas cannot become the centre of their energy. Distinguish the two causes he gives — inhibitive beliefs that *check* the religious faculties (the "agnostic vetoes upon faith as something weak and shameful") and a constitutional *anaesthesia* on the religious side. Why does he immediately add that even the barrenest breast may suffer "some thaw, some release" late in life — and why do such cases "more than any others suggest the idea that sudden conversion is by miracle"?
15. The forgotten name: sometimes effort recalls it, sometimes effort jams it, and giving up lets it come "sauntering into your mind... as carelessly as if it had never been invited." How does this everyday experience generate Starbuck's two types of conversion — the *volitional* type and the type by *self-surrender*? What does the music teacher tell her pupils: "Stop trying and it will do itself"?
16. "In the great majority of all cases, when the will has done its uttermost... the very last step must be left to other forces." Collect the testimony James strings together: "I had said I would not give up; but when my will was broken, it was all over"; "Lord, I have done all I can; I leave the whole matter with Thee"; John Nelson's "Lord, Thy will be done; damn or save!" Why is self-surrender, on Starbuck's analysis, indispensable — what is wrong with the conscious aim, and what is the "better self in posse" doing meanwhile?
17. "Man's extremity is God's opportunity" is the theological statement; "Let one do all in one's power, and one's nervous system will do the rest" is the physiological one. Why does James say both statements "acknowledge the same fact"? And why does he refuse, for now, to treat psychology's "subconscious" and theology's "direct supernatural operations of the Deity" as a final divergence?
18. Why can you not talk a man out of his misery by telling him all is well? James: "We can make ourselves more faithful to a belief of which we have the rudiments, but we cannot create a belief out of whole cloth when our perception actively assures us of its opposite." What are the only two ways of getting rid of anger, worry, fear, or despair?
19. David Brainerd's crisis turned on exhaustion: walking in a solitary place, he saw that all his contrivances were "utterly in vain," that he had "never once prayed from any respect to the glory of God" but only from self-interest — and from Friday morning to Sabbath evening (July 12, 1739) his religious affections were simply *gone*, "not distressed; yet disconsolate." Then, in the thick grove, "unspeakable glory seemed to open to the apprehension of my soul... I had no thought about my own salvation, and scarce reflected that there was such a creature as myself." Why does James italicize the exhaustion passage? How do T. W. B. (who sank into insensibility and woke praying *for others*) and Finney (who tried in vain to make himself anxious again) show the same hinge from the other side?
20. Sit with this: is there something in your own life that yielded only when you stopped trying — a name, a grief, a fear, a decision? What does that experience suggest about where the "centre" of a person actually sits?

## Lecture X — Conversion Concluded: Sudden Conversion and the Subliminal Self

The concluding conversion lecture takes up instantaneous conversion — St. Paul's the eminent type — through Alline's crisis of March 26, 1775, the Oxford graduate converted at three o'clock on a July afternoon, and Ratisbonne's vision in Rome. James then lays out his boldest psychological claim: the discovery, first made in 1886, of a consciousness beyond the margin of the ordinary field — Myers's subliminal self — whose uprushes explain automatisms, and whose "leaky margin" is the condition of sudden conversion, without thereby excluding the higher interpretation. The lecture ends by judging conversion's fruits.

1. Henry Alline's crisis came at sunset on March 26, 1775, stepping over his own threshold, when a "powerful but small still voice" put the audit to him: "You have been seeking, praying, reforming, laboring, reading, hearing, and meditating, and what have you done by it towards your salvation?" Walk through what followed — the old Bible opened unpremeditated at the 38th Psalm, family prayers unattended to, "At that instant of time when I gave all up to him to do with me as he pleased... redeeming love broke into my soul." What is the exact hinge of the crisis? And what do the details of the night add — the devil's warning that sleep would lose it all, his cry "O Lord God, if I am deceived, undeceive me," and waking to find his soul "surrounded by the arms of everlasting love"?
2. James notes that Alline, happy as he became, "never got his taste for even the most innocent carnal pleasures back," and quotes his later diary: "I preached at a wedding, and had the happiness thereby to be the means of excluding carnal mirth." Why does James insist on classing him with Bunyan and Tolstoy among those "upon whose soul the iron of melancholy left a permanent imprint"?
3. Leuba's Oxford graduate was converted in his father's rectory at "precisely three o'clock in the afternoon of a hot July day (July 13, 1886)," in perfect health, off drink for a month, "in no way troubled about my soul" — reading Drummond's *Natural Law in the Spiritual World* only to show off his critical talents. His attention was "absolutely 'soldered'" onto the verse "He that hath the Son hath life eternal." What is most remarkable in his account — that he felt supremely happy *while* certain he was lost, that "I did not repent even; God never asked me to repent," and that the way of escape "crept in upon me so gently, so lovingly, so unmistakably"?
4. The same man's sequel is stranger: drunk again the very next day in the hay-field, he did not ask forgiveness ("I felt that was no good, for I would be sure to fall again") but committed himself to God "in the profoundest belief that my individuality was going to be destroyed... and I was willing." From that hour, drink and even his pipe (smoked since he was twelve) lost all desire for him, permanently. What does he call "the secret of a holy life," and why does James single out "the complete abolition of an ancient appetite as one of the conversion's fruits"?
5. Alphonse Ratisbonne, a freethinking French Jew of twenty-nine, entered the church of San Andrea delle Fratte in Rome in 1842 merely to pass the time, wearing half-jocosely a medal and having read a prayer to the Virgin whose words he could not banish from his mind. Reconstruct the event from his letter: the black dog, the church vanished, the vision, prostration, tears, kissing the medal — "I looked for myself in myself and did not find myself." His only explanation is the simile of one born blind suddenly opening his eyes. Why does James call this "the most curious record of sudden conversion with which I am acquainted," and what predisposing conditions does he honestly list?
6. "Throughout the height of it he undoubtedly seems to himself a passive spectator or undergoer of an astounding process performed upon him from above." How did theology build on this fact (election, grace, a new nature "breathed into us"), and why did instantaneity come to seem *required* — the Moravians first, then the Methodists, with Wesley's testimony of 652 London members, every one "without a single exception" delivered instantaneously?
7. Against Methodism, the more usual Protestant sects and the Catholic Church hold that Christ's blood, the sacraments, and ordinary duties suffice without any acute crisis. Why does James nevertheless say Methodism follows "on the whole the profounder spiritual instinct"? What has "fully evolved Revivalism" made of the crisis — a "codified and stereotyped procedure"?
8. Joseph Alleine: conversion "is not the putting in a patch of holiness... The sincere Christian is quite a new fabric, from the foundation to the top-stone." Edwards adds that gracious affections differ from natural ones not in degree but *in kind*, and argues it is reasonable that the saved first be made sensible of their condemnation. James answers these quotations with a single empirical test later in the lecture. What is it, and does it succeed?
9. James now asks his governing question outright: is instantaneous conversion a miracle, with two classes of human beings partaking or merely seeming to partake of Christ's nature — "or, on the contrary, may the whole phenomenon of regeneration... possibly be a strictly natural process... neither more nor less divine in its mere causation and mechanism than any other process, high or low, of man's interior life?" Keep both horns in view through what follows.
10. Explain the "field of consciousness": a total wave with a centre of interest and a margin fading to unassignable limits, wide in organizing geniuses, narrowed "almost to a point" in drowsiness, illness, or fatigue. What is "the important fact which this 'field' formula commemorates," and how does the margin — lying around us "like a 'magnetic field'" — guide behavior and hold our whole past store of memories ready "at a touch to come in"?
11. Ordinary psychology assumed that what is absolutely extra-marginal is absolutely non-existent. What discovery "first made in 1886" does James call "the most important step forward that has occurred in psychology since I have been a student of that science"? State it carefully: what exists beyond the primary consciousness, and by what signs does it reveal its presence?
12. What is an *automatism* in Myers's generalized sense? Work through James's ladder of evidence: post-hypnotic suggestion (the act performed punctually, with an improvised pretext, and no recollection of the order); the hysteric patients of Binet, Janet, Breuer, and Freud, whose buried painful memories lead "a parasitic existence" and irrupt as hallucinations, pains, convulsions, paralyses — and vanish when the memories are altered by suggestion. Why do these clinical records, "like fairy-tales" at first reading, force "a farther step" in interpreting *every* automatism?
13. Apply the theory: sudden converts, James argues, may simply be subjects "in possession of a large region in which mental work can go on subliminally," from which invasive experiences upset the equilibrium of the primary consciousness. In his own long footnote he confesses the limits — Bradley, Ratisbonne, possibly Colonel Gardiner, possibly St. Paul resist easy incubation-explanations, leaving "a merely physiological nerve storm" or "some more mystical or theological hypothesis." Why does James print this confession, and what does it show about his method?
14. James answers the Methodist objection in advance by recalling Lecture I: why does the subliminal explanation of sudden conversion leave its *value* untouched? State the principle in his words.
15. Now the fruits test: "Converted men as a class are indistinguishable from natural men; some natural men even excel some converted men in their fruits." Why is there no "distinctive radiance" attaching even to the lowliest true convert, if Edwards is right that he partakes directly of Christ's substance? What do the believers themselves retreat to as "the real witness of the spirit" — and where else, James notes, is that same witness found?
16. Why does James call Edwards's *Treatise on Religious Affections* — the richest description of the supernaturally infused condition — "a clearer argument than this book unwittingly offers" for the thesis that "no chasm exists between the orders of human excellence," that generation and regeneration are "matters of degree"?
17. Yet James immediately guards the other side: "a small man's salvation will always be a great salvation and the greatest of all facts for him." What is his image — the flood that goes above one's head — and why does he ask who knows how much worse the lives of "these spiritual grubs and earthworms, these Crumps and Stigginses," might have been without the grace they received? (Read the Emerson footnote: why might Crump, "with his grunting resistance to all his native devils," be *the better Crump* for his second birth?)
18. Professor Coe's study of seventy-seven converts found automatisms, hypnagogic hallucinations, and hypnotic sensibility far commoner among "striking" converts — and found that revival candidates who experienced *nothing* were mostly of the "spontaneous" subclass, fertile in self-suggestions, whose self-suggestion of impossibility blocked the effect. What three factors, united in one subject, let Coe "safely predict" a sudden conversion? And what is Coe's answer — which James endorses — to whether this temperamental origin diminishes the conversion's significance: "the ultimate test of religious values is nothing psychological... but something ethical, definable only in terms of what is attained."
19. James's most careful sentence in the lecture: a developed subliminal self with "a leaky or pervious margin" is a *conditio sine qua non* of instantaneous conversion — but does reference to a subliminal self exclude the direct presence of the Deity? Reconstruct his reasoning: our waking consciousness opens our senses to material things; might the subconscious region be precisely the door through which higher spiritual agencies touch us — "The hubbub of the waking life might close a door which in the dreamy Subliminal might remain ajar or open"? Why does he add that transcendency alone establishes "no presumption that they were more divine than diabolical"?
20. Adolphe Monod's conversion at Naples in 1827 began from an intellectual impasse: his sadness had corrupted "my feelings, my judgment, and my happiness," so that to correct the disorder by reason and will "would be to act like a blind man who should pretend to correct one of his eyes by the aid of the other equally blind one." What resource remained? And how does Luther's thundering passage — God must take "this maul in hand (the law, I mean) to beat in pieces... this beast with her vain confidence" — state the same structure from the theological side: "the more literally lost you are, the more literally you are the very being whom Christ's sacrifice has already saved"?
21. Leuba's "faith-state" — the joyous conviction, the assurance that all is well — is, James agrees, the essential thing, and the conceptual belief about Christ's work "really accessory and non-essential" (Tolstoy's nearly theology-free conversion is the comment). What are the three characteristics of the *state of assurance* James enumerates: the central one (loss of worry; "a passion of willingness, of acquiescence, of admiration"), the sense of perceiving truths not known before, and the objective change in the world's appearance? Illustrate the third from Edwards ("scarce anything... was so sweet to me as thunder and lightning; formerly nothing had been so terrible") and Billy Bray ("everything looked new to me, the people, the fields, the cattle, the trees... like a new man in a new world").
22. Revival automatisms — unconsciousness, convulsions, involuntary utterances, the choking hand at the camp-meeting convert's throat — and the *photisms*: Paul's blinding light, Constantine's cross, Finney's "light perfectly ineffable" that "almost prostrated me," Mr. Peek's oats "arrayed in a kind of rainbow glory." What is James's verdict on their spiritual significance, and to what does he ascribe them? Why does he carefully footnote the cases (Brainerd's "unspeakable glory") where the light is plainly metaphorical?
23. "The most characteristic of all the elements of the conversion crisis... is the ecstasy of happiness produced." Finney: waves "of liquid love," "the very breath of God," fanning "like immense wings," until he cried "Lord, I cannot bear any more." Billy Bray walking: one foot says "Glory," the other "Amen." Why does James end the subject not with ecstasy but with *permanence* — answering the "pitying smile at so much 'hysterics'" by arguing that backsliding cannot diminish what even a brief conversion reveals, "the high-water mark of his spiritual capacity"? What do Miss Johnston's statistics for Starbuck actually show (93 per cent of the women, 77 per cent of the men reporting some backsliding — but only 6 per cent a relapse from the faith itself)? And what is the testimony about Ratisbonne's remaining fifty years?
24. Sit with this: James leaves the door open both ways — the subliminal as mere machinery, or as the very doorway of higher powers. Which reading does the *evidence in this lecture* actually require, and which does it merely permit? What difference would it make to how you treat your own sudden changes of heart?

## Lecture XI — Saintliness (Part One): The Saintly Character, Charity, and Equanimity

James now turns from the experience of conversion to its lasting fruits. The task divides in two: first describe the saintly character, then judge its value. This first saintliness lecture sets out the psychology of character — impulses and inhibitions — defines saintliness by four inner conditions and four practical consequences, and then illustrates the sense of a friendly higher Power, brotherly charity, and the equanimity that follows from self-surrender.

### Study Questions

1. James quotes Sainte-Beuve on Port-Royal: the state of grace is "fundamentally one state in spirit and in fruits" in all communions — "one of love and humility, of infinite confidence in God, and of severity for one's self, accompanied with tenderness for others." Why does James begin the study of saintliness with the claim that the same savor is found "in Saint Teresa of Avila just as in any Moravian brother of Herrnhut"?
2. How does James describe our moral attitude at any moment — as a resultant of what two opposing sets of forces — and what does he mean by saying that inhibition moulds us "as if we were fluids pent within the cavity of a jar"?
3. What does the dandy running to a fire with shaving-lather on his face, and the mother whose maternal excitement turns inconveniences into "the glowing heart of a great joy" (James Hinton), show about what great emotional excitement does to inhibitions?
4. What is "the expulsive power of a higher affection," and how does the story of the royal Bengal tiger — quelled by terror, lying panting like a dog while its brains were blown out — illustrate a new centre of character being installed?
5. Why does James say that anger annihilates inhibition more irresistibly than almost anything else, and what does Moltke's remark about war contribute to the point?
6. How does James explain fixed differences of character — the inborn "genius for an emotion" in men like George Fox, Garibaldi, General Booth, John Brown, and Louise Michel — and why do such people feel no inhibitions where the mere aspirant must overcome them one by one?
7. What distinguishes willing from merely wishing, on James's account, and what happens to conventionality, shyness, laziness, and stinginess when the "steam-pressure" of ideal excitement rises high enough?
8. What is James's definition of saintliness? ("The collective name for the ripe fruits of religion in a character is Saintliness.")
9. Name the four fundamental inner conditions of the saintly character: the feeling of being in a wider life with conviction of an Ideal Power; the sense of that Power's friendly continuity with one's own life and willing self-surrender to its control; the elation and freedom as the confining selfhood melts down; and the shift of the emotional centre toward "yes, yes" where the claims of the non-ego are concerned. Which of the four does the most work in the cases that follow?
10. Name the four practical consequences James derives from those conditions: asceticism, strength of soul, purity, and charity. How does each grow out of the inner conditions?
11. In the illustrations of the sense of Presence, what does Thoreau report of the gentle rain at Walden — that every little pine-needle "expanded and swelled with sympathy and befriended me" — and what does Hilty name as the compensation for lost independence ("the disappearance of all fear from one's life")?
12. Mrs. Jonathan Edwards describes a night in which she floated "like the motes swimming in the beams of the sun," willing to be kept out of heaven a thousand years, and a thousand years in horror, if it were most for God's honour. What does her case show about the second inner condition — willing self-surrender to the Ideal Power's control?
13. James warns against explaining charity merely as a derivative of theistic belief, pointing to Stoicism, Hinduism, and Buddhism. What is his alternative account — that the faith-state, "like love or fear," is "a natural psychic complex, and carries charity with it by organic consequence"?
14. What does Dr. Dumas's patient Marie show — stingy and useless in her melancholy period, full of universal goodwill in her joyous period — about the "organic affinity between joyousness and tenderness"? Why does James include a case from circular insanity in a lecture on saintliness?
15. Richard Weaver, the collier and former pugilist, let Tom strike him five times on the cheek, offering it for a sixth, and on Monday Tom came weeping to ask forgiveness. What had Weaver's own backsliding — pounding a man, breaking another's jaw — to do with the force of this example?
16. James asks whether "Love your enemies" is "a mere Oriental hyperbole" or "sincere and literal." What is his psychological verdict — that the precept is "not self-contradictory" but "the extreme limit of a kind of magnanimity" — and what would radically following it involve ("a breach with our instinctive springs of action as a whole")?
17. What three motives does James find mixed in the saints' love of the personally loathsome — charity pure and simple, humility, and the frenzy of self-immolation — and how do Francis of Assisi kissing his lepers, and Margaret Mary Alacoque and Francis Xavier cleansing sores with their tongues, make the reader "admire and shudder at the same time"?
18. Equanimity: why, on James's account, must the sense of being in the keeping of a Power one can absolutely trust "steady the nerves, cool the fever, and appease the fret"? How does the temper differ in the sombre constitution (resignation) and the cheerful one (joyous consent)?
19. Compare the two resignation cases: Professor Lagneau, whose sole strength and foundation was despair, asking only "the courage to do without the desire of deliverance"; and Pascal's prayer, asking "neither for health nor for sickness, for life nor for death," but only that his will be conformed to God's. What is common to both?
20. Madame Guyon went to prison for heresy without tears, and in a sinking carriage in quicksand felt "quite contented and willing it were so, if it were my heavenly Father's choice." What does James take her serenity in the eleven-day storm at sea to prove about self-surrender?
21. Frank Bullen, hanging head-downwards by one foot over the foam after his shipboard conversion, "felt only high exultation in my certainty of eternal life" and "lived a whole age of delight" in five seconds. What does this case add to the martyrdom case of Blanche Gamond, who under the whip was "swooning with happiness within"?
22. James calls the transition from worry to equanimity the most wonderful of all the shiftings of the personal centre of energy, and says it comes "not by doing, but by simply relaxing and throwing the burden down." Why does he call this abandonment of self-responsibility "the fundamental act in specifically religious, as distinguished from moral practice" — and why does he insist it "antedates theologies and is independent of philosophies," appearing in mind-cure, theosophy, and Stoicism as much as in Christianity?
23. Saint Catharine of Genoa "took cognizance of things, only as they were presented to her in succession, moment by moment," so that "the divine moment was the present moment." How does this concentration on the present connect equanimity with the Hindu, mind-cure, and theosophical practices James mentions?
24. Reflective: James has defined the saint by an inner shift of the emotional centre rather than by creed or conduct. Does that definition let him compare a Catholic saint, a Stoic, and a Buddhist on one scale — and what is gained, and what risked, by the comparison?

## Lecture XII — Saintliness (Part Two): Purity and Asceticism

The saintly character, once its centre has shifted, grows exceedingly sensitive to discord: whatever is unspiritual taints "the pure water of the soul." This lecture follows the impulse to purity through the early Quakers' costly sincerity, and then turns to asceticism proper, distinguishing six psychological levels from which self-mortification can spring, and illustrating each with cases from Carlyle's cold bath to Suso's sixteen years of self-torture.

### Study Questions

1. How does James describe the saint's purity of life — an enhanced sensitiveness to "inner inconsistency or discord," in which "mixture and confusion grow intolerable"? Why must all the mind's objects be re-ordered with reference to the spiritual excitement that is now its keynote?
2. Billy Bray gave up tobacco when the still small voice applied the words "It is an idol, a lust; worship the Lord with clean lips," threw his tobacco in the fire and his pipe under his foot — "ashes to ashes, dust to dust" — and later twice whipped his quid of chewing tobacco under the form at a prayer-meeting. Why does James call this a good example of purity achieved as "a more gradual conquest" rather than at a stroke?
3. The early Quakers suffered blows, punchings, beatings, and imprisonments for refusing to put off their hats or to say "you" to a single person. In George Fox's Journal, why were these "small things in the eye of man" worth such suffering — what did the customs represent to Fox?
4. Thomas Elwood, once secretary to John Milton, lists the evils the divine light showed him: lace, ribbons, and useless buttons; rings; flattering titles ("Sir, Master, My Lord, Madam"); uncovering the head; and "the corrupt and unsound form of speaking in the plural number to a single person." What single principle unifies Elwood's list?
5. John Woolman reasoned that dyeing cloth "to hide dirt" strengthens "a spirit which would conceal that which is disagreeable," and after nine months of unease took to a hat of the natural colour of the fur, accepting that friends grew shy of him. What does Woolman's case show about the craving for moral consistency when it is "developed to this degree"?
6. James says the person who cannot bear discord may unify his life "only by withdrawing from it," and quotes Stevenson's "To omit... is the one art in literature." How does the monastery, on this account, work — by "omissions quite as much as by actions"?
7. Name James's six heads under which ascetic conduct can originate: (1) organic hardihood disgusted with ease; (2) love of purity shocked by the sensual; (3) sacrifice happily made to the Deity; (4) pessimistic self-despair joined with beliefs about expiation; (5) psychopathic obsession or fixed idea; (6) genuine perversion of bodily sensibility, in which pain is felt as pleasure. Why does James warn that in pronounced cases "several of the assigned motives usually work together"?
8. Before the cases, James describes a "strange moral transformation" in the modern West: we no longer expect anyone to face physical pain with equanimity, and even the Catholic Church's ascetic discipline "has largely come into desuetude." Why does he need this historical observation before asking his readers to take asceticism seriously?
9. What is James's psychology of the will in action — why is passive happiness "slack and insipid" for most people, so that some "no! no!" must be mixed in "to produce the sense of an existence with character and texture and power"? How does the machine-and-steam-pressure comparison (each soul has its own best conditions of efficiency) set up the ascetic life as a natural outcome for certain characters?
10. Under head 1, Professor Tyndall reports that Carlyle put him into a bath-tub every morning of a freezing Berlin winter, and an agnostic correspondent got up at night to stand a minute in the cold "just so as to prove my manhood." What do these low-grade cases establish about the normality of courting the arduous?
11. The Protestant in Starbuck's collection secretly made burlap shirts with the burrs next to the skin, wore pebbles in his shoes, and spent nights flat on the floor without covering. Which heads (2 and 3) does James assign, and what marks the step from hardihood to systematic mortification?
12. Channing, the Unitarian minister, chose the smallest room and an unheated attic, wore garments "the world would call mean," and joked that if his bed were his country he was like Bonaparte, controlling only the part he occupied. Why does James find "no pessimistic element" in Channing's case?
13. John Cennick, Methodism's first lay preacher, convicted of sin in Cheapside in 1735, fasted, prayed nine times a day, and came to feed on potatoes, acorns, crabs, and grass, fancying dry bread too great an indulgence. Under which head does he fall, and why does James refuse to call the impulse to expiate "a mercenary incentive"?
14. M. Vianney, the Curé of Ars, imposed on himself never to smell a flower, never to drink when parched, never to drive away a fly, never to sit down — yet when a missionary secretly warmed his confessional he said with emotion, "God is very good... my feet have always been warm." What does Vianney's saying — "it is only the first step that costs... to give one's self entirely, and to keep nothing for one's self" — show about asceticism as the fruit of "highly optimistic religious feeling" (head 3)?
15. Cotton Mather, two hours before his wife died, knelt, took her hand in both of his, solemnly gave her up to the Lord, and "gently put her out of my hands... resolving that I would never touch it more." Why does James call this "the hardest, and perhaps the bravest action that ever I did" a pure case of loving sacrifice rather than penance?
16. James says that when a procedure is codified into manuals (Rodriguez; Ribet's *L'Ascétique Chrétienne*), "the more delicate spirit of it evaporates." What is the "undiluted ascetic spirit" he sends us to autobiographies to find?
17. Saint John of the Cross counsels turning always "not to what is most easy, but to what is hardest; not to what tastes best, but to what is most distasteful... not to will anything, but to will nothing." How do his paradoxes — "To possess all things, resolve to possess nothing. To be all things, be willing to be nothing" — pass, as James notes, from God to "the more metaphysical notion of the All"?
18. Suso wore an undergarment fixed with a hundred and fifty brass nails pointed into his flesh, leather loops fastening his hands at his throat, gloves studded with tacks, and a cross with thirty iron needles on his bare back — for about sixteen years, until a Whitsunday vision told him God required it no longer. Under which heads does James place him, and why does he call the case "distinctly pathological" while still treating the autobiography as "a classic religious document"?
19. Of Margaret Mary Alacoque, founder of the Sacred Heart devotion, James reports: "Nothing but pain... makes my life supportable." Which of the six heads does her case approach, and how does it differ from Suso's?
20. Reflective: James insists asceticism is not one thing but six. Does sorting its motives change how we judge it — and can the same outward act (say, fasting) be healthy under one head and diseased under another?

## Lecture XIII — Saintliness (Part Three): Obedience, Poverty, and the Close of the Description

The last of the descriptive lectures takes up the two remaining monastic vows. Obedience — the sacrifice in which a man offers God "his two most precious goods, his intellect and his will" — is traced from organizational expediency to Ignatius Loyola's corpse-and-stick ideal. Poverty is then examined in Rodriguez's tests, in the soldier's and labourer's detachment, and in Antoinette Bourignon's thrown-away penny, before James closes the description with a methodological confession: such sentiments can only be recorded by those who observe them, since no one can fathom an emotion from outside it.

### Study Questions

1. James confesses that the idealization of obedience "seems something of a mystery" to him, since the twentieth century holds the individual's self-determination among its best-rooted ideals. What lower grounds does he find first — the expediency of a firm organization, and the plain fact that in fatigue and indecision others often counsel us better than we counsel ourselves?
2. How, on the higher ground, does obedience grow out of "the general religious phenomenon of inner softening and self-surrender"? Why might obeying a man "whose fallibility we see through thoroughly" still feel like resigning one's will to infinite wisdom?
3. The Catholic writer James quotes calls obedience the completing sacrifice: "By poverty he immolates his exterior possessions; by chastity he immolates his body; by obedience... his intellect and his will. The sacrifice is then complete and unreserved, a genuine holocaust." What does the holocaust image claim about the order of the three vows?
4. Rodriguez argues that in obeying "we can commit no fault," since God charges the act to the Superior's account — Jerome's "Oh, sovereign liberty! Oh, holy and blessed security by which one becomes almost impeccable!" How does the passenger-and-pilot image (the religious goes to heaven "as if while sleeping") present the surrender of responsibility itself as the consolation?
5. Ignatius Loyola: "In the hands of my Superior, I must be a soft wax... I must consider myself as a corpse which has neither intelligence nor will... like a stick in the hand of an old man." And Rodriguez reports that Ignatius would have sailed from Ostia without mast, sails, oars, or rudder at the Pope's order, "with a great internal satisfaction." What do these sayings show about obedience carried to its ideal limit?
6. Sister Marie Claire of Port-Royal, on a prelate's passing suggestion that it might be better not to speak to Mother Angelique again, remained "for several years without once speaking to her sister." Why does James call this an "extravagance" — and what does it reveal about the mechanism he has been describing?
7. Turning to poverty: why does James deny that the cult of poverty is a paradox, once we recollect "how easily higher excitements hold lower cupidities in check"?
8. Rodriguez's test of poverty in spirit: are you glad of hunger, thirst, cold, and fatigue — glad to wear a patched habit, glad when something is lacking to your meal, glad when your cell is out of repair? And his rule, from Ignatius, that the religious should feel toward clothes, books, and cell "as a statue" that is draped and stripped without grief. What is being tested — the having of things, or the holding of them?
9. Saint Dorotheus refused Saint Dositheus the knife he wanted for the infirmary: "Will you be the slave of a knife or the slave of Jesus Christ?" Why did superiors deliberately test monks as God tested Abraham — moving a man from a comfortable room, taking away a beloved book — and what would happen to "the wall of poverty" without such tests?
10. James finds the first ground of poverty in the immemorial opposition "between the men who have and the men who are": the knight-errant, the soldier "absolutely unincumbered," the day labourer. Why does the property-owner, from this standpoint, seem "buried and smothered in ignoble externalities and trammels"?
11. Whitefield prayed, "When thou seest me in danger of nestling, — in pity — in tender pity, — put a thorn in my nest." And the anarchist poet Edward Carpenter: a soldier on campaign asks "not what fresh furniture he can carry on his back, but rather what he can leave behind." How do these two voices, from opposite camps, state the same ground — that "lives based on having are less free than lives based either on doing or on being"?
12. Saint Francis refused the novice his psalter: "After you have got your psalter you will crave a breviary; and after you have got your breviary you will sit in your stall like a grand prelate." What law of desire is Francis applying?
13. Beyond the athletic attitude, James finds "something profounder still": so long as any secular safeguard is retained, "the surrender is incomplete, the vital crisis is not passed, fear still stands sentinel." How does his medical parallel work — the drunkard or morphine maniac who hides supplies of the drug against the cure — and what is money, in this analogy?
14. Antoinette Bourignon, fleeing her father's house at eighteen in a hermit's habit, carried one penny for bread; hearing within her, "Where is thy faith? in a penny?" she threw it away — "No, Lord, my faith is not in a penny, but in thee alone." Why does James say the penny, "a small financial safeguard," was "an effective spiritual obstacle," and that only when it was gone could "the character settle into the new equilibrium completely"?
15. James names two further mysteries in the cult of poverty: veracity ("My own bare entity must fight the battle — shams cannot save me") and democracy — "humanity, refusing to enjoy anything that others do not share." How does J. J. Chapman's reading of "Sell all thou hast and follow me" make the abandonment of luxury "a mere incident" to "the remorseless logic of our love for others"?
16. James closes the descriptive lectures: "One can never fathom an emotion or divine its dictates by standing outside of it... Each emotion obeys a logic of its own." What method does he prescribe for those outside the pale — and how does that method govern the whole descriptive half of the book you have now read?
17. Reflective: James has now described charity, equanimity, purity, asceticism, obedience, and poverty as fruits of one shift of the personal centre of energy. Which of these fruits, if any, seems to you detachable from the religious excitement that produced it — and which seem inseparable from it?

## Lecture XIV — The Value of Saintliness (Part One): The Empirical Test, Fanaticism, and Theopathic Absorption

Description now gives way to judgment. James concedes that the empirical method he renounced scholastic theology for in Lecture I can never yield clean-cut results — only verdicts "on the whole" — and defends that method at length: gods are believed in, and dropped, by their fruits. He then insists on judging personal religion apart from its institutional partner, and begins the audit of the saintly virtues by their excesses: devoutness unbalanced becomes fanaticism, and in gentler, feebler minds, a theopathic absorption that crowds out all human use.

### Study Questions

1. James opens by imagining the easy road he has renounced: Catholic theologians descending "from above" with fixed definitions, for whom man's perfection is the fulfilment of his end — union with his Maker — along the active, purgative, and contemplative paths. Why can the empirical method never give results "almost mathematically," and what does James say we are left with instead?
2. What is the qualification James fears we shall never escape — "on the whole" — and why is it "so dear to your practical man, so repugnant to your systematizer"?
3. James meets the objection head-on: how can you measure a religion's fruits in human terms without first settling whether its God exists — since if a deity really demanded sacrifices, condemning them would be a theoretical mistake? To what extent does he confess that "we must be theologians"?
4. What is James's account of the secular alteration of the mental climate — that after a few generations "the older gods have fallen below the common secular level, and can no longer be believed in"? Why would a deity requiring bleeding sacrifices not be taken seriously today "even if powerful historical credentials were put forward in his favor"?
5. "The gods we stand by are the gods we need and can use." How does James describe the way the Greek and Roman gods ceased to be believed in, and how Protestants dealt with Catholic notions and liberal Protestants with older Protestant ones — always by the same test?
6. Jonathan Edwards had a "delightful conviction" of the doctrine of arbitrary salvation and damnation, finding it "exceeding pleasant, bright, and sweet." What does the modern abhorrence of that same doctrine show about whose standard is doing the judging?
7. James proposes "to test saintliness by common sense... It is but the elimination of the humanly unfit, and the survival of the humanly fittest, applied to religious beliefs." What does he claim no religion has ever in the long run proved itself in any other way?
8. On the charge of scepticism: why does James say that admitting one's liability to correction is not embarking "upon a sea of wanton doubt," and that claiming only "reasonable probability" leaves us better placed for truth than claiming infallibility? What does he mean by calling the wisest critic right only "up to date" and "on the whole," and by the line "when half-gods go, the gods arrive"?
9. Ought all men to have the same religion? James asks whether hard and soft, proud and humble, healthy-minded and despairing, need "exactly the same religious incentives." What follows for the impartiality of any judge of religious fruits?
10. Before judging, James renews the Lecture II distinction between religion as an individual personal function and religion as an institutional, corporate, or tribal product. What happens, on his account, when a first-hand experience's doctrine triumphs and becomes an orthodoxy — "the spring is dry; the faithful live at second hand exclusively and stone the prophets in their turn"?
11. George Fox's Journal describes fasting, hollow trees, forsaking priests and "separate preachers," until, when all hopes in men were gone, he heard the voice: "There is one, even Jesus Christ, that can speak to thy condition." Why does James read this page aloud at precisely this point in the argument?
12. What are religion's "two wicked partners" — the spirit of corporate dominion and the spirit of dogmatic dominion — and why does James charge the baiting of Jews, the hunting of Albigenses, the stoning of Quakers, and the massacring of Armenians to "aboriginal human neophobia" and tribal instinct rather than to piety? ("Piety is the mask, the inner force is tribal instinct.")
13. Yet of one charge religion cannot be wholly acquitted: over-zealousness or fanaticism. James's preliminary remark: religious phenomena, like all human phenomena, are subject to "the law of the golden mean" — we are glad John Howard, Mazzini, Botticelli, and Michael Angelo existed, but glad there are other ways of taking life. What is the difference between admiring a saint and being bound to imitate him?
14. Excess, James argues, usually means one-sidedness: "it is hard to imagine an essential faculty too strong, if only other faculties equally strong be there to cooperate with it." What, then, does the impression of extravagance in saints usually prove on examination — a strength, or "a relative deficiency of intellect"?
15. Fanaticism is "only loyalty carried to a convulsive extreme." How does the devotee come to idealize the devotion itself, until realizing the idol's merits adequately becomes "the one great merit of the worshiper" — and what part do the honorific legends around the Buddha, Mohammed, and the Christian saints play?
16. Vambéry's Persian dervish had vowed for thirty years to utter no word but "Ali! Ali!" — at home, at the bazaar, treated ill or generously — and was venerated as a saint. Why does James place this footnote where he does?
17. Jealousy for the deity's honour: between his own and Jehovah's enemies "a David knows no difference"; Catherine of Siena could think of no better union for warring Christians than "a crusade to massacre the Turks"; Luther found no word of protest over the tortures of the Anabaptist leaders; Cromwell praised the Lord for delivering his enemies for "execution." James's verdict: fanaticism belongs on "the wrong side of religion's account" only so long as what condition holds about the God believed in?
18. In gentle characters with feeble intellect, devoutness takes the other excess — the "theopathic condition," for which James says there is no English name. How does it differ from fanaticism in temper and in danger?
19. Margaret Mary Alacoque: Christ took out her mortal heart, inflamed it in his own, and promised that his Heart would dilate for all who honoured it on the first Fridays. What were the fruits in her life — tried in the infirmary, the kitchen, and the school, and everywhere too absorbed to attend? Why does James's judgment stop at "indulgent pity"?
20. Saint Gertrude's *Revelations* consist mainly of Christ's compliments and caresses to her undeserving person — including his gratified delight in the odoriferous substances she held in her mouth for a headache. What does James say such a narrative reveals about "the gap between the thirteenth and the twentieth century," and about the God "our own imagination has grown to need"?
21. Saint Teresa is the hard case: a powerful practical intellect, admirable descriptive psychology, a will equal to any emergency, talent for politics and business — yet James confesses his only feeling in reading her was pity "that so much vitality of soul should have found such poor employment," calling her religion "an endless amatory flirtation" and her type, in Dr. Jordan's term, a "shrew." Where exactly does his criticism fall — on her faculties, or on the ideas of God her age supplied her?
22. James closes this part by praising Luther for sweeping away "the very notion of a debit and credit account kept with individuals by the Almighty." What is a God who keeps "a pedantically minute account of individual shortcomings" and loads favourites with "insipid marks of favor" too small for, on James's telling?

## Lecture XV — The Value of Saintliness (Part Two): Purity, Charity, Asceticism, Poverty — and the Economic Verdict

The audit continues through the remaining virtues. Purity in excess builds the church fugient — hermitages and monasteries — and finds its type in Saint Louis of Gonzaga. Charity in excess is charged with preserving the unfit, yet is defended as a creative, prophetic force. Asceticism, seemingly the easiest excess to dismiss, is rehabilitated as the champion of heroism, and poverty is proposed as the moral equivalent of war. The lectures close with the economic verdict: tested by adaptation, the saint is indispensable to the world's welfare, though no absoluteness attaches to saintliness in this world.

### Study Questions

1. The aggressive pietist reaches unity "objectively, by forcibly stamping disorder and divergence out"; the retiring pietist reaches it "subjectively," making a smaller world from which discord is eliminated. What two churches does James set side by side — the church militant with its prisons and inquisition, and the "church fugient" with its hermitages and monasteries — and what single object do both pursue?
2. The sensitive soul drops one relation after another: "Amusements must go first, then conventional 'society,' then business, then family duties." The young sister asks her Superior whether she should not speak at all during recreation, lest she fall into some unconscious sin. What is the inner logic that makes the lives of saints "a history of successive renunciations of complication"?
3. Saint Louis of Gonzaga vowed perpetual chastity at ten and was granted the extraordinary grace of never feeling temptation — yet never raised his eyes in the street, heard his mother's maid only through a barely opened door, avoided being alone with his own mother, and after three months in the refectory had to ask where the rector sat. Why does James say he "can find no other sorts of fruit than these of Louis's saintship"?
4. Louis died in 1591 in his twenty-ninth year and is patron of all young people; letters tied with green and red ribbons are piled at his Roman altar, directed to "Paradiso," to be burnt unread. How does James's final judgment turn on "our conception of God" — and on the secular mutation that now deems helpfulness in human affairs an essential element of worth? ("It is better that a life should contract many a dirt-mark, than forfeit usefulness in its efforts to remain unspotted.")
5. Starbuck's footnote case: a woman of sixty-eight who withdrew from every church, living alone in a top-story room, happy in her own spiritual blessings, writing booklets on sanctification, having been told by the Spirit, "Stop going to church... Go to your own room and I will teach you." Why does James say that listening to her, "one was tempted to forget that it was from the life of a person who could not live by it in conjunction with her fellows"?
6. Charity faces the charge "of preserving the unfit, and breeding parasites and beggars." James's analysis: perfect conduct is a relation between three terms — the actor, the objects, and the recipients — so no critic can judge by "the actor's animus alone." What does he mean by warning that appeals to sympathy are "folly when we are dealing with human crocodiles and boa-constrictors"?
7. Herbert Spencer held that the perfect man's conduct will appear perfect only in a perfect environment. James paraphrases: saintly conduct would be the most perfect conduct conceivable "in an environment where all were saints already." What does he then frankly confess about sympathy, charity, and non-resistance "in the world that actually is" — and what do the modern scientific organization of charity and the whole history of constitutional government testify?
8. And yet: what would be lost if the world were confined to "hard-headed, hard-hearted, and hard-fisted methods exclusively" — if no one would help a brother first and find out afterwards whether he were worthy, or be "duped many a time rather than live always on suspicion"?
9. James's defence: the saints "are authors, auctores, increasers, of goodness," treating people as worthy until they are stimulated to become worthy. Why does he say we have "no right to speak of human crocodiles and boa-constrictors as of fixedly incurable beings" — what do the polyhedron of character and "the resources of the subliminal region" have to do with it?
10. "Force destroys enemies... but non-resistance, when successful, turns enemies into friends; and charity regenerates its objects." The Paton footnote: the Christian chief and four companions walked unarmed into the village under a shower of spears, caught them in their bare hands, and laid them down — and the heathen chief and all his tribe ended "sitting in the school of Christ." What is "the saint's magic gift to mankind"?
11. James compares the Utopian dreams of contemporary socialists and anarchists to the saint's belief in an existent kingdom of heaven. In what respect are they "analogous," and what work do both do as "slow leavens of a better order"?
12. Asceticism's excesses: Saint Peter of Alcantara, in Teresa's account, slept an hour and a half a day for forty years, in a cell four feet and a half long, never raising his hood, sometimes eight days without food. Why do such figures appear to the modern imagination "rather in the light of tragic mountebanks than of sane men"?
13. Against mortification James marshals the Bhagavad-Gita (only the inwardly attached need renounce action), Augustine's "If you only love God enough, you may safely follow all your inclinations," Ramakrishna ("He needs no devotional practices whose heart is moved to tears at the mere mention of the name of Hari"), and the Buddha's middle way. What single principle unites these four witnesses?
14. Yet James rehabilitates asceticism: "in its spiritual meaning asceticism stands for nothing less than for the essence of the twice-born philosophy." What belief does it symbolize — about "an element of real wrongness in this world" — and how does the once-born method of ignoring evil fail as "a general solution of the problem"?
15. "Mankind's common instinct for reality... has always held the world to be essentially a theatre for heroism." Why, on James's account, does the man who can "fling [life] away like a flower" stand as our "born superior," whatever his other frailties — and what truth has asceticism been "the faithful champion" of?
16. James's practical proposal: not to turn our backs on the ascetic impulse but "to discover some outlet for it of which the fruits in the way of privation and hardship might be objectively useful." What contemporary dangers does he name — the worship of material luxury, and an exclusively sympathetic upbringing breeding "a certain trashiness of fibre"?
17. War is the rival school of strenuous life: it annuls the customary inhibitions, death becomes commonplace, and "ranges of new energy are set free." The Austrian officer's testimony: an army's device cannot be "live and let live"; the soldier "must be exactly the opposite of a reasoning and thinking man." What is the "world-wide difference" between the military type of self-severity and the ascetic saint's?
18. James coins the phrase this lecture is remembered by: "What we now need to discover in the social realm is the moral equivalent of war." Why does he look for it in "the old monkish poverty-worship" — and what would voluntarily accepted poverty be, in his phrase, but "'the strenuous life,' without the need of crushing weaker peoples"?
19. "The prevalent fear of poverty among the educated classes is the worst moral disease from which our civilization suffers." What freedoms does James say indifference to poverty would buy for servants of unpopular causes — and what does he concede wealth is genuinely better for?
20. The review: the saint's group of qualities — felicity, purity, charity, patience, self-severity — flows "from the sense of the divine as from its psychological centre," and in social relations "we can count upon the saint lending his hand with more certainty than we can count upon any other person." But what catalogue of "holy excesses" follows when the intellectual outlook is narrow — and by what three things must we judge a saint instead of sentimentally and in isolation?
21. Essentials versus accidents: James warns not to confound "the essentials of saintliness" with "the special determinations of these passions at any historical moment" — refuge in monasteries was an idol of the tribe in the middle ages as bearing a hand in the world's work is today. What would Saint Francis or Saint Bernard, living today, undoubtedly do — and undoubtedly not do?
22. Nietzsche is named "the most inimical critic of the saintly impulses whom I know": for him the saint is "the sophisticated invalid, the degenerate par excellence," and "the sick are the greatest danger for the well." How does James explain the dislike itself — as a negative result of the biologically useful instinct of glorifying the tribal chief, the "beaked and taloned graspers of the world," beside whom saints look like "tame and harmless barn-yard poultry"?
23. The fable of the wind, the sun, and the traveler: how does James answer Nietzsche's contrast — by what different faculty of admiration does the saint charm the world, as the woman subjugates the man "by the mystery of gentleness in beauty"?
24. The feud "revolves essentially upon two pivots: Shall the seen world or the unseen world be our chief sphere of adaptation? and must our means of adaptation in this seen world be aggressiveness or non-resistance?" Why does James deny that there can be "one intrinsically ideal type of human character" — what work does the ideal-horse comparison (dray horse, race horse) do?
25. Spencer's *Data of Ethics* supplies the test: ideality in conduct is "altogether a matter of adaptation." The saint is "abstractly a higher type of man than the 'strong man,' because he is adapted to the highest society conceivable" — yet "as far as this world goes, any one who makes an out-and-out saint of himself does so at his peril." How do both halves of that verdict stand together?
26. Saint Paul was, from the biological point of view, "a failure, because he was beheaded" — yet "magnificently adapted to the larger environment of history." The great saints — "the Francises, Bernards, Luthers, Loyolas, Wesleys, Channings, Moodys, Gratrys, the Phillips Brookses" — are "successes from the outset," like "pictures with an atmosphere and background." What is James's final economic verdict on the saintly group of qualities, and what closing counsel does he give ("in our Father's house are many mansions")?
27. The lecture ends by turning the screw: the reader objects that religion "believes in two worlds and an invisible order" and must be judged by its truth, not its utility. James accepts the responsibility — and announces the next subject. What is it, and why must mysticism come before religious philosophy?

## Lecture XVI — Mysticism (Part One): The Four Marks and the Ladder of Examples

James declares that "personal religious experience has its root and centre in mystical states of consciousness," while confessing that his own constitution shuts him out from their enjoyment almost entirely, so he must speak of them at second hand. He first fences the word "mystical" with four marks, then climbs a ladder of examples from the simplest rudiment — a maxim suddenly seen in depth — through dreamy states and anaesthetic revelation, up to the sporadic cosmic consciousness of Bucke.

### Study Questions

1. Why does James restrict the word "mystical," as he earlier restricted "religion," by proposing marks instead of arguing about the word — and what reproach-use of the word ("vague and vast and sentimental") is he refusing?
2. State the four marks: ineffability, noetic quality, transiency, passivity. Which two, on James's ruling, are sufficient to entitle a state to be called mystical, and which two are "less sharply marked, but are usually found"?
3. Ineffability: the subject "immediately says that it defies expression." Why does James compare mystical states here to states of feeling rather than intellect — what do the symphony and the lover illustrate about the incompetence of outsiders' judgments?
4. Noetic quality: although like feelings, mystical states "seem to those who experience them to be also states of knowledge... illuminations, revelations, full of significance and importance." What tension between the first two marks does the whole lecture have to carry?
5. Transiency: "half an hour, or at most an hour or two" is the usual limit. What two things happen across recurrences — imperfect reproduction in memory, yet recognition and "continuous development in what is felt as inner richness and importance"?
6. Passivity: the mystic feels "as if his own will were in abeyance... as if he were grasped and held by a superior power." How does this connect mystical states to prophetic speech, automatic writing, and the mediumistic trance — and what distinguishes mystical states strictly so called from merely interruptive phenomena?
7. James applies the Lecture I method of serial study: phenomena are best understood "in their germ and in their over-ripe decay." What is the simplest rudiment of mystical experience — the deepened sense of the significance of a maxim or formula — and how does Luther's fellow-monk repeating "I believe in the forgiveness of sins" exemplify it ("It was as if I had found the door of paradise thrown wide open")?
8. Single words, effects of light, odors, and musical sounds can carry the same deepened significance: John Foster was transported by the word "hermit," and an old German lady's imagination was haunted by "Philadelphia." What does James say lyric poetry and music are alive in proportion to — and what happens to us when we lose "this mystical susceptibility"?
9. The next step up the ladder is the feeling of having "been here before." Sir James Crichton-Browne called these "dreamy states" and connected them with disturbances preceding epileptic attacks. Why does James call his view "absurdly alarmist" — and what general lesson does he draw about making a phenomenon "appear admirable or dreadful according to the context by which we set it off"?
10. Tennyson, in the footnote letter to B. P. Blood, describes repeating his own name silently until "individuality itself seemed to dissolve and fade away into boundless being... the clearest, the surest of the surest, utterly beyond words — where death was an almost laughable impossibility." Which of the four marks does his report exhibit, and what did he tell Professor Tyndall about delusion in the matter?
11. Charles Kingsley, walking the fields, was "oppressed now and then with an innate feeling that everything I see has a meaning, if I could but understand it." How does this milder case prepare for J. A. Symonds's extreme one?
12. Symonds's trance: a "gradual but swiftly progressive obliteration of space, time, sensation" until "nothing remained but a pure, absolute, abstract Self," with "the grim conviction that this state was the last state of the conscious Self" and the sense of having arrived at "demonstration of eternal Maya or illusion." Why did Symonds dislike the trance, what question did he ask on waking ("Which is the unreality?"), and why does James note that something in the recital is "suggestive of pathology" yet resist Crichton-Browne's verdict?
13. James next steps deliberately into a realm "branded as pathological": the consciousness produced by intoxicants. "Sobriety diminishes, discriminates, and says no; drunkenness expands, unites, and says yes. It is in fact the great exciter of the Yes function in man." Why must "our total opinion" of drunken consciousness "find its place in our opinion of that larger whole," the mystic consciousness?
14. Nitrous oxide: "Depth beyond depth of truth seems revealed to the inhaler," yet the truth fades at coming-to and any surviving words "prove to be the veriest nonsense" — while "the sense of a profound meaning having been there persists." What does this combination of marks (noetic claim with ineffable content) make the anaesthetic revelation a test case for?
15. James reports his own nitrous-oxide conclusion, "ever since remained unshaken": "our normal waking consciousness, rational consciousness as we call it, is but one special type of consciousness, whilst all about it, parted from it by the filmiest of screens, there lie potential forms of consciousness entirely different." What practical conclusions does he draw — that these forms "may determine attitudes though they cannot furnish formulas," and that they "forbid a premature closing of our accounts with reality"?
16. The keynote of James's own insight was "invariably a reconciliation... as if the opposites of the world... were melted into unity." Why does he suspect the same mystical mood set Hegel his task — and what does Benjamin Paul Blood's account ("each is all, in God... 'The One remains, the many change and pass'") share with it?
17. Xenos Clark, in the long footnote, calls the revelation "if anything, non-emotional... utterly flat" — "we are forever half a second too late" to understand the present. Why does James print this "farrago," and what does Blood's after-twenty-seven-years testimony ("the eye is fearless still") claim for the experience's value for life?
18. Symonds's chloroform experience: God "manifestly dealing with me, handling me, so to speak," followed by the horror of waking — "Why did you not kill me? Why would you not let me die?" — and his lingering question whether the inner sense of reality "was not a delusion but an actual experience." What question does James take from Symonds into the religious cases proper?
19. The English friend's ether dream (footnote): a great Power traveling on lightning made of the spirits of innumerable people, bending his course by means of her hurt — "he thought no more of me than a man thinks of hurting a cork when he is opening wine" — and the caught answer, "Knowledge and Love are One, and the measure is suffering." Why is this anaesthetic revelation so different in emotional tone from Blood's, and what does the difference foreshadow about the "unanimity" of mystics?
20. Nature awakens the mood: Amiel's "cosmogonic reveries" at Faucigny, above Lavey, and on the Northern Ocean shore ("as if they were visits of the Holy Ghost"); Malwida von Meysenbug kneeling before the ocean and learning "what prayer really is: to return from the solitude of individuation into the consciousness of unity with all that is"; Whitman's "Swiftly arose and spread around me the peace and knowledge that pass all the argument of the earth." What is common to the three?
21. J. Trevor walked to the Cat and Fiddle instead of chapel and "suddenly, without warning, I felt that I was in Heaven," bathed "in a warm glow of light." His test of the experiences: they "remain with him when brought closest into contact with the objective realities of life," whereas "dreams cannot stand this test." Is Trevor's test a fruit-test or an origin-test — and which kind has James taught us to use?
22. Dr. R. M. Bucke names the more distinctly characterized phenomena "cosmic consciousness": not an expansion of self-consciousness but "the superaddition of a function." Its prime characteristic is "a consciousness of the cosmos," with intellectual enlightenment, moral exaltation, and "a sense of immortality... not a conviction that he shall have this, but the consciousness that he has it already." How do these features map onto James's four marks?
23. Bucke's own onset: after an evening of poetry and philosophy, in the hansom, "I found myself wrapped in a flame-colored cloud... the fire was within myself"; he "saw that the universe is not composed of dead matter, but is, on the contrary, a living Presence," and that "the foundation principle of the world... is what we call love." The vision "lasted a few seconds," yet its conviction survived a quarter of a century "even during periods of the deepest depression." Which marks are most and least visible here?
24. Reflective: James has climbed from a deepened maxim to cosmic consciousness without leaving the four marks behind. Does the ladder persuade you that these are one kind of state in different degrees — or has the definition been stretched until it covers unlike things?

## Lecture XVII — Mysticism (Part Two): Methodical Cultivation, the Monistic Drift, and the Verdict on Authority

Where Lecture XVI gathered sporadic experiences, this lecture follows mysticism methodically cultivated — Hindu yoga, Buddhist dhyana, Sufi discipline in Al-Ghazzali's autobiography, and Christian orison in Saint John of the Cross, Saint Teresa, Saint Ignatius, and Jacob Boehme. James then states the theoretic drift of the classic tradition — optimism and monism, expressed by negation — and delivers his three-part verdict on what authority mystical states can claim.

### Study Questions

1. James turns from sporadic to methodical mysticism: "Hindus, Buddhists, Mohammedans, and Christians all have cultivated it methodically." What is yoga, in his definition — "the experimental union of the individual with the divine" — and by what exercises (diet, posture, breathing, concentration, moral discipline) is samadhi approached?
2. Vivekananda: the mind "has a higher state of existence, beyond reason, a superconscious state," in which there is "no feeling of I, and yet the mind works, desireless, free from restlessness, objectless, bodiless." How does the Vedantist test of purity match James's own test of religion's value — "its fruits must be good for life" (the man who comes out of samadhi remains "enlightened, a sage, a prophet, a saint")?
3. The Buddhist dhyana has four stages: concentration excluding desire but not judgment; the intellectual functions dropping off, unity remaining; satisfaction departing, indifference with memory and self-consciousness beginning; those perfected — and beyond them regions where the meditator says "There exists absolutely nothing," then "There are neither ideas nor absence of ideas," then stops finally. What does James's bracketed doubt about "memory" and "self-consciousness" in this connection admit about our understanding of such ladders?
4. Why does James quote a Moslem document at length — what "chief difficulty" for the student of non-Christian religions does the rarity of autobiography outside Christian literature create?
5. Al-Ghazzali's distinction: knowing the definitions of health, satiety, drunkenness, and abstinence versus being healthy, filled, drunk, abstinent. "The drunken man knows neither the definition of drunkenness... Being drunk, he knows nothing; whilst the physician, although not drunk, knows well in what drunkenness consists." How does this analogy state the first mark of mysticism from inside the tradition?
6. Al-Ghazzali's crisis: six months' hesitation at Bagdad, a paralysis of the tongue, then the surrender — "feeling my own weakness, and having entirely given up my own will, I repaired to God like a man in distress who has no more resources." What followed: two years in Syria, ten years of retreat, revelations "impossible either to describe or to point out," and the conclusion that "the Sufis are assuredly walking in the path of God." Which earlier lectures' patterns (divided self, self-surrender) does his story repeat in a Moslem key?
7. Al-Ghazzali argues by analogy with sleep: the understanding is a stage where "an eye opens" to objects sensation cannot comprehend; "just so in the prophetic the sight is illumined by a light which uncovers hidden things." And: "one knows only what one can comprehend" — the blind man and colors. Why does James call the incommunicableness of the transport "the keynote of all mysticism," and mystical truth something that "exists for the individual who has the transport, but for no one else"?
8. James notes it is "odd that Protestantism, especially evangelical Protestantism, should seemingly have abandoned everything methodical in this line," leaving it "to our mind-curers to reintroduce methodical meditation." What is orison, and what is its first aim — and what does Ignatius's *Spiritual Exercises* use to expel sensation?
9. Saint John of the Cross on the "union of love" reached by "dark contemplation": the soul "finds no terms, no means, no comparison" for its wisdom; the knowledge comes "clothed in none of the kinds of images," so that "we get neither form nor impression" — like a man seeing a kind of thing for the first time who "can understand it, use and enjoy it, but he cannot apply a name to it." Why does the soul feel placed "in a vast and profound solitude... an immense and boundless desert, desert the more delicious the more solitary it is"?
10. James declines to detail the Catholic stages: the subdivisions "seem to me to represent nothing objectively distinct... these experiences can be as infinitely varied as are the idiosyncrasies of individuals." What, then, is he "directly concerned with" in mystical states?
11. Saint Teresa's "orison of union": the soul "fully awake as regards God, but wholly asleep as regards things of this world," deprived of every feeling, unable even to think — "so a person who falls into a deep faint appears as if dead" — and afterwards "wholly impossible for her to doubt that she has been in God, and God in her." Her answer to how she can be certain of what she did not see during the union: the certitude comes later, "not by any vision, but by a certitude which abides with her and which God alone can give her." How does this handle the tension between ineffability and noetic quality?
12. Teresa's person who, after the grace, disbelieved the half-learned man who said God is in us only "by grace," because she now knew he is in everything "by presence, by power, or by essence": what kind of fruit is this — a truth learned, or a confidence gained? Does the distinction matter to James?
13. Saint Ignatius confessed to Father Laynez that "a single hour of meditation at Manresa had taught him more truths about heavenly things than all the teachings of all the doctors put together." Jacob Boehme at twenty-five, in a green at Görlitz, saw into the essences of herbs and grass, and in a quarter of an hour "saw and knew more than if I had been many years together at an university" — yet "it was impossible for me to explicate the same." George Fox "was come up to the state of Adam in which he was before he fell" and saw how all things had their names given them. What do these three illuminations claim to know, and what do they share with the nitrous-oxide revelation?
14. Teresa saw "in one instant how all things are seen and contained in God," as if the Deity were "an enormous and sovereignly limpid diamond," and while reciting the Athanasian Creed comprehended "in what way it is that one God can be in three Persons." Why does James pass over visions of the future, readings of hearts, and knowledge of distant events as less important than "theological or metaphysical" revelations?
15. The deliciousness of these states "seems to be beyond anything known in ordinary consciousness," verging on bodily pain, spoken of by "God's touches, the wounds of his spear," ebriety, and nuptial union. In raptus, breathing and circulation are so depressed that doctors debate whether the soul is temporarily dissevered from the body. Why does James insist that "to pass a spiritual judgment upon these states, we must not content ourselves with superficial medical talk, but inquire into their fruits for life"?
16. The fruits are various: stupefaction and other-worldliness in passive characters (Margaret Mary in the kitchen again) — but in natively strong minds "quite opposite results": Ignatius's mysticism made him "one of the most powerfully practical human engines that ever lived." Saint John of the Cross says a single "touch" may abolish imperfections the soul had vainly fought its whole life. Teresa emerges from ecstasy, often infirm beforehand, "full of health and admirably disposed for action," laughing at her former care for honour and money ("if human beings might only agree together to regard it as so much useless mud, what harmony would then reign in the world!"). What does this divergence of fruits do to any simple verdict on mysticism?
17. James states the theoretic drift of the majority of mystical states: "One of these directions is optimism, and the other is monism." We pass "as from a less into a more, as from a smallness into a vastness, and at the same time as from an unrest to a rest." Why do mystical states "appeal to the yes-function more than to the no-function in us" — and why is even the Upanishads' "No! no!" for the Atman "a denial made on behalf of a deeper yes"?
18. Dionysius the Areopagite, "the fountain-head of Christian mysticism," describes the cause of all things by negatives exclusively: "neither soul nor intellect... neither number, nor order, nor magnitude... not even divinity or goodness; nor even spirit as we know it." James's gloss: the qualifications are denied "not because the truth falls short of them, but because it so infinitely excels them" — it is "super-lucent, super-splendent, super-essential." Like Hegel's logic, mystics journey "towards the positive pole of truth only by the 'Methode der absoluten Negativitat.'" What does this dialectical use of negation accomplish that direct affirmation cannot?
19. The moral counterpart: denial of the finite self intertwines with the intellectual mystery — Boehme's Love which is Nothing ("I have nothing, for I am utterly stripped and naked; I can do nothing... I am nothing, for all that I am is no more than an image of Being"), and Paul's "I live, yet not I, but Christ liveth in me." James's summary: "This overcoming of all the usual barriers between the individual and the Absolute is the great mystic achievement... In Hinduism, in Neoplatonism, in Sufism, in Christian mysticism, in Whitmanism, we find the same recurring note." Why does he say the mystical classics have "neither birthday nor native land"?
20. "That art Thou!" say the Upanishads — "Not a part, not a mode of That, but identically That." Plotinus: the seer "does not properly see, does not distinguish or imagine two things... Absorbed in God, he makes but one with him, like a centre of a circle coinciding with another centre." Suso: the spirit "is lost in the stillness of the glorious dazzling obscurity and of the naked simple unity." Angelus Silesius: "Gott ist ein lauter Nichts." James concludes that such self-contradictory phrases — "dazzling obscurity," "whispering silence," "teeming desert" — prove that "not conceptual speech, but music rather, is the element through which we are best spoken to by mystical truth." What follows for how a student should read mystical scriptures?
21. Now the verdict, part one: mystical states "usually are, and have the right to be, absolutely authoritative over the individuals to whom they come." James's ground: mystical experiences "are as direct perceptions of fact for those who have them as any sensations ever were for us," and our own rational beliefs rest on evidence "exactly similar in nature." "The mystic is, in short, invulnerable." What are the limits of this concession — against whom does it hold, and against whom not?
22. Part two: "No authority emanates from them which should make it a duty for those who stand outside of them to accept their revelations uncritically" — at most, a presumption. Why does James say the mystics' appeal to unanimity "would only be an appeal to numbers... and the appeal to numbers has no logical force"?
23. And the presumption itself weakens on inspection: classic religious mysticism is "only a 'privileged case,'" an extract "carved out from a much larger mass." Mysticism has been ascetic and antinomian, dualistic in Sankhya and monistic in Vedanta; the great Spanish mystics "are anything but pantheists," their union "much more like an occasional miracle than like an original identity"; Whitman, Carpenter, and Jefferies differ again. James's conclusion: "the mystical feeling of enlargement, union, and emancipation has no specific intellectual content whatever of its own. It is capable of forming matrimonial alliances with material furnished by the most diverse philosophies and theologies." What prestige, then, can mysticism not lend to any special belief?
24. The other half of mysticism: "diabolical mysticism" in delusional insanity — the same sense of ineffable importance in the smallest events, the same voices and leadings and controlling by extraneous powers, "only this time the emotion is pessimistic: instead of consolations we have desolations." Both kinds spring, James says, "from that great subliminal or transmarginal region" where "'seraph and snake' abide... side by side. To come from thence is no infallible credential." By what methods must what comes from there be judged?
25. Part three: mystical states "break down the authority of the non-mystical or rationalistic consciousness... They show it to be only one kind of consciousness. They open out the possibility of other orders of truth." James adds that mystical states "do not contradict" the facts of sense but add "a supersensuous meaning" to them, so that "it is the rationalistic critic rather who plays the part of denier in the controversy." What image does he use for what mystical states might be — and why might the wider world, if real, still have "its valid experiences and its counterfeit ones," leaving us "liable to error just as we are now"?
26. James's final formulation: "They offer us hypotheses, hypotheses which we may voluntarily ignore, but which as thinkers we cannot possibly upset." And his borrowed measure of the stakes: "Oh, the little more, and how much it is; and the little less, and what worlds away!" What does he promise to argue in the last lecture about what "the religious consciousness requires to live on"?
27. Reflective: James grants the mystic everything for himself and nothing over anyone else. Is that a stable settlement — can a truth be authoritative for one person and merely a hypothesis for everyone else — or does the settlement quietly decide the question against the mystic?

## Lecture XVIII — Philosophy

Mysticism corroborated religion but too privately to warrant it universally. Philosophy publishes results that claim universal validity, so James now asks whether philosophy can stamp a warrant of veracity on the religious man's sense of the divine. His answer, confessed in advance, is that feeling is the deeper source of religion and theological formulas are secondary products — "like translations of a text into another tongue." The lecture tests dogmatic theology and modern idealism by one practical standard — do they in fact convince universally? — applies Peirce's principle of pragmatism to God's attributes, and closes by reassigning philosophy a humbler, critical office: a science of religions.

### Study Questions

1. James begins by voicing the audience's suspicion: having undermined mysticism's authority, he will now discredit philosophy's, concluding that religion "is nothing but an affair of faith." To what extent does he admit the guess is right — and what does he mean by calling theological formulas "secondary products, like translations of a text into another tongue"?
2. James's counterfactual: in a world where no religious feeling had ever existed, could any philosophic theology have been framed? Men would have criticised animistic explanations into scientific ones, he says, but "high-flying speculations" need a motive feeling supplies. What does he call such speculations — "over-beliefs, buildings-out performed by the intellect into directions of which feeling originally supplied the hint"?
3. Feeling, James concedes, "is private and dumb, and unable to give an account of itself," while philosophy aspires "to reclaim from mystery and paradox whatever territory she touches." Why, then, will philosophy "always have opportunity to labor at this task" — what three necessities (we construe our feelings intellectually even in soliloquy; the philosophic climate forces its clothing on us; we must exchange feelings with one another in general formulas) keep her employed?
4. James hopes these very lectures might count as "a crumb-like contribution" to a "Science of Religions." What distinguishes that science's intellectual operations from the intellectualism he wishes to discredit — that they are "operations after the fact, consequent upon religious feeling, not coordinate with it"?
5. The intellectualism he attacks "assumes to construct religious objects out of the resources of logical reason alone" and "reaches them in an a priori way, and warrants their veracity." Why have "warranted systems" been "the idols of aspiring souls" — what refuge do they seem to offer "spirits vexed by the muddiness and accidentality of the world of sensible things"?
6. Principal John Caird, quoted against himself: "the first question is, not how they feel, but what they think and believe... it is by the content or intelligent basis of a religion, and not by feeling, that its character and worth are to be determined." Cardinal Newman: theology is "the Science of God... just as we have a science of the stars and call it astronomy"; anything less and "I do not see much difference between avowing that there is no God, and implying that nothing definite can be known for certain about Him." What issue do both extracts set "clearly before us"?
7. James's test is "a perfectly plain one of fact": theology based on pure reason "must in point of fact convince men universally" — if it only formed sects and schools like sentiment, wherein would its superiority consist? What is his historical verdict, and his account of what logical reason actually does in this field — as in love, patriotism, and politics, where "our passions or our mystical intuitions fix our beliefs beforehand"?
8. The proofs of God's existence — cosmological, from design, moral, and *ex consensu gentium* — have had "the waves of unbelieving criticism breaking against them... slowly and surely washing out the mortar from between their joints." Why does James refuse to discuss them technically, and what does the bare fact that "all idealists since Kant have felt entitled either to scout or to neglect them" show? ("If you have a God already whom you believe in, these arguments confirm you. If you are atheistic, they fail to set you right.")
9. On the argument from design, what has Darwin done to it — and what does James's long footnote argue about order and disorder as "purely human inventions" (the thousand beans thrown on a table, from which any pattern can be selected)? Why does the footnote conclude that the argument "will be convincing only to those who on other grounds believe in him already"?
10. Systematic theology deduces God's attributes from his aseity: necessary and absolute; unlimited, hence infinitely perfect; one and only; spiritual and simple, his *essentia* and *esse* given at one stroke; immutable, since any potentiality actualised would be loss or gain; immense and eternal; knowing and willing himself in one eternal act; free *ad extra* but not *ad intra*; omniscient even of our free acts beforehand; creating *ex nihilo*; permitting moral evil but never willing it. James calls this a specimen and stops before the Trinity. What is the lecture's real question about this whole deduction — not whether it is valid, but what?
11. The digression on method: "the chief glory of English and Scottish thinkers" is to have kept in view that "every difference must make a difference." How do Locke on personal identity, Berkeley on matter, and Hume on causation each ask for a conception's "cash-value in terms of particular experience" — and why does James say it was they, not Kant, who introduced "the critical method" into philosophy?
12. Charles Sanders Peirce named the principle "pragmatism" (in "How to Make Our Ideas Clear," 1878): beliefs "are rules for action"; "the whole function of thinking is but one step in the production of active habits"; to develop a thought's meaning "we need therefore only determine what conduct it is fitted to produce." State the principle in Peirce's terms as James quotes them: our conception of an object's conceivable practical consequences "is for us the whole of our conception of the object."
13. Apply the principle to the metaphysical attributes: aseity, necessariness, immateriality, simplicity, indivisibility, actualised infinity, self-sufficiency, absolute felicity. James: "Pray, what specific act can I perform in order to adapt myself the better to God's simplicity? Or how does it assist me to plan my behavior, to know that his happiness is anyhow absolutely complete?" What verdict follows if such qualities "call for no distinctive adaptations of our conduct"?
14. James calls the systematic theologians "the closet-naturalists of the deity" (after Captain Mayne Reid's invective against collectors of skeletons and skins): the deduction of attributes is "a shuffling and matching of pedantic dictionary-adjectives... Instead of bread we have a stone; instead of a fish, a serpent." What, on his account, actually "keeps religion going" — and where do the attributes stand as "after-effects, secondary accretions"?
15. The moral attributes "stand on an entirely different footing": holiness (he can will nothing but the good), omnipotence (he can secure its triumph), omniscience (he can see us in the dark), justice (he can punish), love (he can pardon), unalterableness (we can count on him). Why do these "positively determine fear and hope and expectation" — and why does James still ask, "how stands it with her arguments?" for them?
16. "No! the book of Job went over this whole matter once for all and definitively... 'I have heard of Thee by the hearing of the ear, but now mine eye seeth Thee.'" What situation does James describe as that of the sincere religious man — "an intellect perplexed and baffled, yet a trustful sense of presence" — and what "definitive good-by" follows?
17. Modern idealism's basis is Kant's Transcendental Ego of Apperception — the "I think" that must be able to accompany all our objects — which Kant's successors converted into "an infinite concrete self-consciousness which is the soul of the world." What are the two Hegelian principles James summarises: that every object involves the notion of another which seems to negate it, and that "to be conscious of a negation is already virtually to be beyond it" ("the finite, realized as such, is already the infinite in posse")?
18. Principal Caird's performance: thought itself cannot be thought away, therefore an Absolute Thought exists; and religion, unlike morality, "changes aspiration into fruition" — "the very first pulsation of the spiritual life... is the indication that the division between the Spirit and its object has vanished." James grants that no description of the religious consciousness could be better — the saint "recognizes his own experience" in it. What, then, is his charge: that Caird has "simply reaffirmed the individual's experiences in a more generalized vocabulary"? What plain fact excuses James from proving the failure technically?
19. "What religion reports always purports to be a fact of experience: the divine is actually present, religion says... If definite perceptions of fact like this cannot stand upon their own feet, surely abstract reasoning cannot give them the support they are in need of. Conceptual processes can class facts, define them, interpret them; but they do not produce them... There is always a plus, a thisness, which feeling alone can answer for." State James's conclusion "in all sad sincerity."
20. Philosophy's positive office, if she will "abandon metaphysics and deduction for criticism and induction": eliminate the local and accidental from definitions; remove historic incrustations from dogma and worship; eliminate doctrines "now known to be scientifically absurd"; treat the residuum as hypotheses, refine them, distinguish "innocent over-belief and symbolism" from what is literally taken, and mediate between believers. Why might such a science command general adhesion "as is commanded by a physical science" — and why, like optics fed by seeing persons, could it "never get away from concrete life"?
21. The closing image: the philosopher's formulas "are like stereoscopic or kinetoscopic photographs seen outside the instrument; they lack the depth, the motion, the vitality." Why does James say "no one knows this as well as the philosopher" himself — and what two tasks does he set for his remaining lectures?
22. Reflective: James has now denied coercive warrant to both mysticism and philosophy, yet insists he is no lover of disorder and doubt. What is left standing for religious belief at the end of this lecture — and is "secondary" the same as "worthless," in his usage?

## Lecture XIX — Other Characteristics

James returns to description to finish the picture of the religious consciousness: the aesthetic element in the choice of a religion, the three classic elements of sacrifice, confession, and prayer — prayer, in the wide sense of inward communion, being "the very soul and essence of religion" — and finally the subconscious, whose door in deeply religious persons "seems unusually wide open." The genuineness of religion, he argues, stands or falls with whether something is genuinely transacted in prayer.

### Study Questions

1. James opens by correcting himself: he "spoke too contemptuously" of the scholastic attributes, because he neglected their one use — the aesthetic. Newman's enumeration of them is like intoning a cathedral service; epithets "lend an atmosphere and overtones to our devotion... just as it enriches a church to have an organ and old brasses, marbles and frescoes and stained windows." What does this concession show about the pragmatic test itself?
2. Some minds aim at "intellectual purity and simplification"; for others "richness is the supreme imaginative requirement." The second type needs "something institutional and complex, majestic in the hierarchic interrelatedness of its parts," before which evangelical Protestantism appears flat — "an almshouse for a palace" beside the piled-up structure. James compares it to the patriotic sentiment of ancient empires meeting "a president in a black coat who shakes hands with you." What does he conclude about Protestantism's power to make converts from Catholicism — and about whether the two will ever understand each other ("their centres of emotional energy are too different")?
3. To intellectual Catholics, many antiquated beliefs are childish "in the pleasing sense of 'childlike'"; to the Protestant, childish "in the sense of being idiotic falsehoods." What does this contrast add to the lecture's theme?
4. Sacrifice: burnt offerings are superseded in Judaism, Islam, Buddhism, and Christianity (save as transfigured in Christ's atonement) by "offerings of the heart, renunciations of the inner self." Why does James pass over sacrifice quickly here — where has he already said his say about it?
5. Confession "corresponds to a more inward and moral stage of sentiment" and is "part of the general system of purgation and cleansing." "For him who confesses, shams are over and realities have begun; he has exteriorized his rottenness." Why does James find the decay of confession in Anglo-Saxon communities "a little hard to account for" — and what does he say English-speaking Protestants seem to find enough?
6. Prayer must be taken in the wide sense: "every kind of inward communion or conversation with the power recognized as divine." On prayers for the sick, James states that prayer "may contribute to recovery, and should be encouraged as a therapeutic measure"; on weather, that "droughts and storms follow from physical antecedents, and moral appeals cannot avert them." Why does scientific criticism leave prayer in the wide sense "untouched"?
7. Sabatier, quoted at length: "Prayer is religion in act; that is, prayer is real religion... Wherever this interior prayer is lacking, there is no religion." Why is "natural religion," so called, "not properly a religion" on this view — what does it cut man off from, leaving "but the spectators' part at a play"?
8. James's stake: if in the prayerful consciousness nothing is "really transacted," if "the world is in no whit different for its having taken place," then religion "must on the whole be classed... as being rooted in delusion altogether." Yet he allows it "may well prove that the sphere of influence in prayer is subjective exclusively." What is the fundamental religious point that survives either way — "that in prayer, spiritual energy, which otherwise would slumber, does become active, and spiritual work of some kind is effected really"?
9. F. W. H. Myers's letter: around us a spiritual universe from which comes the energy maintaining the material; prayer is "the general name for that attitude of open and earnest expectancy" by which we draw it in; to whom we pray "does not much matter"; "Better let children pray to Christ... But it would be rash to say that Christ himself hears us." Why does James say this letter shows "how independent the prayer-instinct is of usual doctrinal complications"?
10. George Müller of Bristol (died 1898): five orphanages, thousands of orphans kept and educated, over two million copies of Scripture distributed, nearly a million and a half pounds received and administered — and an estate of a hundred and sixty pounds at his death. His method: general wants public, temporary necessities told to no human being; never running up bills, "Owe no man anything"; praying for a lost key, a late appointment, an unintelligible passage. His prime motive in building by prayer alone: "to have something to point to as a visible proof that our God and Father is the same faithful God that he ever was." When after breakfast there were no means for dinner for more than a hundred persons, he felt "greater and more manifest nearness of the Lord's presence" than ever. What kind of evidence is Müller's career, and for what exactly?
11. James's judgment of Müller is double: "a case extreme in every respect," his God "his business partner," his horizon extraordinarily narrow, "absolutely unphilosophical," his conception continuing "the traditions of the most primitive human thought" — yet James lets his case suffice for the whole literature of answers to prayer. What does the comparison with Emerson's or Phillips Brooks's mind show about "the range which the religious consciousness covers"?
12. The footnote case of Robert Lyde, the English sailor of 1689 who with a boy overcame seven Frenchmen, finding the marlin spike and then his knife each time as God's direct answer to "Lord! what shall I do?" Why does James print this "even more primitive style of religious thought" without comment?
13. Dr. Hilty's "led" life: books, words, and people arrive "just at the very moment in which one needs them"; dangers are glided over "as if with shut eyes"; paths not to be wandered on are "hedged off with thorns"; affairs settle themselves "neither too early nor too late"; one walks continually through "open doors." How does this subtler guidance differ from Müller's petitional order — and why would it "appear to countless Christians... as if transcribed from their own personal experience"?
14. James then shades to a third position: not that events are tempered toward us, but that "we are tempered more towardly for their reception. The outward face of nature need not alter, but the expressions of meaning in it alter. It was dead and is alive again" — like looking on a person with love. Whose spirit is this — Marcus Aurelius and Epictetus, mind-curers, transcendentalists, "liberal" Christians — and how does Martineau's sermon state it ("it is not the want of greater miracles, but of the soul to perceive such as are allowed us still")?
15. Father Gratry, in his melancholy youth, was consoled by a poor drummer beating the tattoo perfectly in a Paris street: "Good is at least possible, I said, since the ideal can thus sometimes get embodied." Senancour's Obermann meets a jonquil in bloom in March: "It was the strongest expression of desire... I felt all the happiness destined for man." Why does James place these two small casual experiences beside the prayerful life — what do they share with the convert's vivified face of the world (and contrast with the melancholiac's withdrawal of expression from it)?
16. The lecture's final topic: religion's manifestations "so frequently connect themselves with the subconscious part of our existence." James says you will hardly find a religious leader of any kind "in whose life there is no record of automatisms" — Saint Paul's visions, ecstasies, and gift of tongues; the Bernards, Loyolas, Luthers, Foxes, Wesleys with their "visions, voices, rapt conditions, guiding impressions, and 'openings.'" What theological consequence follows: why do "incursions from beyond the transmarginal region have a peculiar power to increase conviction," and why are motor automatisms "even more convincing than sensations"?
17. Inspiration discriminates the leaders: in the Buddha, Jesus, Saint Paul, Augustine, Huss, Luther, Wesley, automatic composition was only occasional; in the Hebrew prophets, Mohammed, Fox, and Joseph Smith, frequent or habitual. Sanday on the prophets: insight comes "sharp and sudden," as "an overpowering force from without, against which he struggles, but in vain" — "The Lord spake thus to me with a strong hand" — until "the personality of the prophet sinks entirely into the background; he feels himself for the time being the mouthpiece of the Almighty." Prophecy was also a profession, with schools, and could be counterfeited. What do both halves of that account do to the evidential value of inspiration?
18. Philo of Alexandria: coming to his work empty, he would "suddenly become full; ideas being in an invisible manner showered upon me, and implanted in me from on high," knowing neither place, company, self, nor what he was writing. Mohammed's revelations, per Nöldeke's summary, came with a bell-sound, by Gabriel in human form, in dreams, or by God immediately — eight kinds in one list. Joseph Smith's were predominantly sensorial, begun with the "peep-stones" — "apparently a case of 'crystal gazing.'" Why does James catalogue these differences of mechanism so carefully?
19. The conclusion that closes the circle with Lecture I: call the sunlit level the A-region and the other the B-region if "subliminal" smells of psychical research. The B-region is "the larger part of each of us," abode of inactive memories, springs of passions and prejudices, intuitions, dreams, mystical experiences, automatisms, delusions, "and much that feeds our religion." James's conclusion: "in persons deep in the religious life... the door into this region seems unusually wide open." What has been gained by renaming the subconscious neutrally — and what has not been settled by the conclusion?
20. Reflective: James has staked religion's genuineness on prayer's transactiveness, and located its characteristic phenomena at a wide-open subliminal door. Do these two claims support each other, or pull apart?

## Lecture XX — Conclusions (with the Postscript)

James sums up the whole survey: religion's common characteristics, stated as beliefs and as psychological marks; his emphatic "No" to the question whether all lives should show identical religious elements; his repudiation of the "survival theory" that religion is a mere anachronism; the common nucleus of uneasiness and its solution; and his own hypothesis — that the "more" with which the saved self feels continuous is, on its hither side, the subconscious continuation of our conscious life. The Postscript then names his own position: a piecemeal supernaturalism, with immortality left open and even a pluralistic, finite God not excluded by the evidence.

### Study Questions

1. James's summary of the religious life: (1) the visible world is part of a more spiritual universe from which it draws its chief significance; (2) union or harmonious relation with that higher universe is our true end; (3) prayer or inner communion with the spirit thereof "is a process wherein work is really done, and spiritual energy flows in and produces effects, psychological or material, within the phenomenal world"; (4) a new zest added to life, as lyrical enchantment or appeal to earnestness and heroism; (5) assurance of safety, a temper of peace, and a preponderance of loving affections. How much of the preceding nineteen lectures is packed into these five points?
2. Why did James choose extreme examples? "To learn the secrets of any science, we go to expert specialists, even though they may be eccentric persons, and not to commonplace pupils." What is the reader's duty once the experts have been heard — and what practical question then remains for each of us ("what are the dangers in this element of life, and in what proportion may it need to be restrained")?
3. Ought the mixture of religion with other elements to be identical in all men? "No," emphatically: "No two of us have identical difficulties, nor should we be expected to work out identical solutions"; "If an Emerson were forced to be a Wesley, or a Moody forced to be a Whitman, the total human consciousness of the divine would suffer." Each attitude is "a syllable in human nature's total message." Why must a "god of battles" be allowed to be the god for one kind of person, and a god of peace and heaven and home for another?
4. The footnote on the two types: the contrasts between healthy-minded and morbid, once-born and twice-born, "cease to be the radical antagonisms which many think them." The twice-born outlook, holding more of evil in solution, is "the wider and completer" — evil "not evaded, but sublated in the higher religious cheer" — yet "the final consciousness which each type reaches of union with the divine has the same practical significance for the individual." Why, then, may individuals be allowed to reach it "by the channels which lie most open to their several temperaments"?
5. Could the science of religions itself serve as our religion? James recalls Al-Ghazzali: to understand the causes of drunkenness as a physician does "is not to be drunk." Renan exemplifies how breadth of knowledge may make one "only a dilettante in possibilities, and blunt the acuteness of one's living faith." "Knowledge about life is one thing; effective occupation of a place in life, with its dynamic currents passing through your being, is another." What follows for the relation of this very book to its subject?
6. The science of religions also faces an inner difficulty: constituted, she must decide how far religion's beliefs are true, yet the other sciences are incomplete and conflicting, the scientist in his scientific hours is materialistic, and acquaintance with "groveling and horrible superstitions" breeds the presumption "that any belief that is religious probably is false." Why does James think her conclusions are "as likely to be adverse as they are to be favorable"?
7. State the "survival theory" as James states it. Religion's pivot is "the interest of the individual in his private personal destiny" — "a monumental chapter in the history of human egotism." Science has "utterly repudiated the personal point of view"; the God she recognizes "must be a God of universal laws exclusively, a God who does a wholesale, not a retail business"; our private selves are "bubbles on the foam," epiphenomena in Clifford's word, whose "destinies weigh nothing and determine nothing." Religion, on this view, is "pure anachronism," and deanthropomorphization the remedy. Where is this theory strongest?
8. The long footnotes quote the old natural theology James's grandfathers read: Christian Wolff on the sun's utility and on water ("When one goes into a grinding-mill one sees that the grindstone must always be kept wet"), and Derham on the variety of men's faces, voices, and handwriting — "A God so careful as to make provision even for the unmistakable signing of bank checks and deeds was a deity truly after the heart of eighteenth century Anglicanism." Why does James print these grotesques at such length in his final lecture?
9. James's rebuttal begins: "so long as we deal with the cosmic and the general, we deal only with the symbols of reality, but as soon as we deal with private and personal phenomena as such, we deal with realities in the completest sense of the term." The cosmic objects are "but ideal pictures of something whose existence we do not inwardly possess but only point at outwardly, while the inner state is our very experience itself." The unsharable feeling of "the pinch of his individual destiny" fills up "the measure of our concrete actuality." "The axis of reality runs solely through the egotistic places, — they are strung upon it like so many beads." How does the bill-of-fare image clinch the argument — and what would a bill of fare with one real raisin on it be?
10. Hence: "By being religious we establish ourselves in possession of ultimate reality at the only points at which reality is given us to guard." Individuality is founded in feeling, and "the recesses of feeling, the darker, blinder strata of character, are the only places in the world in which we catch real fact in the making." The intellectual world, by contrast, is like stereoscopic pictures seen outside the instrument — "where in the picture... is the energy or the fifty miles an hour?" How does this explain why James was "so individualistic throughout these lectures"?
11. A footnote entertains a reversal: miraculous healings, dismissed as figments, are now allowed as effects of "suggestion"; even Saint Francis's stigmata "may on these terms not be a fable"; possession is being admitted as "hystero-demonopathy"; "even 'prophecy,' even 'levitation,' might creep into the pale." "Progress may follow a spiral rather than a straight line." What does this possibility do to the survival theory's confidence?
12. James now aims expressly low: "to reduce religion to its lowest admissible terms, to that minimum, free from individualistic excrescences, which all religions contain as their nucleus," on which the "ruddier additional beliefs" — the over-beliefs — may be grafted. His own over-belief will be "of a somewhat pallid kind, as befits a critical philosopher." Why does he think a small but solid result is worth this anticlimax?
13. The first conclusion: "the feelings on the one hand and the conduct on the other are almost always the same, for Stoic, Christian, and Buddhist saints are practically indistinguishable in their lives. The theories which Religion generates, being thus variable, are secondary." Between feeling and conduct "the short circuit exists on which she carries on her principal business," while ideas, symbols, and institutions form "loop-lines" — perfections, perhaps, but "not organs with an indispensable function." Restate this conclusion in your own words, and test it against one case from the lectures.
14. The feelings are a "sthenic" affection (Kant's word), "an excitement of the cheerful, expansive, 'dynamogenic' order which, like any tonic, freshens our vital powers" — Leuba's "faith-state." Tolstoy classes faith "among the forces by which men live"; its total absence, anhedonia, "means collapse." The faith-state "may hold a very minimum of intellectual content" — Bucke's raptures, or "a mere vague enthusiasm... a feeling that great and wondrous things are in the air." What does Henri Perreyve's letter (overwhelmed by happiness, walking a mountain path "like a madman," halted by instinct at a precipice's edge) show about the faith-state's primacy of impulse over direction?
15. Leuba's formulation, quoted by James: "God is not known, he is not understood; he is used — sometimes as meat-purveyor, sometimes as moral support, sometimes as friend, sometimes as an object of love... Not God, but life, more life, a larger, richer, more satisfying life, is, in the last analysis, the end of religion." At this subjective rating, religion "cannot be a mere anachronism and survival, but must exert a permanent function, whether she be with or without intellectual content, and whether, if she have any, it be true or false." Does this vindication satisfy you — and what question does James say must next be asked?
16. The common nucleus: religions meet in a uniform deliverance of two parts — (1) an uneasiness, "a sense that there is something wrong about us as we naturally stand"; (2) its solution, "a sense that we are saved from the wrongness by making proper connection with the higher powers." In developed minds "the wrongness takes a moral character, and the salvation takes a mystical tinge." Test this formula against two documents from earlier lectures of your choosing.
17. The formula of the solution: the man "identifies his real being with the germinal higher part of himself," becoming conscious "that this higher part is conterminous and continuous with a MORE of the same quality, which is operative in the universe outside of him, and which he can keep in working touch with, and in a fashion get on board of and save himself when all his lower being has gone to pieces in the wreck." James says the formula "allows for the divided self and the struggle; involves the change of personal centre and the surrender of the lower self; expresses the appearance of exteriority of the helping power and yet accounts for our sense of union with it." Which earlier lectures does each clause summarize?
18. The question of objective truth turns on the "more": "Is such a 'more' merely our own notion, or does it really exist? If so, in what shape does it exist? Does it act, as well as exist?" The theologies all agree it exists and acts; their "inveterate disputes" concern the union — pantheism and theism, works and grace and karma, immortality and reincarnation. Why does James refuse to begin from the Christian theology's definitions (the "more" as Jehovah, union as imputed righteousness) — what would that be, in his vocabulary?
19. James's hypothesis: "whatever it may be on its farther side, the 'more' with which in religious experience we feel ourselves connected is on its hither side the subconscious continuation of our conscious life." Myers (1892): "Each of us is in reality an abiding psychical entity far more extensive than he knows... there is always some part of the Self unmanifested." On this hypothesis the theologian's external power is vindicated, since invasions from the subconscious "take on objective appearances, and suggest to the Subject an external control" — and the sense of union is "not merely apparently, but literally true." Why does James call this "only a doorway"?
20. Beyond the doorway, "the over-beliefs begin": monistic interpretations, the prophets' visions and voices authenticating incompatible faiths. Those not personally favored with revelations "must stand outside of them altogether" and decide that, corroborating incompatible doctrines, "they neutralize one another and leave no fixed result." Yet "over-beliefs in various directions are absolutely indispensable," to be treated "with tenderness and tolerance so long as they are not intolerant themselves" — "the most interesting and valuable things about a man are usually his over-beliefs." What disciplines our choice among them, if evidence cannot?
21. James's own over-belief: the further limits of our being plunge into "an altogether other dimension of existence," not merely ideal, "for it produces effects in this world... But that which produces effects within another reality must be termed a reality itself, so I feel as if we had no philosophic excuse for calling the unseen or mystical world unreal." "God is the natural appellation... for the supreme reality." "We and God have business with each other; and in opening ourselves to his influence our deepest destiny is fulfilled." His summary: "God is real since he produces real effects." How far is this from Leuba's "God is used" — and how near?
22. The farther step of faith: God's existence as "the guarantee of an ideal order that shall be permanently preserved," in which "tragedy is only provisional and partial, and shipwreck and dissolution are not the absolutely final things." Only here does religion bring "a real hypothesis into play": "The world interpreted religiously... must have, over and above the altered expression, a natural constitution different at some point from that which a materialistic world would have. It must be such that different events can be expected in it, different conduct must be required." Why does James call this "thoroughly 'pragmatic'" view "the deeper way" — and what does he charge transcendentalist metaphysicians with attempting?
23. His personal venture: "the world of our present consciousness is only one out of many worlds of consciousness that exist... the two become continuous at certain points, and higher energies filter in." Against the sectarian scientist's attitude he hears the inward monitor of which Clifford wrote, "whispering the word 'bosh!'" The lecture ends: "Who knows whether the faithfulness of individuals here below to their own poor over-beliefs may not actually help God in turn to be more effectively faithful to his own greater tasks?" What has happened to the God of the scholastic attributes by the time James reaches this sentence?
24. The Postscript classifies James himself: between "crasser" (piecemeal) and "refined" (universalistic) supernaturalism, his belief "that in communion with the Ideal new force comes into the world, and new departures are made here below" places him with the piecemeal kind — miracles and providential leadings admitted, influences interpolated among the forces that determine the real world's details. Refined supernaturalism "surrenders too easily to naturalism": its Absolute "condescends to no transactions of detail" and "can raise no particular weight whatever." James's ground: "I find it hard to believe that principles can exist which make no difference in facts." His reading of Karma agrees — "judgment" there "carries execution with it." Why does he state all this "bluntly," like "a man who must set his back against an open door quickly"?
25. On immortality James has said nothing in the lectures, "for to me it seems a secondary point." Facts are "yet lacking to prove 'spirit-return,'" though he respects "the patient labors of Messrs. Myers, Hodgson, and Hyslop" and is "somewhat impressed by their favorable conclusions" — so he leaves the matter open. Why is this reticence consistent with his method?
26. Finally, the bounds of testimony: religious experience "cannot be cited as unequivocally supporting the infinitist belief. The only thing that it unequivocally testifies to is that we can experience union with something larger than ourselves and in that union find our greatest peace." The larger power "need not be infinite, it need not be solitary. It might conceivably even be only a larger and more godlike self... and the universe might conceivably be a collection of such selves... Thus would a sort of polytheism return upon us." Against the monist's demand for complete security, James answers with common sense: partial and conditional salvation is a familiar notion; "For practical life at any rate, the chance of salvation is enough... The existence of the chance makes the difference, as Edmund Gurney says, between a life of which the keynote is resignation and a life of which the keynote is hope." Is this a satisfying end to twenty lectures — and on James's own terms, what would count as satisfying?

## Capstone Questions — The Whole Book

1. James's rule from Lecture I — judge religious states by their fruits, not their roots — is applied again and again: to ecstasy, to saintliness, to mysticism, to philosophy itself. Trace the rule through at least four lectures. Does James ever break it, and if so, where?
2. Two anthropologies run through the book: the healthy-minded once-born, for whom evil is a maladjustment to be ignored away, and the sick soul, for whom evil is radical and real. James calls the twice-born outlook "the wider and completer," yet refuses to prescribe it for everyone. Reconstruct his reasons on both sides.
3. The divided self (Lecture VIII) is unified in conversion (Lectures IX–X), described again as character's shifting centre of energy (Lecture XI), and finally formulated as the identification of the self with its higher part, continuous with the "more" (Lecture XX). Tell this one story across the four treatments. What changes at each retelling?
4. Sudden conversion, mystical illumination, and answers to prayer are all explained by James through incursions from the subliminal region. What does this single hypothesis unify — and what does James carefully refuse to let it decide?
5. Saintliness is both the finest fruit of religion and the source of its worst excesses: the same charity produces Weaver's offered cheek and the theopathic uselessness of Margaret Mary. Using James's own tests — strength of soul, purity, charity, and the "economic" verdict of Lecture XV — state where the line falls, and whether it can be drawn in advance.
6. The four marks of mysticism (ineffability, noetic quality, transiency, passivity) are proposed in Lecture XVI and used as a measuring rod in XVII. Apply them to three experiences from the book — for instance Bucke's cosmic consciousness, Teresa's orison of union, and the nitrous-oxide revelation. Which experiences satisfy all four marks, and does James's three-part verdict on mystical authority survive the application?
7. Lecture XVIII concludes that demonstration of religious truth by intellectual processes is "absolutely hopeless," and reassigns philosophy the humbler office of sifting hypotheses. Lecture XX then offers James's own hypothesis about the "more." Is the hypothesis philosophy, over-belief, or science of religions — and by what test would James himself classify it?
8. Prayer is called "the very soul and essence of religion" (Sabatier, Lecture XIX), and Lecture XX makes prayer's transactiveness one of religion's three defining beliefs. George Müller's orphanages and Hilty's "led" life are offered as the phenomenon at two levels of crudity. What exactly must be true for James's stake — that something is "really transacted" — to hold, and what may remain undecided?
9. James defines religion in Lecture II without churches, then spends Lecture XIV on what churches do to it ("Piety is the mask, the inner force is tribal instinct"). Is his treatment of institutions consistent with his definition? What, on his account, are institutions for, and what do they reliably corrupt?
10. The metaphysical attributes are dismissed in Lecture XVIII as having no cash-value, then partly rehabilitated in Lecture XIX for their aesthetic value, as Newman intones them like a cathedral service. Can a formula be pragmatically empty and religiously valuable at once? What does James's answer imply for his pragmatic test itself?
11. Evil is handled three ways in the book: ignored by healthy-mindedness (Lectures IV–V), suffered as the essence of reality by the sick soul (VI–VII), and "sublated" in the twice-born cheer (Lecture XX's footnote). Which handling does James finally endorse, and what evidence from the documents decides him?
12. Compare the book's two great sceptical challenges: medical materialism in Lecture I, which explains religion away by its origins, and the survival theory in Lecture XX, which explains it away by its primitiveness. How do James's two answers differ in strategy, and which is stronger?
13. "Over-beliefs" are indispensable, unverifiable, and to be tolerated unless intolerant. James's own over-belief — a wider world of consciousness, continuous with ours at points where higher energies filter in — is stated in Lecture XX and classed as piecemeal supernaturalism in the Postscript. State it precisely. Then state one over-belief from the documents that James treats with tenderness and one he treats as an excrescence, and give his grounds in each case.
14. Leuba says God "is not known, he is not understood; he is used." James says "God is real since he produces real effects." Are these the same doctrine in different dress, or opposed? Answer from the texts of Lectures XIX and XX.
15. The subconscious is James's mediating term: it lets the theologian's external power and the psychologist's inner process both be true. Critics might call this having it both ways. Reconstruct the strongest version of that objection from the book's own materials (Lectures X, XVII, XIX), and then James's best reply from Lecture XX.
16. James insists the religious question is "primarily a question of life, of living or not living in the higher union," yet he also insists ideas can be "essential to that individual's religion" because the excitement often fails until particular beliefs "come home" to a person. How do these two claims fit together — and what do they imply for how a teacher should present this book?
17. Across twenty lectures James quotes Catholics and Protestants, Hindus and Buddhists, Sufis and Quakers, mind-curers and revivalists, and finds the feelings and conduct "almost always the same" while the theories vary. What, then, is the "varieties" of his title finally a variety *of* — and what is not various at all?
18. The book opens with a Frenchman in a panic of horror at his own epileptic double, and closes with a God who may be finite, plural, and helped by our faithfulness to our over-beliefs. Trace the path between those two points. At which single lecture does the book's centre of gravity shift from describing experiences to judging them?
19. James leaves immortality open, God's infinitude unsupported, and salvation possibly partial — yet calls his conclusions "small, but... solid." List what he claims stands on evidence, what stands as hypothesis, and what stands as personal over-belief. Does the sorting hold up under his own criteria of Lecture I (immediate luminousness, philosophical reasonableness, moral helpfulness)?
20. Final question, to be sat with: James writes that the faithfulness of individuals to their own poor over-beliefs may "help God in turn to be more effectively faithful to his own greater tasks." Whether or not you share the over-belief, what would it mean to study this book — its converts, saints, and mystics — in that spirit rather than as a museum of other people's experiences?
