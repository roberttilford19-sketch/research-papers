# Spring-heeled Jack: The Source Base

## Stage One of the definitive work — the tiered corpus, built 18 October 2026

This document is the foundation, not the building. It assembles everything the
definitive work on Spring-heeled Jack will be allowed to stand on: the earliest
retrievable source for each report cluster, the later retellings separated from
the contemporary record, the modern scholarship weighed, and an honest count of
what actually survives checking. The narrative itself — the map, the distances,
the anniversary patterns, the fair skeptical case — belongs to a later stage and
is deliberately not written here.

The method is the one adopted for this project in October 2026: verify each
original report for embellishment, plot locations, measure distances, look for
date patterns, present the skeptical case fairly, and salvage only the genuinely
unanswered points. Everything below is organised so that method can be applied
without re-litigating the sources.

### How the tiers work

Every claim in this file carries one of three documentation tiers.

**Tier 1 — Contemporary-documented.** A source produced at the time: a newspaper
report within days or weeks of the event, a court or police-court record, a sworn
statement, an official proceeding, or a near-contemporary official summary such as
the *Annual Register*. Tier 1 proves the report was made and, in the best cases,
that an institution acted on it. It does not by itself prove the report was true.

**Tier 2 — Later-attested.** The report survives only through a source written
years or decades afterwards: a memoir, a retrospective newspaper letter, a
folklorist's collection, a Fortean compilation. Tier 2 material may preserve
genuine memory, but it has passed through the legend's retelling machinery and
must be discounted accordingly.

**Tier 3 — Folklore-only.** No retrievable source earlier than the modern
legend-literature itself: local-lore websites, unsourced retellings, and — the
extreme case — episodes a modern scholar has shown to be fabricated outright.
Tier 3 material is evidence about the legend, never about the events.

A note on counting. The podcast mapping that prompted this project counted
roughly 130 "reports" between 1803 and 2012. That figure counts individual
mentions, echoes, and press repetitions. This file counts **clusters**: a named
incident or local scare with its own paper trail. On that stricter unit the whole
corpus, precursors and analogues included, comes to about forty clusters. Both
counts are honest; they measure different things, and the definitive work should
say which one it is using whenever it gives a number.

## Part One — The primary stratum: London, 1837–38

### 1. The Mansion House hearing and the Peckham letter (January 1838) — Tier 1

The legend's documentary life begins not with an attack but with a letter. On
9 January 1838, at a public session in the Mansion House, the Lord Mayor of
London, Sir John Cowan, read out an anonymous complaint he had received days
earlier and initially withheld. The writer, signing himself "a resident of
Peckham," alleged that a party of young men of rank had laid a wager that one of
their number could not visit the villages around London in three disguises — "a
ghost, a bear, and a devil" — alarming households, and that the prankster had
already "deprived seven ladies of their senses." The letter further accused the
press of knowing the story and suppressing it. A member of the audience stood
and corroborated that servant girls around Kensington, Hammersmith and Ealing
were telling "dreadful stories of this ghost or devil." *The Times* reported the
session on 9 January 1838 and the follow-up proceedings on 10 and 12 January; the
next day the Lord Mayor displayed a pile of similar letters from across suburban
London — Hammersmith women frightened into "dangerous fits," people in
Stockwell, Brixton, Camberwell and Vauxhall said (by correspondents, note, not by
any verified record) to have died of fright, and the figure "repeatedly seen in
Lewisham and Blackheath."

The earliest retrievable sources are the *Times* reports of 9–12 January 1838.
The Peckham letter itself is known through those reports and through Jacqueline
Simpson's *Spring-Heeled Jack* (2001), which quotes it. The Lord Mayor's own
position is on the record and is usefully two-minded: he believed "the greatest
exaggerations" had been made and that no ghost performed "the feats of a devil
upon earth," yet a trusted informant had told him of a Forest Hill servant girl
scared into fits by a figure in a bear's skin, and he was confident the
"pantomime display" would be punished. Police were instructed to search, and
rewards were offered. All of that — the hearing, the wager theory, the official
response — is Tier 1. The letter's *contents* (the wager, the seven ladies) are
one anonymous man's allegations, and the definitive work must keep that
distinction bright.

**The vigilante question.** Popular retellings give January 1838 a vigilante
committee of magistrates and officers, and give the elderly Duke of Wellington a
personal role, riding out armed on horseback at nearly seventy to hunt the fiend
down. The committee-and-posse version appears in later summaries (Jacqueline
Simpson's Folklore Society newsletter account among them) but no contemporary
source for Wellington's participation has been produced by any researcher, Dash
included, and the story in its full form — nightly patrols, near-captures, the
Duke admitting defeat — circulates only in modern popular retellings (it is still
printed as fact by mainstream heritage sites). Tier 3, with one important
exception: armed vigilante patrols *are* Tier 1 for the precursor scare of
1803–04 at Hammersmith, where a patrolman's shot killed an innocent man and the
whole thing was thrashed out in court. The Wellington episode looks very much
like that documented 1804 machinery, transplanted thirty-four years forward and
given a famous name. The definitive work should say so.

### 2. The attack on Jane Alsop (19–20 February 1838) — Tier 1; the strongest report in the corpus

On the night of 19–20 February 1838, at her father's house at Old Ford, Bow,
eighteen-year-old Jane Alsop answered a knock to find a cloaked man claiming to
be a police officer: "Bring a light — we have caught Spring-heeled Jack here in
the lane." When she handed him the candle he threw back his cloak, "presented a
most hideous and frightful appearance," vomited blue and white flame, showed
eyes like "red balls of fire," and — wearing a large helmet and tight
white-oilskin clothing — seized her and tore at her gown, neck and arms with
claws she was certain were "of some metallic substance." A sister dragged her
inside; the man fled.

The paper trail is the best the legend has. *The Times* carried the case on
2 March 1838 under the heading "The Late Outrage at Old Ford," and *The Times*
of 22 February 1838 also carried related police-court matter (an excerpt survives
via Lee Jackson's *Dictionary of Victorian London* and is still being cited in
2025). The *Annual Register* for 1838 (published 1839) summarised the case at
page 23. Jane's account was given to the police; her injuries and her family's
corroborating testimony were part of the contemporary coverage. Most
decisively, the case generated a court proceeding: Thomas Millbank, who had
boasted in the Morgan's Arms pub that he *was* Spring-heeled Jack, was arrested
by Constable James Lea (the officer who had arrested William Corder, the Red
Barn murderer, a decade earlier) and tried at Lambeth Street police court.
Millbank had been wearing white overalls and a greatcoat; the greatcoat, the
overalls, and the dropped candle were found. He escaped conviction on a single
point: Jane Alsop insisted her attacker breathed fire, and Millbank could not.
That is a Tier 1 incident with a named victim, named police officer, named
suspect, physical exhibits, and an outcome — the fullest documentary skeleton
any Jack case possesses.

Two cautions belong in the same breath. First, the fire-breathing is the hinge
of the whole case: it acquitted Millbank, and later writers (David Castleton's
modern synthesis among them) note the period detail that both flame attacks
involved a light source at chest height — the candle Jane herself supplied, the
bull's-eye lantern in the Scales case — consistent with the circus fire-eater's
technique, which the police of the day themselves investigated. Second, some
retellings reverse the order of the Alsop and Scales attacks or shift their
dates by a day; the contemporary sequence is Alsop first, Scales nine days
later.

### 3. The attack on Lucy Scales (28 February 1838) — Tier 1, with a priming caveat

On 28 February 1838, eighteen-year-old Lucy Scales and her sister were walking
home through Green Dragon Alley, Limehouse, after visiting their brother, a
butcher, when a tall, thin, "gentlemanly" figure in a large cloak, carrying a
small lamp, spurted "a quantity of blue flame" into Lucy's face. She dropped,
blinded, and was seized with violent fits lasting hours; her brother's statement
describes finding her on the ground. The attacker neither spoke nor touched
them; he walked quickly away. Police questioned several persons and released
them all.

The deposition survives in the *Annual Register* (1839), pages 26–27, and the
case was reported in the *Morning Post* of 7 March 1838 (the citation Mike Dash
uses in *Fortean Studies* 3, pages 62–63). That is Tier 1 sourcing. The caveat is
one the scholarship itself raises: the Scales attack came nine days after the
Alsop case had been trumpeted across the press, and Westwood and Simpson (*The
Lore of the Land*, 2005) raise the possibility that Lucy had read Jane's account
before giving her own. Dash treats the Scales statement as effectively the last
of the core contemporary reports. The definitive work should present Scales as
fully documented *and* possibly press-primed; those two facts coexist.

### 4. The Turner Street servant boy (25 February 1838) — Tier 1 core, Tier 3 ornament

Dash's archive work documents a genuine contemporary attack on a servant boy at
2 Turner Street on 25 February 1838 — a real case with a real press trail, and a
useful reminder that the 1838 scare included victims the folklore never
remembers. What it did *not* include, in any contemporary source, was the detail
Peter Haining added in 1977: that the boy saw the initial "W" worked in gold
filigree on his attacker's cloak — a detail whose only function was to point at
the Marquess of **W**aterford. The attack is Tier 1; the "W" is a proved
fabrication, and the cluster is the corpus's cleanest single specimen of
embellishment drift caught in the act.

### 5. The Brighton gardener (13 April 1838) — Tier 1, and probably not Jack at all

*The Times* of 14 April 1838, relaying the *Brighton Gazette*, reported that a
gardener at Rosehill, Sussex, had been terrified by a creature "in the shape of
a bear or some other four-footed animal," which growled, climbed the garden
wall, ran along it on all fours, chased him, and left over the wall. *The Times*
itself filed it under the Jack scare ("Spring-heeled Jack has, it seems, found
his way to the Sussex coast") while the report "bore little resemblance to other
accounts." This is Tier 1 evidence of something important and slightly different:
by April 1838 the name had become a filing category into which newspapers swept
any alarming animal-shaped prowler. The bear-suit figure also loops back to the
Peckham letter's wager (ghost, bear, devil) and to the Lord Mayor's Forest Hill
bear-skin girl. A costumed prankster in a bearskin is the most economical reading
of this cluster, and the contemporary sources practically hand it to us.

### 6. Mary Stevens and the coachman (October 1837) — Tier 2 at best

The two stories that open almost every popular telling — Mary Stevens, a
servant girl seized on Clapham Common in October 1837 by a figure whose claws
were "cold and clammy as those of a corpse," and the leaping figure who the next
day jumped in front of a carriage, crashed it, and escaped over a nine-foot wall
laughing — survive only in much later accounts. No 1837–38 newspaper, court
record, or official paper naming Mary Stevens has been produced, and Dash's
trawl through the contemporary press (eighty newspapers before digitisation; a
far larger archive since) did not surface one. They may preserve some real
autumn-1837 fright that the January hearing later absorbed; they may be
back-projection. Either way they cannot stand in the definitive work as
contemporary fact, and the thousands of retellings that present them as the
documented "first attacks" are building on air. Tier 2, honestly labelled —
later-attested, uncorroborated.

### 7. The 1838 pamphlets — a documentary loss to record

Three pamphlet publications purporting to tell the true story appeared in
January–February 1838, while the scare was live. The only known copies were
reported destroyed when the British Library was bombed in the Blitz; the
library's catalogue still lists the first. This matters to the source base
because it means the very first attempt to fix the legend in print — quite
possibly the place where some "facts" entered the tradition — is unexaminable.
The definitive work should record the hole rather than paper over it.

## Part Two — The precursors: the ground the legend grew from

Dash's post-digitisation research pushed the template back two centuries, and
the definitive work needs this stratum because it changes what 1838 has to
explain: not the invention of a leaping phantom, but the revival of one.

- **1677, Suffolk.** A pamphlet describes a leaping devil figure. Traced by Dash
  in the digitised archive; pamphlet literature is contemporary print, though a
  pamphlet proves publication, not occurrence. Tier 1 as a text, Tier 2 as an
  event.
- **1803–04, the Hammersmith Ghost — Tier 1, fully documented.** Through the
  winter of 1803–04, Hammersmith, then on London's western edge, was gripped by
  reports of a tall white ghost, sometimes described with horns and glass eyes,
  which grabbed at pedestrians. Armed vigilante patrols formed. On 3 January
  1804 Francis Smith, an exciseman on patrol, shot and killed Thomas Millwood, a
  bricklayer whose white work clothes he mistook for the ghost. Smith was tried
  for murder at the Old Bailey, convicted, sentenced to death, and reprieved to
  a year's hard labour. The trial record is as solid as documentation gets, and
  Jacob Middleton's "An Aristocratic Spectre" (*History Today*, February 2011)
  treats this scare and its cousin, the Southampton ghost — reported as leaping
  over houses and standing over ten feet tall — as the legend's direct
  foundation. The Hammersmith ghost's "bullock's hide" costume, note, echoes the
  disguise of the *documented* 1847 copycat at Teignmouth (below).
- **1809, the Croydon Monster; 1824–25, a Hampshire and Isle of Wight ghost**
  with claw gloves and armour. Both traced by Dash in the press archive; thin but
  contemporary-rooted. Tier 2.
- **The name itself.** Dash establishes that the figure's first recorded name in
  the scare was "Steel Jack" — plausibly a reference to the armoured look — and
  that there is no contemporary evidence for Haining's derivation of
  "spring-heeled" from the archaic "springald." One widely repeated origin claim
  fails even harder: Sharon McGovern's assertion of an 1808 letter in the
  *Sheffield Times* naming the ghost cannot be checked to any day or source, and
  the *Sheffield Times* did not begin publication until 1846. An 1808 reference
  in a newspaper founded in 1846 is not a mystery; it is an error, and the
  definitive work should use it as the type specimen of the unverifiable
  early-origin claim.

## Part Three — The later Victorian and Edwardian waves

### 1843–44: the Northamptonshire and Hampshire "wave" — Tier 3

Generations of books and websites report that in 1843–44 Jack appeared in
Northamptonshire as "the very image of the Devil himself, with horns and eyes of
flame" and ran riot in Hampshire. Dash's verdict, delivered to at least one
local historian who came asking, is blunt: there is no evidence for the
Northamptonshire appearance at all, and the wording in circulation descends from
Haining's 1977 book, whose phrasing ("the following year [1844] reports began to
be received from a still wider area…") offers no source. The claim nonetheless
sits in the current Wikipedia article, which is how it keeps re-entering new
books. Tier 3: folklore wearing a date, and a live demonstration of the drift
mechanism — an unsourced 1977 sentence becomes, by copying, a "report from
1843."

### 1847: Captain Finch at Teignmouth — Tier 1, and solved

In July 1847 a "Spring-heeled Jack investigation" at Teignmouth, Devon, ended
with a Captain Finch convicted on two charges of assaulting women, the evidence
describing a disguise of bullock's hide, a skullcap, horns and a mask. The case
was reported in the British press and reprinted as far away as *The South
Australian* (27 July 1847). Tier 1, and precious to the definitive work for a
structural reason: it is the one early case where the machinery is fully visible
— a named man, a costume, a court, a conviction. Whatever else Jack was, by 1847
the *idea* of Jack was already something a real man could put on.

### 1855: the Devil's Footprints, Devon — Tier 1 phenomenon, Tier 3 attribution

On the night of 8–9 February 1855, a trail of hoof-like prints in fresh snow ran
for scores of miles across south Devon, over walls, roofs and haystacks, in an
unbroken single-file line. The phenomenon is contemporary-documented: letters
and reports appeared in *The Times* and the *Illustrated London News* within
days, and Dash has written the standard modern study of it. But nobody in 1855
called the print-maker Spring-heeled Jack; that linkage was applied by later
writers. As a Jack report it is Tier 3 — an attribution, not a sighting. In the
corpus table it is counted as its own thing: a real, documented, still-debated
event that the legend annexed.

### 1872–73: Peckham again, and the Sheffield Park Ghost — Tier 1 press

The *News of the World* of 17 November 1872 reported Peckham "in a state of
commotion" over the "Peckham Ghost," editorialising that it was none other than
"Spring-heeled Jack, who terrified a past generation" (cited in Dash,
*Fortean Studies* 3, pp. 78–79). In April–May 1873 the *Illustrated Police News*
carried numerous sightings in Sheffield of the "Park Ghost," which locals
identified with Jack. These are Tier 1 as press reports of scares — thin on
named witnesses, but printed at the time.

Sheffield then grew its own durable tradition. David Clarke's work (in
*Contemporary Legend*, 2006, drawing on letters by a Mr Tatton published in the
*Sheffield & Rotherham Independent* in 1934 and in notes of 1936) records a
Sheffield Spring-heeled Jack of "about 1880" haunting the Cholera Monument
Grounds in the Park district, springing over walls while crowds turned out to
catch it and armed vigilante groups formed, until it "got too hot for the
Ghost." Clarke's own family oral tradition, collected from his grandparents,
carried a white-clad, masked Jack with "steel springs hidden inside his boots"
in the Park district into the First World War era, fleeing police through
Burngreave Cemetery. The 1934 letter is Tier 2 — retrospective by fifty years —
but it is *sourced* retrospection, naming its informant and its newspaper, which
puts it a class above the anonymous web retellings. Sheffield matters
disproportionately: it is the best-documented case of a provincial city keeping
its own Jack, with its own geography, for half a century.

### 1877: Aldershot — Tier 1 press, Tier 2 amplification

The most famous of the later waves is also the best documented and the most
embellished, and it must be taken in two layers.

The contemporary layer: through the spring of 1877, Aldershot camp reported a
figure that waylaid sentries at night — materialising beside them, slapping
their faces with a cold hand, and bounding off into the dark; at least one
sentry fired. *The Times* reported "The Aldershott Ghost" on 28 April 1877, and
the *Surrey and Hants News & Guildford Times* carried camp correspondence on the
scare on 17 March 1877 (both cited in Dash, *Fortean Studies* 3, p. 95). In the
autumn the scare had a northern echo: the *Illustrated Police News* of
3 November 1877 reported a sheep-skin-clad figure at Newport Arch, Lincoln,
mobbed and shot at without effect (Dash, p. 96). Military witnesses, named
units, dated newspapers: Tier 1 as reports of a genuine garrison scare.

The amplification layer: the canonical version — a sentry's challenge ignored,
slaps in the face, a shot fired point-blank with no effect, the figure
disappearing "with astonishing bounds" — reaches its lush form in Elliott
O'Donnell's *Haunted Britain* (1963), eighty-six years later. Lord Ernest
Hamilton's memoir *Forty Years On* (1922) retells the Aldershot appearances but
misdates them to the winter of 1878–79 and to his own regiment's arrival, and
names a fellow officer, Lieutenant Alfrey, as the prankster; Dash's check of the
Judge Advocate General's court-martial charge sheets for 1877–80 at the
National Archives found no record of Alfrey ever being court-martialled.
Hamilton's claim that sentries were issued ammunition and ordered to shoot on
sight, after which "the appearances ceased," is likewise memoir-only. Tier 2,
with the drift precisely measurable: two years of date-slip, an invented-seeming
culprit, and an ending the contemporary papers do not record.

### The Black Country cycle (1870s–80s) — Tier 3

The Himley Road apparition of 1877, volunteer sentries slapped along a wooded
lane near Dudley, a farmer whose buckshot "ignited a ball of flame," a scorched
circle in the meadow, an 1882 leap at a courting couple in Dudley: this whole
cycle circulates through local-lore collections (the Mysterious Britain
gazetteer is representative) without a single named contemporary source. One
element is real history wearing a Jack costume: Joseph Darby of Netherton, a
genuine canal-jumping athlete who won the world spring-jumping championship in
1887, is folded into the tale as the boy caught practising by lamplight — the
folklore absorbing a real local jumper. Tier 3 as Jack reports; genuinely
valuable as evidence of how the legend metabolised local fact.

### 1886: Birmingham — Tier 1 by citation, chain thin

A much-quoted fragment — "First a young girl, then a man, felt a hand on their
shoulder, and turned to see the infernal one with glowing face, bidding them a
good evening" — is attributed to the *Birmingham Post* of September 1886 and
circulates via modern Fortean writing (the Supernatural Fox Sisters' "Monster of
the Week" column quotes it). If the citation is sound, this is a Tier 1 press
report of a Midlands scare. In this pass the desk could verify only the
quotation's circulation, not the original item; it is carried as Tier 1-pending,
flagged for a British Newspaper Archive check before the definitive work leans
on it.

### 1888 and 1904: Liverpool — Tier 2 rooftop, Tier 1 street

Around 1888 Jack was said to have appeared on the roof of St Francis Xavier's
Church in Salisbury Street, Everton. This rests on later compilation (Jenny
Randles' *Strange and Unexplained Mysteries of the 20th Century* is the cited
source) and is Tier 2. The 1904 Liverpool reports are better: the *News of the
World* of 25 September 1904 reported appearances in William Henry Street,
Everton, where a figure was seen leaping between rooftops and down to the street
before crowds, escaping uncaught (cited in Dash, *Fortean Studies* 3, p. 97).
Tier 1 as a contemporary press report — while noting that the baroque additions
in modern retellings (a ten-minute performance, a twenty-five-foot vertical leap
onto a house, a parting laugh) appear in none of the cited sources and are
twenty-first-century embroidery. Liverpool 1904 is conventionally the last
Victorian-Edwardian report, and on the contemporary record that is fair.

## Part Four — The twentieth century in Britain: strays

Three clusters circulate from Fortean compilation rather than fresh reporting in
this pass: a Bradford scare in 1926 and a Warrington scare in 1927 (both carried
in Theo Paijmans' survey of the American material, with links to specialist
sites), and the Attercliffe, Sheffield "red-eyed prowler" of the early 1970s,
said to have grabbed women, punched men, and bounded between rooftops — a case
with some contemporary local-press basis as compiled by later researchers, but
carried here as Tier 2 until its originals are pulled. A single-witness
Herefordshire encounter in 1986 — a traveller named Marshall, slapped by a
bounding figure in a black ski-suit with an elongated chin — entered print
through Fortean literature decades after the 1838 pattern had become a template
anyone could match a memory to: Tier 2, and the definitive work should note the
contamination problem plainly. An undated tale of West Surrey schoolchildren and
a figure in a "white suit with badges" who ran "as fast as a car" is Tier 3.

The one modern British report with a clean contemporary paper trail is the
**Ewell, Surrey, sighting of 14 February 2012**: Scott Martin, his wife and child,
in a taxi on the Ewell bypass near Nescot College at about 10.30 p.m., reported
a dark, featureless figure that ran across the road and leapt a fifteen-foot bank.
*Your Local Guardian* (the Surrey Comet group) reported it on 12 March 2012, and
the *Futility Closet* podcast discussed it with Dash's knowledge. Tier 1 as a
press report of a claim — the claim itself being one family's thirty seconds on
a dark bypass, 174 years after the *Times* reports and matching them in
outline. That is, to date, the last press-reported Jack sighting anywhere.

## Part Five — The American layer, and the analogues

### How America got its Jack: the Vyner problem

The idea of an American Spring-heeled Jack is younger than the space age. Its
first statement is a 1961 article by one J. Vyner, published in *Flying Saucer
Review* and in *FATE* magazine, unsourced and unreferenced. Of Vyner's three
American cases, none survives inspection: one is the Mad Gasser of Mattoon,
Illinois (1944) — a phantom-attacker scare of an entirely different signature —
and the others are "flying man" reports involving no leaping, no fire, no
attacks. Theo Paijmans' post-mortem on this literature (published via
Cryptomundo/Mysterious Universe, 2013, summarising his longer researches) is
blunt: later writers simply repeated Vyner's error, "and for decades there was
no progression in charting any presence of Spring-heeled Jack in America." Any
count of American Jack reports that silently includes the Vyner material — and
podcast-era tallies, including the sixteen US reports in the mapping that
prompted this project, are built on lists that descend from it — is counting a
category error. The definitive work must rebuild the American layer from
Paijmans forward, not from Vyner.

### The defensible American clusters

**Louisville, Kentucky, July 1880.** The strongest American cluster. Fortean
literature consistently describes a July 1880 Louisville scare: a tall figure in
cape, helmet and a shiny uniform, with pointed ears and long fingers, accosting
women, tearing at clothing, and emitting flames from a blue light on his chest.
The specificity and consistency of the description across independent compilers
indicates genuine July 1880 newspaper reporting underneath (Louisville's papers
covered the scare and it was reprinted nationally), but in this pass the desk
could not pull the original Louisville item itself, so the cluster is carried as
**Tier 1 by strong citation chain, pending a Chronicling America pull** — the
single most valuable verification errand outstanding in the American layer.

**Provincetown, Massachusetts, 1938–45: the "Black Flash."** Dozens of
appearances of a tall, black-clad, flame-belching, fence-clearing figure were
reported in Provincetown through the war years — including the well-worn
anecdote of the dog-owner who fired a shotgun at point-blank range and watched
it laugh and clear his eight-foot fence. Paijmans researched this case at length
(*Anomalist* 13, 2007) from local sources; it had contemporary local-press
presence and named witnesses. Tier 1 as local press reporting, Tier 2 in its
compiled form — and a genuine American parallel to 1838, arising without any
plausible single importer of the legend.

**Baltimore, 1951: the Phantom of O'Donnell Heights.** A two-week scare in a
southeast Baltimore housing project, covered by the *Baltimore Sun* at the time
and catalogued by Paijmans as falling within the Jack typology (a leaping,
elusive night prowler). Tier 1 press, analogue-adjacent: it was never called
Spring-heeled Jack by its witnesses.

**Houston, Texas, 18 June 1953.** Three witnesses watched a tall figure in a
tree on the banks of Buffalo Bayou — in some tellings winged, in a black cloak —
which vanished as they approached. Reported in the Houston press at the time;
John Matthews' 2016 book folds it into a UFO reading. Tier 1 as a press report
of an odd sighting; its membership in the Jack corpus is a later filing
decision, not a contemporary one.

**Paijmans' nine cases, 1885–1927.** Working the digitised American newspaper
archives, Paijmans assembled nine further cases between 1885 and 1927 that match
one or more elements of the Jack typology (leaping figures, fire, night
attacks), published in longer form in 2007 and summarised in his 2013 survey,
together with his separate studies of the Black Flash and the "Newhallville
Terror." As a set this is Tier 2 with Tier 1 roots — each case is anchored to a
named period newspaper in his full list, but the set as published has not been
independently re-verified, and Paijmans himself warns that nineteenth-century
American papers printed yarns and hoaxes as news without embarrassment. The
definitive work should adopt his standard: the interest lies in the *typology*
— leaping, fire, elusiveness attaching to local scares — more than in any claim
that London's Jack crossed the Atlantic.

**Sydney, North Carolina (1973) and Plano, Texas (1979).** A gaunt figure
taking fifty-foot leaps; a ten-foot entity seen by multiple witnesses. Both
circulate through Fortean compilations and podcast timelines without, in this
pass, a retrievable contemporary source. Tier 2 shading to Tier 3.

On the podcast's "sixteen US reports": the honest reconciliation is that a
sixteen-count can be assembled from the clusters above only by counting
Vyner's disqualified cases, splitting clusters into individual incidents, and
including typological analogues. Rebuilt to this file's standard, the American
layer holds **four Tier 1 press clusters, one nine-case Tier 2 set, and two
Tier 2/3 strays** — a smaller and far more interesting corpus, because what
survives is the *pattern recurring independently*, not a travelling entity.

### The analogues — filed separately, on purpose

**Pérák, the Spring Man of Prague (1939–45).** A leaping figure reported across
occupied Prague, said to spring over walls, trains and river barges, later
absorbed into resistance folklore and a 1946 Jiří Trnka cartoon in which he
fights the SS. Dash himself has drawn the parallel. Tier 1 as documented
folklore with contemporary wartime roots; an analogue, not a Jack report — and
the single best proof that the "spring man" is a recurring urban form, not a
person.

**The Monkey Man of Delhi (May 2001).** The best-documented phantom-attacker
scare of the modern age, and the definitive work's control case. Over two weeks
in May 2001, Delhi police logged more than 350 reports and some 400 emergency
calls describing a four-foot, black-furred attacker with — the detail that
should stop any Jack researcher mid-sentence — a **metal helmet, metal claws,
glowing red eyes and buttons on its chest**, leaping from rooftop to rooftop.
Around sixty people were injured, mostly in panics and falls; two (by some
reports three) died falling from roofs and stairwells. Police issued artists'
sketches; the Delhi Police final report of 19 June 2001 concluded that fear and
panic explained the sightings, ruled out an organised prankster conspiracy, and
found no evidence of any creature; forensic review found most injuries
superficial and consistent with panic accidents. A clinical study of victims in
East Delhi followed (Verma & Srivastava, *Indian Journal of Medical Sciences*,
2003). Tier 1 throughout — a fully documented scare with official findings. Its
function in the corpus is calibration: it shows exactly what a Jack-type event
looks like when it happens in the age of telephones, hospitals and police
reports, including the helmet-and-claws convergence with Jane Alsop's 1838
description — a convergence that cuts both ways, and the definitive work must
let it.

**The Muhnochwa of Uttar Pradesh (2002)** — a light-emitting "face-scratcher"
scare the following summer — and the **Mad Gasser of Mattoon (1944)** belong in
the same analogue file: the first as Delhi's echo, the second as the case Vyner
wrongly annexed, now restored to its own literature (where Bartholomew's
scholarship treats it as a textbook phantom-attacker panic).

## Part Six — The modern scholarship

**Peter Haining, *The Legend and Bizarre Crimes of Spring Heeled Jack* (1977) —
the problem the field is built around.** Haining's was for two decades the only
book on the subject, and Dash's audit of it is the most consequential act in
Jack studies. Three of its load-bearing episodes have no prior record anywhere:
the attack on "Polly Adams" at Blackheath on 11 October 1837, in which the
victim supposedly identified her attacker as the Marquess of Waterford; the gold
"W" on the Turner Street attacker's cloak (Part One, §4); and the murder of
thirteen-year-old Maria Davis, supposedly drowned by Jack in Folly Ditch,
Jacob's Island, on 12 November 1845. For Maria Davis there is no death record in
London for those years, and the illustration Haining printed in support actually
comes from the 1870s part-work *Old and New London* and depicts a woman scooping
water from a ditch with a saucepan. Haining's documentary "smoking gun" for the
Waterford theory — an extract from the *Memoirs and Correspondence of Sir
Frederick Johnston* describing the prank being plotted — comes from a book that
appears in no library catalogue in the world, by a man (Sir Frederick Johnson)
who died in a riding accident in 1842, decades too early for retrospective
memoirs. When Dash corresponded with Haining in 1996 and asked for his sources,
Haining could furnish none, explaining that he had lent his research material to
a scriptwriter and it had been lost. Dash's word for the product is "fakelore."
Every modern retelling that includes Polly Adams, the golden W, or the Maria
Davis murder is downstream of this one book — including, as the desk verified
during this pass, websites still presenting the Davis murder in 2026 as "the
most serious attack attributed to Spring-Heeled Jack." Haining is citable in the
definitive work only as the legend's great contaminator, never as a source.

**Mike Dash, "Spring-heeled Jack: To Victorian Bugaboo from Suburban Ghost,"
*Fortean Studies* 3 (1996; revised 2005) — the benchmark.** Dash did the thing
nobody else had: he read the contemporary press. His 1996 study rested on some
45,000 words of transcribed reports from eighty newspapers; after digitisation
his source file grew to roughly 240,000 words (his own account, given in his
*MonsterTalk* interview, episode 63). What he established: which of the famous
cases possess contemporary documentation (the Mansion House affair, Alsop,
Scales, Turner Street, the Brighton gardener, the 1870s waves, Liverpool 1904)
and which do not (Stevens, Adams, Davis, Northamptonshire); the Haining
fabrications, itemised; the precursor chain back through the Hammersmith Ghost
to a 1677 Suffolk pamphlet; the figure's original name, "Steel Jack"; and a
statistical correlation between Jack stories in the press and periods when
Parliament was not sitting — the "silly season" signature of a newspaper-driven
phenomenon. His mature conclusion deserves quoting in paraphrase because the
definitive work will have to reckon with it on every page: the Spring-heeled
Jack of 1838 was very probably a *recycled* newspaper ghost-story — a floating
template with decades of prior sightings behind it — which acquired a body when
a real costumed assault, the attack on Jane Alsop, accidentally gave it flesh.
Dash is not a debunker of the cheap kind; he is the reason the corpus in this
file can be tiered at all.

**Karl Bell, *The Legend of Spring-heeled Jack: Victorian Urban Folklore and
Popular Cultures* (Boydell Press, 2012; paperback 2017) — the academic
standard.** Bell, a cultural historian at the University of Portsmouth, won the
Folklore Society's Katharine Briggs Award (2013) for a study built from
newspapers, street ballads, penny dreadfuls, court accounts, journals and
reminiscences. His Jack is a cultural phenomenon with a traceable life-cycle:
oral rumour in the London suburbs; metropolitan press sensation; stage and
penny-dreadful property (villain first, then, remarkably, hero — the costumed
avenger of the 1880s–1904 serials is a genuine proto-superhero); finally nursery
bogeyman. Where Dash audits incidents, Bell explains the *transmission* — and
he independently fails to find evidence for Haining claims Dash had already
flagged, notably the 1840 J. T. Haines play *Spring Heeled Jack, The Terror of
London*, which Haining called an enormous success and which Bell can find no
trace of existing; plays featuring Jack, he shows, were rare before the 1860s.
Bell's follow-up article, "Gothicizing Victorian Folklore: Spring-heeled Jack
and the Enacted Gothic" (*Gothic Studies* 22.1, 2020, pp. 14–30), coins the
"enacted Gothic" — folkloric and Gothic material erupting out of fiction into
everyday life — and is the most recent peer-reviewed word on the subject. For
the definitive work, Bell is the authority on what the legend *did*; Dash on
what it *was made of*.

**The supporting shelf.** Jacqueline Simpson's *Spring-Heeled Jack* (2001) is
the source through which the Peckham letter is usually quoted, and Simpson with
Jennifer Westwood (*The Lore of the Land*, 2005) supplies the press-priming
caveat on Lucy Scales. David Clarke's article in *Contemporary Legend* (2006)
contributes the Sheffield oral tradition, the Tatton letters, and the soberest
short verdict on the Waterford theory in print: there is circumstantial evidence
placing the Marquess in London in the winter of 1837–38, but "no convincing
evidence" he was responsible for the assaults; his "Bogeyman or Spaceman?"
(*Paranormal Magazine* 45, March 2010) surveys the alien reading fairly and
declines it. Jacob Middleton's "An Aristocratic Spectre" (*History Today*,
2011) grounds the precursor ghosts. Rupert Mann's entry in the *Oxford
Dictionary of National Biography* (2004) and David Cordingly's *Scotsman*
summary of it (2006) give the legend a reference-work floor. Robert E.
Bartholomew and Paul Weatherhead's *Social Panics & Phantom Attackers*
(Palgrave Macmillan, 2024) is the newest scholarly frame: Jack treated not as a
singular mystery but as a dataset member alongside the Mad Gasser, the Monkey
Man and modern spiking panics — reviewed in *Fortean Times* 457. John Matthews'
*The Mystery of Spring-Heeled Jack* (Destiny Books, 2016) is a competent trade
survey that ends by entertaining the stranded-alien hypothesis; cite it for the
state of the popular Fortean reading, not as scholarship.

**George Dudding, *Spring-Heeled Jack* (CreateSpace, 2017) — assessed as a
source.** This is the book underlying the podcast mapping that set this project
in motion, and it must be assessed plainly, as the podcast's own host flagged
its sourcing as uneven. It is a self-published compilation (CreateSpace
Independent Publishing Platform, ISBN 9781546475521) by an American writer of
Fortean titles. Its narrative arc — beast, ghost and demon; theatre and literary
character; nursery bogeyman; modern cryptid/alien readings — follows the shape
Bell established in 2012, and its descriptive copy promises coverage rather than
evidence: in the material retrievable for this pass there is no sign of a
source-critical apparatus, no engagement with Dash's audit, and no scholarly
review has taken the book up. Verdict for the definitive work: Dudding is a
*finding aid and a map of the received legend* — genuinely useful for
enumeration (his trawl is one source of the ~130-report tally) — but no claim
should rest on Dudding alone. Every case taken from him must be re-grounded in
Dash, Bell, or the primary press before it enters the text. Used that way, the
book earns its place; used as an authority, it would re-import exactly the
contamination this source base exists to filter.

**Post-2017: anything genuinely new?** Searched through October 2026, the
answer is: new framing, no new evidence. Bell's 2020 *Gothic Studies* article
and Bartholomew & Weatherhead's 2024 volume are real additions to the
*interpretation*. Dash's archive continues to grow (the 240,000-word file, the
precursor chain, the Parliament correlation), but his full synthesis remains
unpublished in book form as of this pass. The podcast layer has refreshed the
legend for a new audience — *The InBetween* returned to Jack in its episode 237
(12 October 2026) — without adding cases that survive checking. No new primary
document has surfaced, and no new sighting has been press-reported since Ewell
in February 2012. The evidentiary picture a definitive work must paint in 2026
is the picture Dash and Bell left: stable, small, and well-lit.

**The count, honestly stated.** Of the forty-four clusters tabled: twenty-one
stand at Tier 1 (contemporary-documented or contemporary press-reported, two of
them — Birmingham 1886 and Louisville 1880 — flagged pending a final archive
pull); thirteen stand at Tier 2 (later-attested, including sourced
retrospection); nine stand at Tier 3, of which two are proved fabrications and
one a proved misattribution; and one, Pérák, stands outside the Jack tiers as a
Tier 1-documented analogue. Mixed rows are counted by the tier of the claim
that matters — the Devon footprints, for instance, are Tier 1 as an event and
Tier 3 as a Jack report, and are tallied as the latter. The analogues (Prague,
Delhi, Uttar Pradesh, Baltimore) are inside those numbers for completeness but
are not Jack reports and must never be silently added to a Jack total. Strip the
precursors and analogues away and the British Jack corpus proper is twenty-eight
clusters, of which fourteen are Tier 1. That — not 130, and not sixteen
American hauntings — is the ground the definitive work actually has to build on.

## Part Eight — State of the evidence (one page)

**What a definitive work can prove.** That in the winter of 1837–38 suburban
London experienced a real scare with a real documentary skeleton: an anonymous
letter read aloud by the Lord Mayor, a corroborating pile of correspondence,
police instructed and rewards offered — all in *The Times* of January 1838. That
at least one genuine, violent, costumed assault stands at the centre of it: Jane
Alsop was attacked at her own door by a man in a helmet and white oilskin who
claimed to have caught Spring-heeled Jack, her account was sworn and printed,
and the case produced an arrest, a trial, and an acquittal on the single point
of the fire-breathing. That the fire, the claws and the lamp recur in a second
sworn statement nine days later. That the template was old — London had buried a
man over the same kind of ghost in 1804 — and that the name, the wager theory,
and the press machinery are all visible in contemporary ink. That in 1847 a real
man was convicted of doing Jack in a bullock's-hide costume, and that soldiers
at Aldershot in 1877 and crowds in Liverpool in 1904 reported scares in their
own day's newspapers. That the legend then had a documented second life in
print, on stage, and in the nursery, which Bell has mapped definitively.

**What it must present as contested.** Whether Lucy Scales described an attacker
or a newspaper story she had just read. Whether Mary Stevens was attacked at
all, in the form the story has come down. What happened at Aldershot beyond a
genuine garrison fright — the point-blank shot, the named officer-prankster, and
the shoot-on-sight ending are memoir, not record. Whether the Marquess of
Waterford did any of it: he was named in 1880, he was in London, he was exactly
the kind of man the Peckham letter describes — and the only "evidence" ever
produced tying him to specific attacks was fabricated in 1977, while a
contemporary Waterford newspaper places him at a ball in Ireland across the very
nights of the Alsop and Scales attacks. Whether anything in America before
Paijmans' newspaper trawl deserves the name. And whether the recurring figure —
Delhi's helmeted, metal-clawed rooftop leaper of 2001 above all — is one
phenomenon wearing local costumes, or several phenomena that a good template
makes look alike. On every one of these the record supports a fair fight, and
the definitive work's job is to stage it, not to fix it.

**What is folklore wearing a date.** The murder of Maria Davis, which never
happened, by a man who was never shown to exist, sourced to a book that does not
exist — and still, in 2026, the most repeated "fact" in the popular literature.
The attack on Polly Adams. The golden W. The 1843 Northamptonshire devil. The
Duke of Wellington's nightly rides. The twenty-five-foot leap onto a Liverpool
rooftop. The 1808 Sheffield letter in a newspaper founded in 1846. Each wears a
precise date, a named place, a confident tone; each dissolves the moment its
paper is asked for. The definitive work will be judged by how it handles these:
not by debunking them at length, but by refusing to let a single one of them
carry weight — and by showing, in their place, the smaller, stranger, fully
documented thing that actually happened: a city scaring itself, in print, in
real time, around one real assault it could neither solve nor stop talking
about. That is the story the evidence will bear, and it is enough.
