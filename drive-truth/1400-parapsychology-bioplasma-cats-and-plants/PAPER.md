## Premise

The article sets out to report, from the margins of an October 1976 congress on frontier areas of natural science at Alma-Ata in the Kazakh Soviet Republic, on the state of parapsychology in the Soviet Union: whether the field officially existed there, how its researchers reconciled it with Marxist materialism, what theoretical framework — the "bioplasma" concept — Soviet researchers had developed for the phenomena, and what experiments, including a claimed communication experiment between a cat and a plant, the author witnessed or was told about.

## Abstract

Scott Hill's article, published in the magazine *Esotera* in April 1977 and read here in the English translation preserved in a Central Intelligence Agency release, reports on Soviet parapsychology as the author encountered it around a congress held at Alma-Ata in October 1976, which only three Western guests were permitted to attend and at which parapsychology itself was not on the programme. Hill observes that the field had no institute and no place in official Soviet psychological literature, yet had acquired a formal definition in the 1974 edition of the Soviet Encyclopedia, a marked change from earlier editions that had dismissed it as an outgrowth of decadent Western culture. He recounts a story circulating in the Soviet Union attributing the field's revival to a journey to India by Premier Khrushchev, reviews the earlier telepathic-hypnosis experiments of the Leningrad physiologist Leonid L. Wassiliev, and then examines the bioplasma theory developed at Alma-Ata: the proposal that an electron-hole plasma in cell membranes and mitochondria constitutes a biological field capable of mediating the reported phenomena. The article closes with Hill's eyewitness account of an experiment in which a plant's light transmission changed while a cat beside it was electrically stimulated, an experiment its conductors admitted was unpublished and in need of repetition.

## Content Account

### A field that officially was not there

Hill begins from a paradox he reports directly: parapsychology was officially outside the realm of traditional Soviet psychology, unmentioned in its literature, and had no institute, yet the 1974 Soviet Encyclopedia defined its domain as forms of sensory perception occurring through a mode of information transmission not explainable by known sensory organs, together with corresponding forms of action on physical phenomena outside the organism without muscular force. Hill notes that the concept of the psyche does not occur anywhere in the definition, and he frames the researchers' central problem as an ideological one: whether the existence of a psyche or soul contradicts a materialistic philosophy. The revival story he relays — that Khrushchev, having observed yogis under scientific observation in India, returned to Moscow and ordered an investigation with an initial budget of one million rubles — is presented in the article as a story circulated in the Soviet Union, and it is recorded here on exactly that basis.

### Wassiliev and the older research

Hill reviews the work of Leonid L. Wassiliev at the Leningrad Brain Research Institute between 1925 and 1965, known for experiments in "telepathic hypnosis" or mental suggestion from a distance. He notes, as a point Sovietologists considered significant, that experiments performed in the 1920s and 1930s were first published in the 1960s, by a political publisher, in a volume entitled *Mysterious Phenomena of the Human Psyche*, a major part of which defended the compatibility of psychic research with scientific Marxism. This is Hill's account of the publication history, attributed to the article.

### The bioplasma theory

The article's longest section examines the bioplasma concept, which Hill attributes to a theory developed in 1967 by the engineer V. S. Grieschenko (spelled "Grieschenko" in the translation's recognition) and pursued at the Institute of Biophysics of the Kazakh State University under Professor V. M. Inyushin. Hill first reviews the physics of plasma as a fourth state of matter, and the semiconductor analogy of an "electron-hole plasma" at room temperature, before presenting the Soviet proposal: that electron plasma in mitochondria and electron-hole-exciton plasma stored in cell membranes form an organized, low-entropy system giving rise to an anisotropic "biofield." He cites supporting claims attributed to named researchers, including St. Georgi's remarks on delocalized electrons, Pirozyan and Aristarhov's demonstrations of semiconductor properties in membranes, and Tarusov's work on the weak bioluminescence of tissues, together with an experiment in which ultraviolet light emission from an electrically stimulated frog heart rose and fell with the strength of its beat. Hill presents this material as the theory and its claimed supports as reported to him; the article itself remarks that the notion of a body-temperature plasma system with an absolute temperature near zero is hard to accept.

### The cat and the plant

The closing report describes an experiment Hill says he witnessed after the congress, conducted by Inyushin and his associates as a variation on the plant-response experiments of the American polygraph specialist Cleve Backster, first reported in 1968, but using optical detection: a plant inside a double-walled Faraday cage interrupted a helium-neon laser beam, while a cat in a metal box outside the cage received 50-volt impulses to electrodes on its ears. When the cat meowed under stimulation, the intensity of polarized laser light transmitted through the plant leaf rose. Hill states plainly that Inyushin and his associates had not yet published the study and admitted that many more repetitions were necessary before an official report could be given. The experiment is an eyewitness magazine account of an unpublished procedure, and this assessment records it as nothing more.

## Errors and Anomalies

The text on disk is optical character recognition of a scanned translation, and it carries recognition noise throughout: broken hyphenation, misread words such as "Ooes" for "Does" and "stte" for "state," a garbled picture caption crediting "Dr F. Popp" with the view that DNA functions as a biolaser where the body text attributes related views to Popp about DNA as a "bio-laser," and variant spellings of names including Wassiliev, Grieschenko, and Inyushin. Several figures in the text are physically implausible as recognized — a count of "1049 mitochondria in the human body," electron densities rendered as "Jol3 electrons/sg cm" — and read as recognition corruption of superscript notation rather than as the article's own numbers; no claim in this assessment rests on them.

The article predates any independent evaluation of the experiments it describes, and its own text acknowledges that the cat-plant experiment was unpublished and unrepeated. Its historical anecdotes, including the Khrushchev story, are presented in the article as circulating accounts, not as documented fact.
