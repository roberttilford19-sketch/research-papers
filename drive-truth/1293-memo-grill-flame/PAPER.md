## Premise

The memorandum answers a legal question with practical consequences: whether the Army's part in the joint Army and Defense Intelligence Agency Grill Flame programme counted as experimentation on human subjects under Department of Defense rules, and, if that question could not be put to rest, how the Army should organise its participation in future years so that the question was answered in advance rather than after money had been committed.

## Abstract

This two-page memorandum, dated 17 July 1981 and signed by Delbert L. Spurlock Jr., General Counsel of the Army, is addressed to the Assistant Chief of Staff for Intelligence. It reports a meeting with Brigadier General Rapmund, chairperson of the Human Use Review Committee, held to resolve whether the joint Grill Flame programme fell within Procedure 18, "Experimentation on Human Subjects," of Department of Defense directive 5240.1-R. On the immediate question, the memorandum decides that Army money already committed to the fiscal year 1981 portion of the joint programme may be released to SRI, the contractor. The reasoning is narrow. It rests on the position that it is at least arguable that none of the fiscal year 1981 taskings, Army or Defense Intelligence Agency, involved human experimentation, on Secretary Marsh's approval of the Army's involvement, and on Rapmund's representations that the Army's 1981 taskings involved only the theoretical refinement of remote viewing techniques and that Army personnel would not be involved for the remainder of the fiscal year. For future years the memorandum recommends consolidation, annual Secretariat-level review, and designing joint programmes on the assumption that they include human experimentation.

## Content account

The memorandum is candid about the gap between its decision and its author's preference. Spurlock writes that from both a legal and a policy perspective he would feel much more comfortable calling all Grill Flame activity human experimentation. The decision to release the 1981 funds is expressly built on the representations described in the abstract rather than on a settled legal conclusion, and on the judgement that the Army could take part in the 1981 portion without insisting on Secretariat review and approval of the Defense Intelligence Agency's involvement.

The forward-looking guidance occupies the second half of the document and is the part with lasting administrative content. Because any Army involvement in Grill Flame had to be reviewed and approved by the Secretary or Under Secretary of the Army annually, Spurlock strongly recommends that from fiscal year 1982 all Army Grill Flame activity be consolidated under the supervision of the Assistant Chief of Staff for Intelligence and coordinated with Rapmund on human use issues. At the beginning of each year, an inventory of all planned Army involvement should be submitted for Secretariat-level review and approval, covering in-house operational work, in-house research, and outside contracting. He states his underlying view directly: that the operational aspects of parapsychology, by their very nature, cannot be divorced from medical and scientific research, that this is exactly what had happened in both the in-house programme and the SRI contract, and that it must be corrected.

On contracting, the memorandum does not discourage the use of outside organisations such as SRI. It anticipates instead that the Army's likely continuing interest in using military personnel, and in the physiological aspects of parapsychology, means that future joint programmes will be viewed as including both research and human experimentation. As a general rule, therefore, such programmes should be designed so that all participants comply with all aspects of Procedure 18 before the Army enters into any contract. The simplest way to avoid the problem, he recommends, would be to contract independently rather than as a participant in a joint programme. Copies were sent to the Secretary of the Army, to a named official in the Office of the Secretary of Defense General Counsel, and to Rapmund.

Read as a whole, the document is evidence of a specific institutional position in July 1981. A legal office permitted one fiscal year's funding to proceed on stated factual representations, while recording its own discomfort and setting conditions intended to prevent the same unresolved question from recurring.
