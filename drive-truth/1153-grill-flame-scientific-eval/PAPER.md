## Premise

The document is the December 1979 report of a scientific evaluation committee appointed after the Under Secretary of the Army suggested, in June 1979, that the Army's parapsychological activities be independently reviewed. At the recommendation of Dr. Ruth Davis, the committee's remit was widened to the whole Department of Defense posture, and it reported to the Grill Flame Oversight Committee. Its stated mission had three parts: to review parapsychological research, investigations, and applications within the Department of Defense and the intelligence community; to assess the validity of claims made for the alleged existence of psi phenomena, with particular emphasis on experiments intended as proof of principle; and to recommend a course of action for future Department of Defense activity in the field. The committee states that most of its work concerned remote viewing, with psychokinesis reviewed as a secondary topic.

## Abstract

In December 1979, a Scientific Evaluation Committee chaired by Manfred Gale reported on United States government involvement in parapsychology under the code word Grill Flame. The committee visited the installations involved, interviewed government and non-government investigators, and reviewed classified reports, intelligence summaries, and the open literature. Its central finding was restrained. Remote viewing research and applications had not, in its judgment, proved the existence of the phenomenon or established any dependable parameters, and many anecdotal successes did not survive careful scrutiny. At the same time, the committee recorded that it had been persuaded there was some probability that effects attributed to remote viewing occur under unexplained circumstances with particular individuals, while stressing that existing experimental techniques had not adequately documented such effects. It found no adequate theory, no evidence of fraudulent intent, inadequate documentation and controls throughout the programmes, and a lack of management direction. Its recommendations divided accordingly. Work on characterising the phenomena should wait until existence was established. The remote viewing work at AMSAA and the psychokinesis experiments at MICOM, together with their supporting contracts at SRI, should be discontinued. A majority favoured continuing carefully monitored operational testing for intelligence value, against a signed minority view that such tests would be unreliable and potentially dangerous.

## Content Account

### How the committee worked

The executive summary, comprising Chapters 1 to 3 of the report, explains that committee members were drawn from psychiatry, biostatistics, psychology, physics, engineering, and operations research, and were screened to avoid persons with preconceived views for or against the subject. Annex 1 names the members and their affiliations, and Annex 2 lists the places visited between July and December 1979, which included SRI International, the Missile Research and Development Command at Redstone Arsenal, the Central Intelligence Agency, Princeton University, the Defense Intelligence Agency, the Intelligence and Security Command at Fort Meade, the Army Materiel Systems Analysis Activity at Aberdeen Proving Ground, and Lawrence Livermore Laboratories. The committee also notes that total Department of Defense and intelligence community funding for this work since 1972 was, in its account, less than 1.5 million dollars, a figure the chronology chapter breaks down by agency.

### Major findings

Chapter 2 sets out the committee's findings in its own terms. It states that remote viewing research had not proved the existence of the phenomenon and had not conclusively established any parametric dependencies, and that the same applied to application-oriented activity. It identifies inadequate documentation and failure to apply adequate controls as the most frequent causes limiting the credibility of reported successes, and it singles out two principal shortfalls in the programmes reviewed: the reliance on subjective judgment of the degree of correlation between a transcript and a target, and the ambiguous roles of the experimental designer, the viewer, and the interviewer. It warns that operational programmes, by their existence, assume the reality of remote viewing and may inadvertently establish that assumption in other communities. It finds the government programme lacking in focus, objectives, and top-down management review. It also states plainly that the committee found no evidence or suggestion of fraudulent intent in any of the work examined.

On psychokinesis, the committee judged that the Army-sponsored experimentation at MICOM and the related SRI contract, directed at influencing a computer-generated random bit stream, would not by itself prove or disprove the existence of psychokinetic phenomena. It viewed the acoustic-emission type of experiment pursued by Dr. Hawke at Livermore more favourably than random-number-generator work. On theory, it found no adequate explanation of the remote viewing process among the mechanisms proposed, and no unifying concept linking remote viewing and psychokinesis.

### The minority opinion

A minority opinion signed by Orlansky, Holloway, and Tang is printed immediately after the recommendations. The minority argues that a test programme in an operation-like environment is not likely to provide useful or reliable data, because operational tests arise with little warning and cannot be systematically varied, and it warns that endorsing such tests gives scientific credibility to activities exploiting phenomena that the committee elsewhere notes have not been proved or disproved. This signed dissent is part of the committee's own record and is reported here as such.

### Recommendations

Chapter 3 recommends that work demonstrating the existence of remote viewing and psychokinesis be supported if a credible approach emerges, preferably in an agency other than the Department of Defense so that the work can be conducted openly and subjected to peer review. Characterisation research should not be sponsored until existence is established. The remote viewing work at AMSAA and the psychokinesis experiments at MICOM, with their associated SRI contractual support, should be discontinued and terminated in the most cost-effective manner. The intelligence community should continue collecting information on foreign parapsychological activity in order to avoid surprises, and private-sector research should be monitored and periodically reviewed. A central Department of Defense authority should manage and fund whatever programme continues. On operational applications, a majority of five out of eight, as the report states the division, favoured continuing operationally oriented remote viewing aimed at determining its empirical value to intelligence, subject to oversight, adequate controls, attention to false alarm rates, compliance with human-use requirements, and a phasing out of dependence on the SRI approach.

### The chronology chapter

Chapter 4 is expressly not the committee's own judgment. A note at its head states that it is a chronological overview of the recorded status before the committee's work, and that value judgments in it are not attributable to the committee. It recounts, among other episodes, the SRI work of Hal Puthoff and Russell Targ beginning in 1971, the participation of Ingo Swann and Pat Price, Central Intelligence Agency interest from April 1972, Price's coordinate descriptions of a sensitive facility in West Virginia and of a Soviet facility, and the Agency's withdrawal after July 1975. It then traces Army interest through MICOM, AMSAA, and INSCOM, including INSCOM's plan to train six remote viewing specialists and AMSAA's contracts with SRI. Every episode in this chapter is the report's own recounting of earlier claims; none of it is endorsed by the committee in that chapter, and none of it is adopted as established fact in this assessment.

### Definitions, evaluation method, and foreign activity

Chapter 5 defines the field for the report's purposes. Extrasensory perception is presented as including telepathy, precognition, retrocognition, and clairvoyance, with remote viewing treated as a category under clairvoyance, and psychokinesis as the production of physical effects not mediated by any obvious mechanism. The chapter cautions that there is no theory or plausible concept associating remote viewing and psychokinesis with each other. Chapter 6 is a tutorial on evaluating parapsychological experiments. It stresses guarding against deception, the necessity of independent replication, and the limits of statistical significance tests, concluding that the quality of the data matters more than whether the statistical analyses are correct, and that the burden of proof lies with those who advocate the existence of paranormal phenomena. Chapter 7 summarises intelligence assessments of Warsaw Pact activity, drawing on a report of the United States Army Medical Intelligence and Information Agency and a study by Garrett AiResearch. It states that Soviet work, beginning with the laboratory of L. Vasiliev in Leningrad in the 1930s, concentrated more on behaviour modification and on physical mechanisms than on remote data acquisition, and that the available data were limited, speculative, and difficult to evaluate. Chapter 8 reviews four proposed mechanisms, namely an extremely low frequency electromagnetic hypothesis, a quantum correlation hypothesis, an extra-dimensional hypothesis, and a thermal noise theory, and finds none of them sufficiently persuasive or congruent with the empirical evidence to justify designing experiments to verify them.

### The evaluation of the SRI experiments

Chapter 9, the longest chapter in the main body, evaluates the remote viewing experiments of Puthoff and Targ at SRI by type. On the long-distance coordinate work known as Project SCANATE, it finds the Kerguelen Island and West Virginia results impressive on a first reading, but raises the timing problem that the most detailed information was obtained in second readings on the following day. On the local target studies, it criticises the classification and shifting categorisation of subjects, the selection and preparation of the target pool, the opportunities for cueing, the feedback given to subjects, and above all the judging procedure, including the selection of judges and the vagueness of the definition of a target. On the technology target studies, it notes inconsistent reporting between accounts of the same experiments. On long-distance targets with cueing, it notes that one celebrated response was produced by Russell Targ himself, standing in for a missing subject. Its summary is balanced in form: it credits SRI with developing a protocol within which apparently useful remote viewing had been demonstrated, and concedes that some results are difficult to explain except by a paranormal ability or by uncontrolled procedures, while listing as negative characteristics the insufficient experimental detail, the conflicting and inaccurate reporting, and the large methodological weaknesses that leave non-paranormal explanations open. On statistics, it judges the Morris free-response procedure valid as far as the committee could evaluate it, while noting that it uses only part of the data and that SRI's chosen sample sizes bordered on infringing the technique's assumptions. It concludes that mere replication of the existing protocol would provide no additional useful information, that objective scoring based on target elements must replace subjective matching, and that future experimenters must be independent of investigators with a vested interest in the results.

### The annexes present in the text read

Annex 1 lists the committee membership and Annex 2 the visits made. Annex 3, on AMSAA, commends that staff's candour but concludes that replicating the SRI procedures will not settle the military value of remote viewing, and recommends that the SRI effort and the current AMSAA effort both be terminated. Annex 4, on MICOM, examines the proposed psychokinesis experiment on random sources in detail. It questions whether the experiment is a replication at all, since the SRI experiment it mirrors had not yet been performed, doubts that one further result among the fifty-five previously reported could be decisive, finds the assumed connection between psychokinesis and remote viewing uncompelling, and recommends that the psychokinesis effort represented by the SRI and MICOM proposals be stopped and the research effort concentrated on remote viewing. The text read ends partway through Annex 4, at approximately page 55 of the report's own pagination.

## Errors and Anomalies

The scanned text is heavily affected by optical character recognition errors, including garbled running heads, release stamps, and individual words throughout. Those errors belong to the scan and its recognition, not to the 1979 report, and no finding in this assessment rests on a garbled passage where the intended reading was in doubt.

The report itself records reporting inconsistencies in the SRI literature it reviewed, including a target described in one report as an airplane simulator and in another as a complete machine shop, with the same data and judging responses in both. That inconsistency is the committee's observation about SRI's reporting, not an error of this committee report.
