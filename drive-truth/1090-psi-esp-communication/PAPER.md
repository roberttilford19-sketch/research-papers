## Abstract

This file pairs a 1985 SRI International proposal with the published literature behind it. The white paper argues that a psi communications channel would be valuable because, on its premise, psi cannot be shielded or jammed, but that three obstacles block operational use: the analytical character of messages such as code numbers, delays in feedback to the percipient, and the displacement effects of performing several tasks before any feedback is given. It proposes to translate numbers into binary to bypass psychological biases toward particular digits, citing a pilot study in which one subject achieved a 76 percent hit rate over one hundred computerized binary trials. Phase I would test performance under feedback delayed by one, two, four, and twenty-four hours, with a bracketing technique of immediate-feedback experiments run before and after to anchor the delayed trials. Phase II would extend the number of bits determined without intermediate feedback, aiming at eighteen bits at a stable 76 percent rate with feedback delayed a full day. Phase III would then transmit three-digit numbers encoded from decimal to octal to binary, together with their complements, using statistical enhancement procedures the paper describes as more efficient than Ryzl's. Appended articles present Ryzl's model of parapsychological communication and his repeated-guessing method, and a study of ESP under social competition.

## Content account

The white paper, SRI document GF-0283, is dated November 1985 and consists of nineteen pages as its cover states. Its introduction sets out the attraction and the obstacles summarized in the premise and abstract, and names its goal: a careful investigation of Ryzl's experiment, in which, the paper states, five three-digit numbers were correctly identified by the subject Pavel Stepanek, with the method described in an appendix. It notes that the area of psychoenergetic message sending was not being addressed by any of SRI's clients at the time, and proposes shifting emphasis toward foreign experimental replication.

The encoding idea is explained in the introduction and Phase III. A percipient's bias toward a lucky number, for example seven, could corrupt direct reception of digits, so the target number is converted from base ten to its octal equivalent and then into binary, giving an equal probability of a one or a zero in any position; the paper's worked example converts 349 to octal 535 and then to a binary string. The number's complement is also generated, producing eighteen bits in total, which can be randomized for the percipient to determine one by one. The pilot result offered in support is a single subject's 76 percent hit rate over one hundred trials in a computerized coin flip experiment using sequential sampling, which the paper itself calls by no means definitive, only a preliminary indication.

Phase I would run twenty-five binary choice trials under each feedback condition, immediate and delayed by one, two, four, and twenty-four hours, using a computerized binary search program. If delayed feedback degrades performance, the paper proposes to identify the decay rate, determine whether it is subject-specific or universal, and test the untested bracketing technique, in which identical immediate-feedback experiments are run directly before and after the delayed experiment to provide space and time anchors. Phase II would require the percipient to determine increasing numbers of bits, two, then three, then more, without intermediate feedback, under the same delay schedule, with the stated target of maintaining a 76 percent hit rate on eighteen bits with global feedback delayed by twenty-four hours. Phase III, conditional on success in the first two phases, would run the three-digit number transmissions.

The appendixes reproduce the literature. The principal one is Milan Ryzl's article "A Model of Parapsychological Communication" from the Journal of Parapsychology, adapted from his 1964 Czechoslovak technical journal paper. It reviews attempts to make ESP practically usable, including code arrangements, the repeated-guessing technique used by Fisk and West, Thouless, and Ryzl himself, Stuart's and Cadoret's index methods for estimating reliability in advance, and Taetzsch's designed but unbuilt psi communication system using communication theory to fix reliability in advance. Ryzl describes his own experiment in which a sequence of ten white or green card colors was allotted to each three-digit number, the subject repeatedly called the colors of cards in opaque covers, and the accumulated calls, treated by majority criteria with index trials mixed in, identified the coded number. His conclusion, as printed, is that the experiment is proof that ESP as a means of communication can be practically applied, while conceding the technique was short-distance and that long-distance use would need further research. The file's final pages begin a further article, on ESP and social stimulus, reporting a competitive game-like test with pairs of junior-high-school students in which the experimenter predicted which member of each pair would score positively and which negatively, with a reported overall difference at p less than .001; only its opening is present in the sampled text. Ryzl's claims are his article's, reported here as the literature the white paper relied on, not as findings of this assessment.
