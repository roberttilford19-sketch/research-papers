## Abstract

This three-page file is a released file-search printout reproducing two published articles on extrasensory perception research. The first, from Time magazine, dated 23 January 1984, reports claims by Ronald McRae, a former investigative reporter for columnist Jack Anderson, in his forthcoming book Mind Wars: that on the third Tuesday of every month in the fall and winter of 1980 a Navy officer allegedly carried a briefcase handcuffed to his wrist to a Washington psychic called Madame Zodiac, who, for 400 dollars cash, attempted to predict the movements of Soviet submarines off the East Coast from top-secret photographs and charts. The article reports a Defense Department denial that any money had been spent on such research, a former Defense Intelligence Agency director's statement that the military had been involved in psychic research, and claims about remote viewing work at SRI International. The second item, Constance Holden's "Parapsychology Update" from Science, dated 2 December 1983, summarizes a Congressional Research Service report on psi phenomena: funding levels in the United States and the Soviet Union, the main research centers and methods, a claimed 70 percent success rate in remote viewing experiments at Stanford Research Institute, and the absence of any conclusive physical theory of psi. Both articles are reported here as their authors wrote them; their claims are attributed, not endorsed.

## Content Account

The first article is the Time item "An E.S.P. Gap; Exploring Psychic Weapons," dated 23 January 1984 in the printout's database header. It opens with the submarine episode: on the third Tuesday of every month in the fall and winter of 1980, a bizarre rendezvous allegedly took place in Washington, D.C., where a Navy officer in a plain civilian suit carried a briefcase handcuffed to his wrist into the parlor of "Madame Zodiac," psychic and palm reader, who by looking at top-secret photographs and charts attempted to predict the movements of Soviet submarines off the East Coast, for a payment of 400 dollars cash. The article attributes this account to Ronald McRae, a former investigative reporter for columnist Jack Anderson, in his book Mind Wars, then forthcoming, and notes that several works by skeptics and believers on the military's involvement in parapsychology were appearing that spring. It describes parapsychology as the study of the interaction of mind and matter, and reports McRae's claim — McRae himself being skeptical of psychic claims — that the Department of Defense had spent six million dollars annually in recent years researching extrasensory perception and mental telepathy. Against that, it reports the Pentagon's denial: a Defense Department spokesman said officials had scrutinized the budgets for fiscal years 1983 and 1984 and could find no money spent for extrasensory perception under any label. It also reports an interview given to the New York Times by retired Lieutenant General Daniel O. Graham, former head of the Defense Intelligence Agency, who considered McRae's six-million-dollar figure an exaggeration but indicated that the military had unquestionably been involved in psychic research, adding that the intelligence community would be remiss if it were not following the field.

The article continues with further programs McRae is said to have uncovered: Pentagon-financed psychic research connected with the "shell game" basing mode for the MX missile, which would shift missiles among concrete shelters to confuse Soviet strategists, and esoteric program titles such as "Novel Biological Information Transfer Systems," presented as the Pentagon's way of saying extrasensory perception. It cites the December 1980 Military Review cover story "The New Mental Battlefield" by Lieutenant Colonel John B. Alexander, who wrote that there are weapons systems that operate on the power of the mind and whose lethal capacity had already been demonstrated, equated a first strategic breakthrough in defense extrasensory perception with sole possession of nuclear weapons, and urged the United States to step up research. It quotes physicist Russell Targ, co-author with Keith Harary of the forthcoming book The Mind Race, saying "I know the Government's involved. I did the work," while declining, because of his special clearances at SRI International, to specify whether the Defense Department, the Central Intelligence Agency, or both funded his psychic research, part of which focused on remote viewing experiments. In one test the article describes, Targ went to Grant's Tomb in New York City and tried to transmit an image to a clairvoyant in California, who described a high place with a view that made her think of a restaurant or museum — a description Targ argued was remarkably accurate. The article notes Targ's October visit to the Soviet Union, where he found the Soviets had replicated some of his reported experiments, and his statement that in the Soviet Union psychic research is taken seriously at the highest levels. It closes with Representative Charlie Rose, a North Carolina Democrat on the House Select Committee on Intelligence, saying the subject may be worth a look: if the Soviets feel it is worthwhile, he was willing to spend a few dollars on it.

The second item is Constance Holden's "Parapsychology Update," from Science, volume 222, page 997, dated 2 December 1983. Holden reports that Senator Claiborne Pell had publicly expressed interest in parapsychology, and that quite a few other members of Congress had been sufficiently curious to ask the Congressional Research Service to report on the status of such research, the service not revealing the requesters' names. The resulting report, completed the previous summer under the title "Research into 'Psi' Phenomena: Current Status and Trends of Congressional Concern," is summarized as discussing at length the potential educational, military, anticrime, and health applications if psychic phenomena were ever understood well enough to be replicable. On funding, Holden reports the Congressional Research Service figure of a paltry 500,000 dollars a year going into psi research in the United States, almost all from private sources, against speculation putting Soviet funding at tens of millions of dollars; the only recognized Soviet application she names is dowsing, taught to mineralogists and geologists at the Omsk Polytechnical Institute, and she notes a fairly recent Chinese interest in parapsychology, including psychic healing.

Holden's summary of the research landscape states that in the United States the field had been explored chiefly by psychologists, with physicists increasingly involved and employing the latest technologies to detect slight effects of mind on matter. Most research, she reports, is devoted either to extrasensory perception or to telekinesis, with extensive efforts to correlate psychic ability with personality variables and altered states of consciousness, the prime location for this work, according to the report's author Christopher Dodge, being the Maimonides Medical Center in New York. Another major thrust she describes is the use of random number generators to seek statistically significant psi-induced deviations, naming Robert Jahn, identified in the article as a dean of engineering, who claimed weak but persistent positive results, and noting growing engineering interest in possible psi interactions with computers, including disturbance of the memory functions of single microelectronic chips. She reports that the most striking results appeared to be coming from remote viewing experiments, where at Stanford Research Institute physicist Harold Puthoff was conducting double-blind experiments in which a person was sent out to engage in an activity and the subject asked to visualize where the person was, with investigators claiming a 70 percent success rate. She closes by reporting that the Congressional Research Service found no conclusive physical theory of psi phenomena, though theoreticians had tried explanations based on electromagnetic or sound waves and were looking to extensions of quantum mechanics and hyperspace concepts, and she observes that the report concludes with a glowing catalog of fields that could be enhanced by harnessing psi abilities while making no mention of the social disruption such powers could also bring.
