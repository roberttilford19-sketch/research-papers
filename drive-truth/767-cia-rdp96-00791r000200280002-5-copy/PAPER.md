## Premise

The file was produced to state a theory and then to apply it. Decision Augmentation Theory proposes that anomalous mental phenomena in the statistical regime are not caused by a mental force perturbing physical systems, but arise because anomalous cognition supplies information that biases ordinary human decisions toward desired outcomes, leaving nature unperturbed and biasing only the sampling. The theory paper develops that proposal formally and shows how it can be tested; the applications paper then runs the tests against the accumulated random number generator literature and related studies.

## Abstract

This file binds two related papers from the Cognitive Sciences Laboratory. The first, dated 16 May 1995, presents Decision Augmentation Theory itself: the proposal that anomalous cognition supplies information that biases ordinary human decisions toward desired outcomes, leaving the physical system unperturbed, in contrast to force-like models in which a mental force perturbs the system itself. It derives expected values for both model families under normal and binomial assumptions, shows that the models predict different dependence on the number of observations per decision, and gives power calculations and a Stouffer Z test for designing experiments that can separate them, noting one degenerate case in which they cannot be separated. The second paper, dated 14 May 1995, applies the theory to the historical random number generator database, to Princeton laboratory data, to a prospective pseudorandom generator test, and to six published discussions of the theory's earlier form, and assembles six circumstantial arguments against a force-like account. Its applications largely parallel a separately released applications paper, but the two versions differ in their treatment of two biological studies and in several reported figures, and each must be read as its own text.

## Content Account

The theory paper begins from the negative definition of its subject: anomalous mental phenomena are what happens when nothing else should, at least as nature is currently understood. It divides anomalous perturbation into macroscopic effects, which need no statistics to be seen, and microscopic effects in noisy data, which do, and it explains why experimenters trade complete physical isolation of a target system for protocols with counterbalanced control and effort periods, at the price of resting their arguments on statistical inference. A brief review distinguishes phenomenological models, which order observations, from fundamental models, which modify physical theory: Stanford's psi-mediated instrumental response model is presented as the phenomenological ancestor of the present theory, and quantum-mechanical observation theories are treated as premature speculation. The theory's history is traced to a carefully engineered 1980 random number generator experiment whose authors concluded that some form of anomalous data selection had occurred, and to a 1986 observation that effect size in binary generator studies varied as one over the square root of the number of bits, which produced the earlier Intuitive Data Sorting model.

The theory itself is then stated generally before it is formalized. Any experimental outcome rests on human decisions, including decisions that appear mechanical, such as how an entry point into a random number table is chosen; the theory holds that anomalous cognition information is integrated into such decisions, biasing them toward desired outcomes, so that participants become, in the paper's phrase, statistical opportunists sampling from an unperturbed world. Its domain is expressly limited to outcomes within a few standard deviations of chance. Four mechanisms are defined: mean chance expectation, anomalous perturbation, decision augmentation, and their combination. Under perturbation, a force shifts the mean of the parent distribution; under decision augmentation, the parent distribution is unchanged and the sampling distribution is shifted instead. The paper derives the expected value and variance of the squared Z-score for each mechanism under normal and binomial assumptions. The discriminating prediction is the dependence on n, the number of observations averaged after a single decision: force-like effects require effect size to grow with the square root of n, while informational effects are independent of n.

The remainder of the theory paper is given to test design. A retrospective test plots squared Z-scores against n and fits a straight line; for a force model the slope is the square of the effect size, and a zero slope with an intercept above one supports decision augmentation. Prospective tests require at least two values of n, with participants blind to them, and the paper works through power calculations, including a worked example in which 1,368 runs at the second sequence length would separate the models at ninety-five per cent confidence for an assumed effect size, and a corresponding calculation for biological target systems. A Stouffer Z test is derived as a further discriminator. The discussion concedes the degenerate case: if a perturbation effect size happened to fall exactly as one over the square root of n, the models could not be separated by these tests, and the authors resolve the degeneracy by an appeal to parsimony rather than by data. A mathematical appendix carries the derivations for the normal and Bernoulli cases in full. The authors also state their survey judgment plainly: they reject the evidence for macroscopic perturbation because of poor artifact control, accept the evidence for precognition and microscopic effects, and propose that the theory reduces the mechanisms of laboratory phenomena to a single one, the anomalous transtemporal acquisition of information. These are the authors' positions, reported as they state them.

The applications paper bound behind it, dated 14 May 1995, runs the theory against the data. For the historical binary random number generator database it reports the same central result as the separately released applications paper at census 765: a weighted fit over 128 studies with intercept 1.036 and a slope not significantly different from zero, median-half analyses, a two-point Princeton analysis in which a force prediction misses by 8.6 standard errors, a prospective pseudorandom test consistent with the theory, reviews of six published discussions of the earlier model, and six circumstantial arguments against a force-like account. It is not, however, the same text as the census 765 paper, and the differences matter for anyone citing either version. In this version, the Braud and Schlitz electrodermal study is accepted as its authors presented it: the present authors write that Braud and Schlitz correctly concluded their data were more consistent with an influence mechanism, and, taken with Braud's hemolysis study, they treat the biological evidence for a force-like interaction as growing; the census 765 version instead reinterprets that study through its epoch timing and describes its causal reading as premature. Several reported figures also differ between the versions, including the reaction-time probabilities and excess-hit counts in the answer to Vassy and the predicted value in the Princeton analysis. This assessment records the existence and location of these differences without adjudicating between the versions; each paper must be cited as its own text.

## Errors and Anomalies

The file's chief anomaly is structural: it is a bound pair of papers, not a single document, and the second paper's presence is easy to miss because the theory paper's appendix intervenes. The applications paper's running date, 14 May 1995, precedes the theory paper's date of 16 May 1995 by two days, although the applications paper cites the theory paper as its source for the derivations; the file itself offers no explanation, and none is supplied here. The two papers also differ in their author lists and orderings between their title pages. Within the text, the optical character recognition rendering degrades the mathematics throughout: the tables of expected values are present largely as headings, formulas survive in fragments, and figures survive as captions, axis labels, and scattered numerals, so no quantitative claim in this assessment was taken from a table, formula, or figure. Character-level noise is heavy in the running heads and release stamps. The prose, including every statistic quoted in this assessment, is legible.
