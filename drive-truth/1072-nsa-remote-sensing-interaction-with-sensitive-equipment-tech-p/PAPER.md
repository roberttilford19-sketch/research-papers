## Premise

The document is a thirteen-page intelligence paper titled "Paraphysics," dated 15 October 1976, identified on its face by the key intelligence question designation KIQ-09 and by the accession number CIP76004742 of 21 September 1976, originally classified CONFIDENTIAL with a warning notice for sensitive intelligence sources and methods, and released in part, in sanitised copy, in 2011 under NSA-RDP96X00790R000100010028-7. It was produced to state why paraphysics research mattered to the intelligence community, to assess what the Soviet Union was believed to be doing in the field, to describe the trends of Soviet research from the 1920s onward, and, in an appendix, to give two examples of recent American remote viewing experiments as illustrations of the kind of data being reported.

## Abstract

The paper defines paraphysics research as investigation into the paranormal functioning, or abilities, of the human brain, covering extrasensory perception, telepathy, clairvoyance, and psychokinesis, and states that investigations at medical centres, universities, and scientific institutes had increasingly demonstrated that such phenomena are valid, the major difficulty being their control and repeatability. It identifies five potential applications: access to information about secure or remote areas, including the location of submarines and clandestine operations; communication over widely dispersed areas, including to submarines and spacecraft, by mental transmission of imagery or by monitoring a receiver's physiology; direct interaction with sensitive equipment, including remote switching and interaction with computers; subconscious influence on a target individual; and whatever further applications research might reveal. Its assessment is that the Soviets were pursuing applications-oriented paraphysics research with military support, probably headed by I. M. Kogan, drawing on a research base reaching back to Bekhterev and Vasil'yev, and that specific applications under investigation included telepathic suggestion associated with the space programme and experiments similar to remote viewing in the Leningrad area. An appendix describes two Stanford Research Institute remote viewing experiments, a long-distance description of the Louisiana Superdome and a description of an underground cave site.

## Content Account

### Importance and applications

The opening section makes the paper's framing explicit. Paraphysics is presented not as a curiosity but as a field whose significance lies in its unique application potential, and the paper adds that the research could also contribute to fundamental issues, including modified or new models of physical reality and an improved understanding of basic human perception with consequences for neurophysiology, biology, and psychology. Of the five applications, three correspond to the subjects in the Drive filename. Information access is described as possibly the easiest application to pursue, particularly in the remote viewing mode, and the paper observes that such information, assuming reliability had been demonstrated for the people involved, could on a real-time basis be considered a breakthrough development. Communications rests on the paper's statement that distances have not been observed to degrade paranormal functioning. Direct interaction with sensitive equipment rests on experiments the paper cites as indicating that unknown mental processes can interact with quantum-level physical mechanisms, and it names remote switching operations and interaction with sensitive equipment such as computers as the possible applications. The fourth application, subconscious influences, is stated cautiously: since information can apparently be obtained from remote mental sources, it is possible that some quantum-level interaction at the subneural level could impact on the person from whom information is obtained, in the form of subconscious suggestions or mental imagery that confuses rational thinking; the paper says little is known about such a process and that it should be considered as a possible application alongside the others.

### The assessment of Soviet work

The assessment section is the paper's intelligence judgment, and it is hedged in the manner of such judgments. It states that there are indications the Soviets were pursuing applications-oriented research in several aspects of paraphysics, that the research is believed to have military support, is multidisciplinary, and may be headed by I. M. Kogan, a leading researcher who ceased open publication in 1969; that some research appears in the open literature, while that receiving military support would no doubt be highly classified; and that research by workers who do not appear to be involved in classified projects appears to be of a part-time nature and not clearly applications-oriented. It identifies the likely base of the military-funded effort in the work of V. M. Bekhterev in the early 1920s and subsequently L. L. Vasil'yev, and the later interdisciplinary approach of G. Sergeyev, A. Roman, and Kogan, and it notes evidence suggesting that leading neurophysiologists, including A. Luria at Moscow State University, and B. Lomov, head of a new Institute of Psychology under the USSR Academy of Sciences, were interested in the research.

Two specific applications are reported as under investigation. Experiments in telepathic suggestion are reported to be associated with the Soviet space programme and may also involve remote bases, including Antarctic locations; the paper cautions that the term may be a carry-over from Vasil'yev's early research and may actually refer to telepathic communication, and that specific details, scope of effort, and institute associations are as yet unknown. Experiments similar to remote viewing are reported to be conducted by researchers in the Leningrad area, by hypnosis, perhaps using methods similar to those developed by Dr. M. Ryzl, a Czechoslovak researcher who defected to the United States in 1967, possibly associated with Leningrad University, where Vasil'yev began research in 1960, or with Sergeyev at the Ukhtomskii Physiological Institute. The paper adds that people who had demonstrated psychokinetic ability had been studied by leading Soviet researchers, that no specific application is known to be pursued, and that the work of Czechoslovak researchers F. Kahuda and R. Pavlita on these phenomena would no doubt be available to Soviet researchers.

### Trends

The trends section traces Soviet research from Bekhterev and his student Vasil'yev at Leningrad University in the 1920s, noting that Vasil'yev continued work emphasising mental telepathy until his death in 1966, that unfavourable attitudes during and shortly after the Stalin regime meant he did not openly report his work until 1959 and 1962, that he established a laboratory for telepathic phenomena at Leningrad University in 1960, and that in 1965 a Bioinformation Department was formed in Moscow as part of the Scientific and Technical Society of Radio Engineering and Telecommunications, with Kogan as its first director. It names the more recent researchers and their backgrounds, identifies Nina Kulagina and Alla Vinogradova as the most carefully studied Soviet psychics, both appearing to possess psychokinetic abilities, and records telepathy tests at great distances, between Moscow and Novosibirsk and between Leningrad and Moscow, whose results were evaluated as significantly greater than chance. It gives particular weight to a 1973 article, "Parapsychology: Fiction or Reality?" in Questions of Philosophy, by authors including Luria, Lomov, Zinchenko, and Leont'yev, which admitted the reality of some so-called parapsychological phenomena and called for the removal of ignorance about how the phenomena operate and for a multidisciplinary research approach, and it remarks on the unusual character of such views appearing in an officially recognised Soviet scientific journal given the official philosophy of dialectical and historical materialism and the vigorous official attacks on anything connected with mysticism, religion, or the occult. Its conclusion is expressly inferential: although there is no direct evidence, the 1973 article, statements by symposium attendees, and the high interest shown by Soviet researchers in recent American research are strongly suggestive that the Soviets were pursuing a multidisciplinary, probably military-funded, highly classified, and very likely applications-oriented effort, possibly headed by Kogan and possibly associated with the new Institute of Psychology in Moscow and with basic research at Novosibirsk.

### Appendix A: two American experiments

The appendix states that it includes two remote viewing experiments performed recently as part of a feasibility investigation at Stanford Research Institute, under the monitorship of government observers, as examples of the type of data reported by gifted subjects. The first is a long-distance experiment over some four thousand kilometres, with the subject at Menlo Park under careful observation and the target, the Louisiana Superdome, selected by an SRI researcher during a visit to that state; the paper states that the subject's sketches show a marked resemblance to the actual scene and that the subject had no knowledge that the researcher had chosen the Superdome. The second is a long-distance experiment over some one thousand kilometres, with a subject in New York City and a target in a midwestern state, the experimenters having no prior knowledge of the intended target and the whole test under the direction and control of the government monitor. The appendix reproduces, as a verbatim description reported by the SRI researchers, the monitor's account of an underground cave site entered through an entrance arch and a small building with a long flight of steep stairs, with rock-lined tunnels, rooms lined with calcite stalactites and stalagmites, and an exit through a large metal door, followed by the subject's dictated impressions, which begin before the session with a flat semi-industrial countryside, a mountain range, and something to do with underground caves or mines or deep shafts, half man-made and half natural. The appendix closes with the observation that, as is often the case, the basic gestalt of the target site is cognised while specifics are misinterpreted, and that results of this type encourage continuation of investigations into the feasibility of paranormal perception and provide insight useful for threat assessment. Both experiments are the appendix's own accounts of SRI's reporting; the paper presents them as examples of reported data, and this assessment does not adopt them as verified results.

## Errors and Anomalies

The Drive filename asserts a provenance and a subject emphasis that the document does not carry on its face. The document's own title is "Paraphysics." Its released form bears a National Security Agency release stamp, and two of its five applications, information access by remote sensing and direct interaction with sensitive equipment, correspond to the filename's description; but the filename presents those two applications as if they were the document's subject, whereas the document is a general assessment of paraphysics whose centre of gravity is Soviet activity. Ledger card [#1072] recorded the filename's provenance and subject claims as unsupported; the body as now read supports the release channel and the presence of those two applications, and does not support treating them as the document's whole subject.

One figure caption in the appendix is dated, in the recognised text, 31 October 1978, which is two years after the date of the paper itself. A caption in a document of 15 October 1976 cannot record an experiment of October 1978, so the recognised date is either a recognition error or an error in the released copy. It is not relied on in this assessment, and the appendix's experiments are dated here only as recent relative to the paper.

Redactions marked (b)(1) and (b)(3) appear in the released copy, and several passages are legible only in part. Names are given in this assessment only where the recognised text renders them legibly and consistently; the name of the Czechoslovak researcher is rendered in the recognised text in a partly garbled form and is given here as Ryzl on the basis of the same document's clearer rendering in the trends section.

The paper's opening assertion that recent investigations demonstrate that such phenomena are valid is the paper's own assertion. It is not supported within the paper by any analysis of the investigations referred to, and this assessment reports it as the paper's position, not as an established fact.
