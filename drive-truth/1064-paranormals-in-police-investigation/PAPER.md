## Abstract

This memorandum for the record, released by the Central Intelligence Agency with redactions, reports two cases in which paranormal subjects under test at Stanford Research Institute were said to have produced information of operational value in police cases: the Patricia Hearst kidnapping and a Stanford University student homicide. The author states that in the Hearst case the subject Pat Price was approached by Alameda police the night after the kidnapping, before publicity, and provided a significant amount of data, including identification of people later shown to be involved; the session, the memorandum says, was taped by the investigators Harold Puthoff and Russell Targ and verified by an SRI group leader. The homicide case, the author states, was witnessed by an officer of the Office of Technical Services, and evaluations were said to be attached from the Alameda County district attorney's office and the officer in charge. The author is careful about the limits of his own knowledge: the wider stories of aid to San Francisco area police reached him anecdotally from the SRI investigators, and he states that he did not attempt direct verification, partly because even the appearance of Agency involvement in domestic police operations would be politically explosive.

## Content Account

The memorandum's first paragraph states its whole subject. Two paranormal subjects being tested at Stanford Research Institute had, in the author's words, produced impressive data on two live police cases: the Patricia Hearst kidnapping and a Stanford University student homicide. The data for both cases, he writes, was generated spontaneously in an uncontrolled field environment. The generation of the homicide data was witnessed, as the released text renders it, by an officer identified by the initials of the Office of Technical Services. An evaluation of the data, he adds, is independently given in attached memoranda arising from a second-party interview with the Alameda County district attorney's office and with the police officer in charge of the homicide case; those attachments are referred to throughout the memorandum but are not themselves present in this file.

The second paragraph is a disclaimer, and it is the most careful writing in the document. During the past year of investigations at the institute, the author writes, several stories of aid given by people with paranormal ability to police in the San Francisco area were related to him by the institute's investigators, Dr. Puthoff and Mr. Targ. His personal knowledge of these activities, he states, has remained anecdotal for several reasons, one compelling reason being that even the appearance of Central Intelligence Agency involvement with domestic police operations is, in his phrase, a political explosive association; that fact, he writes, has kept him from attempting direct verification of the institute's claims. With respect to the Hearst kidnapping, he adds, there were explicit threats on the lives of those working on the case, and the case was not yet solved.

The two documented cases are then taken in turn. In the Hearst case, the memorandum states that the subject, Mr. Pat Price, was approached by the Alameda police the night after the kidnapping and before any publicity occurred, and that he provided a significant amount of data, including identification of people later proven to be involved. The interaction, the author writes, was taped by the principal investigators, Puthoff and Targ, and verified by the Electronics and Bioengineering group leader at the institute, Mr. E. Jones, and all of this documentation is available for inspection. In the homicide case, the memorandum states that the results are described in its second attachment, and that the case is of interest because an officer of the Office of Technical Services witnessed the sessions; a separate description was being prepared, and from remarks made to the author, it appears, as the text renders it, that all the claimed results and more are verified. The memorandum closes by stating why the cases are inserted into the record: they involve two subjects the Agency has under test, who produced operationally significant data for the police in two completely independent cases under different circumstances, and there are, the author writes in a sentence whose ending is not legibly recovered, no explanations for their performance. Every factual assertion in this account is the memorandum's own, reported as its author states it; the memorandum itself distinguishes what was documented from what the author knew only anecdotally, and this assessment preserves that distinction and adopts none of the claims.
