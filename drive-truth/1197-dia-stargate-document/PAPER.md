## Premise

The report exists to document what was discussed and decided at a two-day off-site review of the Defense Intelligence Agency office responsible for the Star Gate programme, held on 7 and 8 March 1991, and to record the issues that needed further attention and the action items that followed from them.

## Abstract

This Defense Intelligence Agency report, numbered DT-S-1033-SL and dated 29 April 1991, records an off-site review attended by fourteen people whose names are withheld in the released text. It opens with the fiscal year 1991 Congressional direction under which the office was working, and with the contradictions that direction created: shifting interests among new Congressional staff, the psychological effect of the office's billets being zeroed out for fiscal year 1992, and the amount of project time consumed by support to Desert Storm. It recommends that the main emphasis fall on supporting external research and on foreign assessment, and that a clear charter and a long-range comprehensive plan be prepared. A separate section argues that Star Gate reporting should be tasked, reported, and evaluated inside the same all-source management system used for other intelligence disciplines. The report then summarises foreign parapsychology activity, reviews external research objectives including brain-response measurement, lucid dreaming, and personality research, and closes with its central finding: that the lack of a clear, management-approved mission statement was the greatest single hindrance to the office's work, with a draft strategic plan due by 30 April 1991 as the resulting action item.

## Content account

The report's scope is deliberately administrative. It does not present experimental results of its own. Its value is as a record of how a government office understood its own position at a particular moment, and its content should be read in that light.

The overview section records that the Congressional direction was organised around research and development, foreign assessment, and the use of the office's personnel in support of various functions, and that thirty project reports had been produced to date. It also records the office's own diagnosis of its difficulty. Existing constraints and uncertainties left it operating under apparent contradictions, unsure which aspect of its directions deserved emphasis, and short of time to accomplish its fiscal year objectives. The agreed response was to prepare a clear charter with priorities identified, to place the main emphasis on external research support and foreign assessment, and to complete the long-range comprehensive plan called for in the Congressional guidance in the early to mid-May period.

The data flow section argues for normalisation. Requirements would be levied as they are for any other intelligence discipline, results would be evaluated in an all-source environment, and comparisons with other disciplines would be made on that basis. Selected analytical personnel would be made cognizant of the project so that comparisons would be meaningful, and selected personnel would act in an oversight capacity to protect objectivity. The procedures identified as most appropriate were those in Volume III of the Department of Defense HUMINT management manual, DIAM 58-13.

The foreign activities section summarises reporting on the Soviet Union, China, and one further country whose name is withheld. The presenter is reported as noting three obstacles: a lack of good reporting, a tendency for available reports to express scepticism about the collection activity itself, and a difference of approach between East and West. The report attributes to that presentation the view that the West is preoccupied with proving the phenomena exist, while the East accepts their reality and concentrates on mechanisms and applications, and that the East is therefore probably ahead. That is a claim made inside the meeting and recorded by the report. It is not a finding this assessment can verify, and it is not adopted here.

The research sections describe plans rather than outcomes. They include magnetoencephalograph work on whether brain responses differ when a remote viewer views a light stimulus, data pattern analysis of past experiments, work on environmental correlations such as geomagnetic levels, training and proficiency practice at Fort Meade, search applications, a Scientific Oversight Committee and a Human Use Review Panel, lucid dreaming as a possible supplement to remote viewing, personality characteristics associated in the reported studies with psi experiences, and a plan for mental communication. An office automation section lists five objectives for a planned data system. A history briefing traced the field from mesmerism and depth psychology through psychical research and parapsychology to remote viewing, and noted the 1988 National Research Council report and responses to it.

The closing section returns to the mission problem. The report states that the lack of a clear mission statement, approved by management, was agreed unanimously to be the greatest single hindrance to the office's sense of direction, with direct effects on its ability to prioritise tasking, develop schedules, and identify resource requirements. A generic mission statement was drafted at the meeting for expansion. The second action item was a draft strategic plan by 30 April 1991, ahead of the June planning and budgeting cycle.

## Limits

This assessment rests on the full OCR text of 22,632 characters, covering the report's stated 17 pages. The report is evidence of what the office discussed and planned in March 1991, and it is presented as that and no more.
