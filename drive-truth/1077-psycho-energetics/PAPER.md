## Premise

The document is a draft proposal, marked Secret in its original classification markings, for an integrated Department of Defense psychoenergetics program. It proposes a five-year research and development effort building on the Grill Flame project, dividing psychoenergetics into informational phenomena, chiefly remote viewing and extrasensory perception, and energetic phenomena, chiefly psychokinesis. The proposal sets out to justify the program to defense decision makers, define its scope and objectives, estimate its costs, describe its management, and list candidate research tasks, with applications to intelligence collection emphasized and foreign, especially Soviet and Chinese, work treated as a potential threat to be assessed.

## Abstract

This draft proposal asks the Department of Defense to fund a coordinated five-year psychoenergetics program growing out of the earlier Grill Flame project. It defines psychoenergetics as two families of claimed capability: informational phenomena such as remote viewing, and energetic phenomena such as psychokinesis. Drawing on what it presents as the key findings of Grill Flame, the proposal asserts that remote viewing is a real and reproducible phenomenon not degraded by distance or shielding, that training methods for it can be developed, and that significant, well-funded foreign research in the Soviet Union and China poses a potential threat to United States national security. On that basis it proposes a program weighted toward applications for phenomena it treats as sufficiently evidenced, and toward validation for phenomena it treats as uncertain, all under strict scientific evaluation methods and frequent review. It estimates total costs of 11.5 million dollars across fiscal years 1984 to 1988, divided among applied investigations, applied research, basic research, and database work. An appendix details candidate tasks, including psychological profiling and mass screening for remote viewing talent, discrete search techniques for locating targets, physiological correlates, and psychokinesis experiments on quantum and classical systems, together with fundamental mechanisms research. The document is a proposal: its findings are inherited claims, and its program is a plan, not a record of results.

## Content account

The proposal opens with a preface defining its terms. Remote viewing and extrasensory perception are defined as the ability to describe remote geographical areas or concealed data through undefined transmission mechanisms, and psychokinesis as the mental ability to influence physical or biological systems without known physical mechanisms. The preface states the program's sorting rule: phenomena with sufficient evidence supporting their reality, remote viewing being the example, receive application emphasis from the outset, while uncertain phenomena, such as psychokinetic influence on sensors, receive validation emphasis until the effects can be statistically demonstrated.

The background section states that United States Government investigations began in 1971, when the Central Intelligence Agency initiated remote viewing work with SRI International, and that seven defense elements had been involved at various times. It describes the three-year Grill Flame effort begun in 1981, funded mainly by DIA and Army INSCOM with DIA providing central management, and lists the findings the proposal takes from it: that remote viewing is real and reproducible, that its data do not appear degraded by long distances or shielding, that training methodologies can be developed, that descriptive, configurational content is more reliable than analytical content such as function or purpose, and that significant foreign activity exists in the Soviet Union and China. The proposal treats these as established inputs; they are the prior project's claims as the proposal reports them, and the proposal itself offers no new experimental evidence for them.

The scope and timing sections describe a five-year program mixing direct application investigations, application-oriented research, theoretical work on possible mechanisms, and continuing assessment of the foreign threat. The first year is planned mainly for database collection, task preparation, and contractor selection, the middle years for the bulk of applied and basic research, and the final year for detailed evaluation of operational utility and reliability, threat assessment, and countermeasures. Table I estimates costs in millions of dollars by fiscal year: totals of 1.2, 1.9, 2.6, 3.2, and 2.6 million for fiscal years 1984 through 1988, summing to 11.5 million, divided among applied investigations at 2.7 million, applied research at 5.9 million, basic research at 2.0 million, and database collection and management at 0.9 million.

The investigations section explains how applications would be evaluated by data type, distinguishing configurational data, such as sketches of shapes and spatial relationships, from analytical data, such as function, purpose, numbers, and names, and states that improving analytical content would receive emphasis because the configurational kind had been observed to be more reliable. The appendix of candidate tasks, sampled for this assessment, includes a psychological profiling and screening project intended to identify individuals likely to perform well at remote viewing, costed at 92,000 dollars over twelve months; a mass screening project using automated call-in number-guessing formats, at 150,000 dollars over twenty-four months; a discrete search technique project, dividing a target area into zones and averaging guesses statistically to locate objects, individuals, facilities, submarines, or missiles, at 240,000 dollars over twenty-four months; a physiological correlates project examining galvanic skin response, EEG, EMG, blood flow, and voice measures, at 350,000 dollars over thirty-six months; quantum systems and classical systems psychokinesis projects, each at 240,000 dollars; fundamental mechanisms research on electromagnetic, quantum mechanical, and multidimensional models, at 145,000 dollars; and supporting tasks on theory and on upgrading the program's computer facility. Each task is presented in a standard format with objectives, implementation, funding, timing, deliverables, and the stated consequences of not acting.
