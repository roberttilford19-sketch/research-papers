## Premise

The report sets out to determine, by statistical treatment of the sighting reports the U.S. Air Force had accumulated from mid-1947 through the end of 1952, whether any trend or pattern in those reports indicated that some of them recorded technological developments not known to the United States. To do this, the study group reduced roughly 4,000 narrative sighting reports to standardized IBM punched-card abstracts, evaluated each report into an identification category, and then compared the reports that remained unidentified against those that had been identified, in order to estimate the probability that any of the unidentified residue represented a genuinely novel class of aerial phenomenon — what the report, following its own careful definition, calls "flying saucers."

## Abstract

Project Blue Book Special Report No. 14, issued by the Air Technical Intelligence Center on 5 May 1955, is a statistical study of the unidentified aerial object reports received by the U.S. Air Force between June 1947 and December 1952. A study group advised by a panel of consultants inside and outside the Air Force reduced approximately 4,000 sighting reports to IBM punched-card abstracts through a standardized questionnaire, coding system, and work sheet, rejecting about 800 reports as too nebulous or self-contradictory to use. The 3,201 retained sightings were evaluated into categories such as balloon, aircraft, astronomical, other, insufficient information, and unknown; 474 of all sightings, or 21.5 percent, were evaluated as unknown. The analysis tabulated frequency and percentage distributions across sighting characteristics, computed the sun's position for each sighting, and applied chi square tests comparing identified and unidentified sightings across six characteristics. A second pass re-examined the unknowns and found only twelve described in enough detail to test against criteria for a "flying saucer" model; no model satisfied the criteria. The report concludes that the data show no marked patterns or trends, that there was a complete lack of valid evidence of physical matter in any case, and that it is highly improbable the reports examined represent technological developments outside the range of present-day scientific knowledge.

## Content Account

### Origin, scope, and stated limitations

The introduction dates the public phenomenon to Kenneth Arnold's sighting of June 1947 and quotes the definition of a "flying saucer" offered by Dr. J. Allen Hynek of Ohio State University, who had advised on the problem since 1949. For its own working purposes, however, the report defines a "flying saucer" more narrowly, as a novel airborne phenomenon not readily explainable by the scientific knowledge of the Free World, a class that could in principle include intruder aircraft of an unknown type. The report is explicit that its data are subjective: reports consisted of observers' impressions and estimates rather than measurements, were often written down long after the event, and came from military channels, direct civilian letters, and roughly 1,000 returned questionnaires. Its conclusions, the authors warn, rest "NOT on facts, but on what many observers thought and estimated the true facts to be."

### Reduction of the data

Because the raw reports were not in a form suitable for study, the group built a questionnaire, a master coding plan, and an intermediate work sheet (later called the Card Bible), and transferred the data to IBM punched cards. Evaluation proceeded in three steps: deduction of discrete facts from the narrative, rating of the observer and the report, and identification of the phenomenon. Identifications were made twice — a preliminary identification by the person transcribing the report and a final identification by a conference of four, two from the Air Technical Intelligence Center and two from the panel of consultants. Any report finally recorded as unknown required a group decision. Identifications carried two degrees of certainty: "Certain," glossed as better than 95 percent probability of being correct, and "Doubtful," better than even chance. About 800 reports were eliminated before analysis as too nebulous or internally conflicting. One acknowledged gap is recorded in this section: maneuver was recognized as a critical factor too late to define and code, so no maneuver code appears in the coding system, the work sheets, or the cards.

### The samples and the headline numbers

The analysis distinguishes three presentations of the data: all sightings (3,201 cards), unit sightings (2,554 cards), and object sightings (2,199 cards). Figure 2 distributes the evaluations: 474 of all sightings (21.5 percent) were evaluated as unknown, and among object sightings the unknown figure is 434 (19.7 percent). The report states that a critical study of the tabulations across all three bases shows no apparent change in the distribution of any variable from one basis to another, and no marked patterns or trends in any sample.

### Frequency distributions and the position of the sun

Pages 14 through 55 present the distributions graphically in 38 figures, broken out by year, month, sighting reliability, observer type, color, number of objects, duration, shape, speed, brightness, and geographic region, including comparisons for twelve regional areas of the United States and the strategic areas within them. A separate computation used IBM equipment and a deck of 9,000 cards carrying seven-place trigonometric tables to calculate, for each sighting, the sun's angle of elevation and bearing from the observer's time, date, latitude, and longitude. Among other uses, this made it possible to test whether a reported object could have been a mock sun (sun dog) or a specular reflection from an aircraft.

### The chi square tests

Mirror graphs of the distributions suggested that identified sightings ("knowns") and unknowns followed the same general trend across six characteristics: color, number of objects, shape, duration of observation, speed, and light brightness. The study tested this with the chi square test in Tables II through VII. Where significant discrepancies appeared, the study reports, the excess of knowns over unknowns could in every case be attributed to an excess of identifiable astronomical phenomena. The tests were therefore run a second time with astronomical sightings deleted from the knowns (Tables VIII through XIII), which gave a better fit, though the authors call the decision "not clear cut," since the chi square increased for light brightness and speed — two characteristics they note are highly subjective. The stated result is inconclusive in a specific direction: the tests neither confirm nor deny that the unknowns are primarily unidentified knowns, but they indicate that relatively few of the unknowns are actually astronomical phenomena. The study declined to carry the fitting process further, judging that the inaccuracies in the reports would make the result distorted and meaningless.

### The "flying saucer" model test

As a second approach, the study re-examined the unknowns to see whether a verified model of a "flying saucer" could be constructed. Of 186 object sightings in the sun-elevation groups examined folder by folder (164 folders were available), the panel reclassified many as possible knowns — 18 possible aircraft, 20 possible balloons, 19 other possible knowns, including two established as mock suns — leaving 100 still unknown and only 7 "good unknowns" described in enough detail for model-building; a scan of the nighttime groups added 5 more, for 12 good unknowns in all, out of 434 object sightings originally evaluated unknown. The report observes that all but about 40 of the unknowns were classified as such solely because of reported maneuvers that could not be ascribed to known objects, and that these maneuvers rested on visual estimates of size, distance, and speed. Radar sightings without a matching visual observation are dismissed as of no significance, with temperature-inversion reflections offered as the most logical, unverified, explanation. The twelve good unknowns are summarized with diagrams and grouped by shape (aircraft-like, cigar-shaped, and elliptical or disc-shaped). Four conditions for a good model are stated — the model must fit many unknowns, rest on reliable reports, contain elements that exclude familiar explanations, and be derivable from two or more non-conflicting good unknowns — and the case-by-case discussion finds that no case, and therefore no model, satisfies all four.

### Conclusions and the 1953–1955 update

The conclusions chapter (page 94) holds that the data as a whole showed no marked patterns or trends; that the unknowns failed identification mainly because of the reported maneuvers and the unavailability of supplemental data such as flight plans and balloon-launch records; that a verified example or model of a "flying saucer" could not be attained from the data; and that there was "a complete lack of any valid evidence consisting of physical matter in any case." The probability that any unknown in the study is a "flying saucer" is concluded to be extremely small. The summary, which the conclusions restate, adds material postdating the main study: 854 reports from 1953 and 1954 were evaluated on the same basis, with unknowns at 9 percent, and the report credits Air Force Regulation 200-2 (12 August 1954), which assigned field investigation to the 4602d Air Intelligence Service Squadron, with reducing insufficient-information cases to 7 percent and unknowns to 3 percent in the first months of 1955.

### The appendices

Appendix A (pages 95–254) contains the underlying evidence base: tabulations of frequency and percentage distributions by characteristics, indexed as Tables A1 through A240, cross-classifying evaluations by year, month, duration, and the other coded characteristics for all, unit, and object sightings. Appendix B (pages 255–312) reproduces the working paper forms: the tentative observer's data sheet and questionnaire, the U.S. Air Force Technical Information Sheet, the codes for the work sheet, the work sheet itself, the codes for the Card Bible, the Card Bible, and an example IBM card.

## Errors and Anomalies

The following are observations about this copy and its text extraction, made at this desk; none is a correction to the document, which is staged unaltered.

1. **Appendix A is largely illegible in the extracted text.** In this copy, the numeric entries of the Appendix A tables render in text extraction as disconnected glyphs rather than digits, while the row labels and table headings remain readable type. The appearance is that the entries in this particular copy are handwritten. The consequence is practical: the figures in Tables A1–A240 cannot be read, checked, or quoted from this text extraction, even though the appendix is the largest part of the file.
2. **The producing contractor is never named in the text.** A full-text search of the extraction finds no occurrence of "Battelle." The document credits the Air Technical Intelligence Center and an unnamed "panel of consultants" and "panel of scientists." The ledger card for this item describes it as the "U.S. Air Force / Battelle statistical study"; that attribution is not supported by anything in the document text read here and is recorded in this assessment as coming from the ledger card, not from the document.
3. **Internal figures differ by basis, and the report says so.** The widely quoted unknown share depends on which sample is used: 21.5 percent of all sightings (474 of 3,201) against 19.7 percent of object sightings (434 of 2,199). Both figures appear in the report's own Figure 2 and text; they are not an inconsistency, but any citation of a single percentage without its basis misstates the document.
4. **The study's own numbers move between chapters.** The summary states that 3,201 cases were used in the charts and that approximately 4,000 reports formed the study base, with about 800 rejected in evaluation. These figures are consistent with one another as presented, but a reader who meets only one of them will misjudge the size of the analyzed sample.
5. **The extraction is noisy throughout.** The scan is a photographed or photocopied copy with heavy optical character recognition error in headings and marginalia (for example, the title page's "PROJECT BLUE BOOK" and "SPECIAL REPORT NO. 14" are garbled in extraction, though legible in sense from context and repetition). Quotations in this assessment were taken only from passages where the extraction is clean and internally consistent.
6. **The report's 1955 update percentages rest on the report's own assertion.** The figures for 1953–1954 (9 percent unknown) and early 1955 (3 percent unknown) are stated in the summary without accompanying tabulations in the main study; they are reported here as the document's claims, not as verified findings.
