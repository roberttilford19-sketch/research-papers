## Abstract

This nine-page extract is Section IV, "ESP and Psychokinesis," of a sanitized United States government report dated July 1972, released under the Central Intelligence Agency reference CIA-RDP96-00787R000500250024-6. The section traces Soviet telepathy research from V. M. Bekhterev's work at Leningrad University in the 1920s, through L. L. Vasilev's experiments at the Bekhterev Brain Institute, which the report says led him by 1937 to conclude that known electromagnetic waves do not carry telepathy, to the later work of I. Kogan and E. K. Naumov at the Popov institute in Moscow. It describes Soviet interest in Kirlian photography and the "bio-plasma" concept, including claims that illness appears in bio-plasma flare patterns before physical symptoms, and it reports solar-flare correlations claimed by A. Podshibyakin. Its longest passage concerns psychokinesis research on Nina Kulagina by Genady Sergeyev, who, the report states, registered heightened bioluminescence from her eyes during apparent object movement and postulated that bio-plasma interacts with the environment to produce the effect. The section closes by noting topics it has no space to treat, including eyeless sight and psychotronic generators. Every substantive statement in the extract is the report's own reportage of Soviet claims and is attributed to the report here; none is endorsed by this assessment.

## Content Account

The section opens by explaining why its boundaries are artificial. It states that no distinct separation is possible between, for example, apports and certain aspects of telepathy, and that hypnosis also enters the area, which is why hypnosis is treated as a separate section elsewhere in the parent report.

The historical account begins with Soviet research in the 1920s at Leningrad University under V. M. Bekhterev, who collaborated with V. L. Durov on mental suggestion with performing dogs. It then follows L. L. Vasilev, shown in the first illustration and described as a pioneer of Soviet psychical research, who set out at the Bekhterev Brain Institute to identify the electromagnetic waves believed to carry telepathy. According to the report, by 1937 Vasilev had amassed evidence that known electromagnetic waves do not carry telepathy, based on tests in electrically shielded chambers and over extreme distances; the report also notes that some long-range telepathy experiments, using Zener cards and more unusual strobe-light and code techniques, had been published. After Vasilev's death in late 1965 or early 1966, the report says, the work passed to I. Kogan, chairman of the Bio-Information Section of the Popov Radio and Technical Institute in Moscow, who continued trying to relate telepathy to the electromagnetic spectrum. E. K. Naumov, chairman of the Division of Technical Parapsychology at the same institute, is reported to have conducted long-range telepathy tests from Moscow to several other cities; the second illustration shows sender Y. Kamenskiy and receiver K. Nikolaev with Naumov.

The report relays, with attribution, a 1967 Soviet Maritime News item claiming that cosmonauts in orbit communicate telepathically more easily and that a psi training system had been incorporated into cosmonaut training, while noting that the News provided no further details. It also relays informal reports, through the authors Ostrander and Schroeder, that the Soviets were working on psi systems for space use involving precognition as well as telepathy.

A substantial passage concerns Kirlian photography, named for Semyon and Valentina Kirlian, described as photography in a high-frequency electrical field oscillating between 75,000 and 200,000 cycles per second. The report states that by the 1960s research on the bioluminescence it revealed was under way in many Soviet universities, and that the Soviets coined the term "bio-plasma" for the phenomenon. It reports a claim from Kazakh State University at Alma-Ata that illnesses show up in advance as disordered flare patterns in the bio-plasma, and, again through Ostrander and Schroeder, that the Soviets might be attempting to link Kirlian photography with computers to analyze flare spectra. It further reports that A. Podshibyakin, an electrophysiologist at the Institute of Clinical Physiology in Kiev, found a correlation between bio-plasma, charted through acupuncture points, and changes on the surface of the sun, with skin electrical potential changing at the moment solar flares occur.

The section's longest single subject is psychokinesis. It states that Genady Sergeyev of the A. A. Uktomskii Military Institute in Leningrad believed Kirlian photography might uncover the mechanism of psychokinesis, and that he had devised mathematical and statistical methods for analyzing the electroencephalogram. It reports his intensive laboratory research on the Leningrad subject Nina Kulagina (given the pseudonym Nelya Mikhailova), including a claim that he registered heightened biological luminescence radiating from her eyes during apparent movement of objects, and his postulate that the body's bio-plasma must interact with the environment to produce psychokinesis, supported in the report by the observation that she could not move target objects placed in a vacuum. The report lists objects she reportedly moved — large crystal bowls, clock pendulums, bread, and matches among them — and recounts a test in which a raw egg in salt solution inside a sealed aquarium six feet away was reportedly separated into yolk and white. It states that Western scientists who observed her ability reported verification of it, but also that as of February 1971 they had not been able to visit or observe her, a veil of secrecy having been placed on Sergeyev and Kulagina for unknown reasons. It adds that Soviet researchers instrumented her during demonstrations and found her heart rate, breathing, brain activity, and surrounding electromagnetic fields all speeding up and pulsing in rhythm when an object began to move.

The section closes by declining, for lack of space, to discuss eyeless sight, which it describes as more of a fad since the mid-1960s with quieter research continuing at the State Pedagogical Institute in Sverdlovsk, and psychotronic generators, described as devices reported to store human bio-plasmic forces for later use. It directs readers to the parent report's non-cited bibliography for further reading on extrasensory perception.
