## Premise

The report sets out to review recent intelligence on Soviet psychoenergetics (parapsychological) research, with particular attention to the results of a high-level Soviet commission that reviewed the field, and to describe the new, more centralized research structure that, in the Defense Intelligence Agency's account, followed from that review. It is expressly a follow-on to the Agency's earlier study, *Paraphysics R&D — Warsaw Pact* (DST-1810S-202-78), and its stated information cutoff date is 15 January 1982.

## Abstract

This Defense Intelligence Agency technical intelligence report, dated 22 March 1982, reviews Soviet research into psychoenergetics, the Agency's term for parapsychological phenomena including extrasensory perception, remote viewing, and psychokinesis. Its central account is organizational. The report states that in 1975 a high-level commission was established in the Soviet Union under the direction of a vice president of the USSR Academy of Sciences, and that after a three-year review the commission's recommendations produced an integrated research effort: a centralized coordinating and review group with Ministry of Defense representation, and a new Bioelectronics Laboratory, housed in the A. S. Popov Society for Radioengineering, Electronics, and Communications, which reviewed and integrated work at other laboratories, conducted its own research, and screened the general population for people who performed well on psychoenergetics tasks. The report describes earlier Soviet reviews, including a critical assessment associated with the Society of Psychologists and the closure, on paper, of an earlier bioinformation laboratory that intelligence reporting suggested continued at a more secure location. It surveys types of investigation, from psychokinetic effects on instruments and biological specimens to healing, and closes with translated appendices giving the Bioelectronics Section's goals and an eighteen-name staff list. Throughout, the report cautions that specific data on claimed Soviet successes were not yet available for evaluation.

## Content Account

### The report's structure

The document as scanned comprises a foreword signed for the Director by Jack Vorona, Assistant Vice Director for Scientific and Technical Intelligence; an executive summary; an introduction; a long second section on the integrated USSR psychoenergetics program; a third section on types of investigations; a short fourth section on implications; and two appendices. The foreword states that the Defense Intelligence Agency considered investigation in this area to have breakthrough potential and that foreign research in it would continue to be closely followed. That is the Agency's institutional position in 1982, reported here as such.

### The commission narrative

The executive summary and the second section present the report's main narrative. A commission established in 1975, directed by a vice president of the USSR Academy of Sciences and including institute directors, deputy directors, and Party officials, reviewed Soviet psychoenergetics over three years. During the review, experiments were set up at various laboratories for the commission's observation, and the report states that as a result of these direct observations the chairman affirmed the reality of psychoenergetics phenomena in public statements. The report attributes backing for the resulting integrated effort to the Ministry of Defense, in some aspects to the KGB, and to high-level Communist Party officials, probably at the Council of Ministers level. Each of these attributions is the Agency's intelligence assessment, stated in the report with classification markings; none of it is independently verified here.

### Earlier reviews and the closed laboratory

The second section also describes an earlier, critical review associated with the Society of Psychologists, whose recommendations included comprehensive evaluation of future experiments by Academy institutes, a direction that parapsychological research not be published in the popular scientific press, and a prohibition on independent contact with foreign researchers. It reproduces at length a 1975 resolution of the A. S. Popov Society's Praesidium closing the Section for Bioinformation's laboratory on the ground that its possibilities as a public organization had been exhausted, while noting intelligence data indicating that the laboratory nonetheless continued to function at a new, more secure location, with some people who had open press or Western contacts barred from its activities. The report describes the later review, associated in the text with the name Kobzerev, as the mechanism that re-established a public sector and set up the integrated effort through a new Bioelectronics Section.

The section further recounts a 1970 Leningrad review of the subject N. Kulagina, in which a commission reported, in guarded language, that she could produce exposures on sealed photographic material by a method unknown to the commission, and states that similar conclusions were reached when she was evaluated again in 1978. These are the report's accounts of commission findings, not findings of this assessment.

### Party interest and healing

A subsection on Party interest discusses the difficulty of separating paranormal healing claims from psychological and psychosomatic effects, notes reported interest in healers among senior Party figures, and speculates on several diverse reasons for Party support, including military potential and the wish to express any valid phenomena in terms compatible with Marxist doctrine. The report presents this as analysis of motives, and it is recorded here on that basis.

### Types of investigations and implications

The third section reviews types of investigation known to the Agency at various Soviet facilities, covering information-type phenomena such as extrasensory perception and remote viewing, and energetic phenomena such as claimed psychokinetic interference with sensitive electronic devices, deformation of material samples, influence on plant growth rates and chemical reactions, and influence on the psychological and physiological states of people. The report repeatedly qualifies this survey: in most cases, it states, not enough laboratory data was available for detailed evaluation, and no firm conclusions could be reached regarding the validity of the investigations. The executive summary likewise states that claims of successful experiments had been made, including at long distances, but that specific details were not yet available. The fourth section, on implications, is listed in the table of contents at page 22; the sampled text does not include its full wording, and no claim in this assessment rests on it.

### The appendices

Appendix I reproduces, in translation, the goals of the Bioelectronics Section: to attract the scientific community to work on a theory of the biofield, to construct sensors and transducers of bioradiation, and to introduce results into technology, biology, medicine, and agriculture, with methodological leadership for analogous regional sections. It is signed in the original by V. I. Siforov, president of the Society's Central Directorate, and A. G. Spirkin, president of the Bioelectronics Section. Appendix II lists eighteen members of the Section's bureau with their positions, including Ministry of Defense representatives. These appendices are the report's reproductions of Soviet documents, and their contents are attributed to the report.
