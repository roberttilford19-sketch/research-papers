## Premise

The article was written as a narrative history of the United States government's remote-viewing programs for a general newspaper readership. It sets out to trace the work from its Cold War origins in concern about Soviet psi research, through the SRI experiments and the Fort Meade military unit, to the program's decline, drawing on interviews with former remote viewers and officials, and to weigh the claimed operational successes against the personal and institutional breakdowns its interviewees describe.

## Abstract

This item is a feature article by Jim Schnabel, "Tinker, Tailor, Soldier, Psi," published in the London Independent on 27 August 1995 and preserved as an FBIS wire copy in a declassified CIA file. Drawing on interviews with former remote viewers and officials, it traces United States remote-viewing work from early-1970s concern about a Soviet "psi gap," through Harold Puthoff and Ingo Swann's work at SRI International and Pat Price's 1973 description of a sensitive NSA facility, to the Grill Flame program and the Army's Fort Meade unit, its extended and coordinate remote-viewing methods, and the targets its sources say it was used against, including the 1986 Libya raid. The second half follows individuals: Mel Riley's career, Major-General Albert Stubblebine's patronage and fall, the DIA years under Jack Vorona, the unit's channeling and tarot practitioners, and Ed Dames's departure to commercial remote viewing. It closes with the 1988 Inspector General review, the retirement of the program's protectors, the end of the SRI program, and the Fort Meade buildings standing empty. The article's operational claims rest on the testimony of its interviewees and unnamed former officials, are presented by the article as their accounts, and are recorded here on that basis only.

## Content Account

The article opens with a constructed scene: a dictator on the run, conventional collection systems hunting him, and, in secluded buildings on a military base near Washington, a unit searching for him by remote viewing, around the clock, including attempts to see his future movements. The article then states its thesis as the account of retired servicemen and intelligence officials: that the United States intelligence community made serious use of psychic phenomena for two decades, that the DIA, CIA, NSA, FBI, Secret Service, Navy, Army, and Air Force were all involved, and that remote viewers were employed in hundreds of operations, including, its sources say, the 1986 bombing raid on Libya.

The historical narrative begins with Soviet psi research in the 1960s and American anxiety about a "psi gap." It quotes an unclassified 1972 DIA report expressing concern that Soviet efforts might enable them to learn the contents of top secret documents, mould the thoughts of leaders at a distance, cause the instant death of officials at a distance, and disable military equipment, and notes that the analysis now sounds alarmist. It then follows the American response: a young Stanford Research Institute scientist, named in the article in connection with laser research, receiving CIA funds; Ingo Swann's arrival; and the coining of "remote viewing" as a modern-sounding term for the techniques. The first claimed success is dated to early June 1973, when Pat Price, a retired local politician working as an SRI remote viewer, described a sensitive NSA facility on the East Coast in detail, including an overhead view, the layout of underground offices, and top secret code-word labels on file folders, an episode a former senior CIA official is quoted as saying settled the program's credibility with its sponsors.

By the late 1970s, the article says, the agencies pooled their resources into a DIA-managed program codenamed Grill Flame, and in 1978 the Army's Intelligence and Security Command set up its own unit at Fort Meade, encouraged by Major General Edmund Thompson, who is quoted that he became convinced remote viewing was a real phenomenon and was interested in its practical use rather than its explanation. The article distinguishes the unit's methods: extended remote viewing, in which the viewer lay in a darkened room in a self-hypnotic trance and described impressions aloud, and coordinate remote viewing, Swann's structured verbalization and sketching procedure, which he taught to five Fort Meade recruits and claimed could make a trained ordinary person more effective than a natural psychic. An unnamed long-time member describes using coordinate viewing for target acquisition and extended viewing for in-depth work. Operational anecdotes follow, attributed to the article's sources: remote-viewer clues said to have exposed financial misconduct by a foreign agent on the CIA's payroll during a subsequent lie-detector test, and Mel Riley's account of viewing a captured KGB colonel, in which a pocket calculator-type object he described turned out, as Riley tells it, to be a covert communications device. The article lists further targets its sources named: facilities in Tehran during the hostage crisis, hostages in the Middle East, the location of Manuel Noriega in 1989, weapons facilities in the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe, Silkworm missiles during the Iran-Iraq war, drug-smuggling ships, and Scud missiles during Desert Storm. It adds the qualification its sources offered: remote viewers were not always successful, and their findings were often used only to direct more conventional collection. Congressional supporters named are Senator Claiborne Pell and Representative Charlie Rose.

The article's second half is biographical. Mel Riley, an imagery interpreter selected as a founder member of the Fort Meade unit in 1977, is profiled in retirement in Wisconsin. Major-General Albert N. Stubblebine III, who became head of INSCOM in 1981 and was remote viewing's chief Army supporter, is presented as the program's turning point: his psychokinetic spoon-bending sessions for staff officers, neurolinguistic programming and firewalk courses, and his sending of staff officers to the Monroe Institute for its hemi-sync process are described through named and unnamed former colleagues. The article recounts that in 1983 an INSCOM staff officer suffered a breakdown at the Monroe Institute and was taken away in a straitjacket, that Stubblebine retired in 1984 amid other problems including a financial corruption scandal among covert-action personnel, and that a former senior Pentagon official is quoted that Stubblebine gave remote viewing a bad name.

After the Army expelled the unit in 1986, the article continues, the DIA took it over as Center Lane and later Sun Streak under Jack Vorona. It describes the unit's later character through the testimony of Ed Dames and Mel Riley: two women practitioners, Angela Dellafiora, who channeled entities in personal sessions for officials including Vorona, and Robin Dahlgren, who practiced tarot reading. Dames himself is then presented as caught in the same dynamic: trained by Swann, he used spare time on what he called advanced training targets, including apparitions of the Virgin Mary, Atlantis, the Loch Ness monster, and flying saucers, left the unit in 1989, formed the company Psi Tech, and predicted to local media that aliens would emerge from the New Mexico desert in August 1992. The article's own assessment, supported by its interviewees, is that prolonged alteration of consciousness can be dangerous for the inherently unstable, and that without regular independent verification remote viewing becomes, in Riley's quoted phrase, a generator of idiosyncratic fantasy.

The closing narrative covers the 1988 Defense Department Inspector General review, during which Dames and Riley both claim that files were shredded and burned ahead of the inspectors; the curtailing of operational contacts and recruiting; Vorona's retirement at the end of 1989; the death of the SRI program the same year, a brief resurrection at Scientific Applications International Corporation, and its final end in 1994; and Dames's commercial continuation of remote viewing through Psi Tech. The article ends at the emptied Fort Meade buildings. Its final state-of-play claim, dated to 1995, is that the DIA unit still existed in reduced form, transferred within the Pentagon bureaucracy, with its future disputed between the former colleagues quoted.

## Errors and Anomalies

The wire copy is generally clean, and OCR noise is limited to release stamps, page furniture, and occasional characters inside words; no name or date used in this assessment rests on a garbled passage. The article carries the FBIS notice that it may contain copyrighted material. Its historical claims are journalistic: many rest on unnamed sources described as former remote viewers, former officials, or a source who prefers not to give his name, and several of its most specific episodes, including the file destruction alleged during the 1988 review, are presented as the recollections of two named interviewees. This assessment attributes those claims to the article and its sources throughout and does not adopt them.
