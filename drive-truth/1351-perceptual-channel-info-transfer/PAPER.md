## Premise

The principal document sets out to examine whether information about remote geographical and technical targets can be obtained by mental processes that are not presented to any known sense, and to describe that ability as a perceptual channel whose performance can be measured in the manner of a communications channel. It combines a historical survey of inquiry into so-called paranormal perception with a report of experiments conducted at Stanford Research Institute, and it closes by suggesting areas of physics from which an explanation might eventually come.

## Abstract

The principal document is the article by Harold E. Puthoff and Russell Targ published in Proceedings of the IEEE, volume 64, number 3, in March 1976, under the title given above. The authors survey roughly a century of scientific inquiry into extrasensory perception, then describe a program conducted over about three years in the Electronics and Bioengineering Laboratory of Stanford Research Institute. In the experiments described, an outbound experimenter visited a randomly selected target location while a subject who remained at the laboratory described and sketched the location. The article reports series with experienced subjects, with learners drawn from the professional staff, and with visiting scientists acting as subjects, and it evaluates the results by blind rank ordering of transcripts against target locations. The authors report that the experienced-subject series reached statistical significance, that a learner series did not, and that accuracy did not appear to degrade with distance or with Faraday cage shielding. They discuss extremely low-frequency electromagnetic waves as one candidate mechanism and consider time-related and quantum-theoretical questions. This staged copy also contains later journal correspondence about the article, including critical comments and authors' replies, and reports of follow-on group experiments. All experimental findings summarised here are the authors' reported results, not independently verified findings of this desk.

## Content Account

The article proceeds in five main parts. The introduction frames extrasensory perception as a long-standing scientific problem and argues that information theory, quantum theory, and neurophysiology provide tools for studying it. A historical section surveys earlier inquiry. The experimental sections describe the remote viewing protocol, in which target locations were drawn from a pool by random procedure, an outbound experimenter travelled to the selected location, and the subject's narrative and drawings were later matched to locations by blind judging. Tables in the article give the distribution of rankings assigned to transcripts for experienced subjects identified as S2 and S3, for learner subjects S5 and S6, and for visiting scientist subjects. The article reports a total sum of ranks described as significant for the experienced subjects and a nonsignificant result, at p equals 0.08, for the learners, while noting individual direct hits within the learner series. Sections on technology targets, on considerations concerning time, and on discussion consider possible mechanisms and applications, including the characterisation of the channel in information-theoretic terms. Appendices give a channel specification procedure and an unedited remote viewing transcript.

Material following the article in this copy consists of journal correspondence and related items printed later in the same journal. That material includes a group experiment conducted with a large number of participants, critical comments on the article's reasoning and on the use of public funds, the authors' replies, a study using students in a university art course as percipients, and a computer conference experiment in which participants described target specimens at distances the authors describe as up to about 2,500 miles (as printed; the unit rendering in the OCR text is imperfect). Fragments of unrelated articles from the same journal issue, concerning electrical power transmission equipment and adaptive beamforming, are also interleaved in the OCR stream. Those fragments are not part of the Puthoff and Targ article or its correspondence.
