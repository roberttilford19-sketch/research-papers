## Premise

The file is a Central Intelligence Agency project file released in 2002 under the document number CIA-RDP79-00999A000300080001-3. Its opening memorandum, numbered DD/S&T 3697-73 and dated 23 November 1973, recommends that the Agency initiate a research and development investigation of paranormal perception phenomena at Stanford Research Institute, jointly funded and monitored by the Office of Research and Development and the Office of Technical Service. Attached to the memorandum is the Institute's proposal of 1 October 1973, "Perceptual Augmentation Techniques," Part One, the technical proposal, prepared by Harold E. Puthoff and Russell Targ. The file was produced to obtain a decision on funding a one-year programme and to place on record the preliminary results offered in support of that decision.

## Abstract

This file combines a Central Intelligence Agency decision memorandum of 23 November 1973 with the Stanford Research Institute proposal it recommended for funding. The memorandum proposes a tightly directed investigation of paranormal perception, allocating about half the effort to the operational use of gifted individuals, a fifth to identifying such subjects, a fifth to neurophysiological correlates, and a tenth to validation, and it argues that an earlier Office of Technical Service project had already indicated the existence of the phenomena, including remote perception of documents, objects, activities, and locations. The attached proposal, "Perceptual Augmentation Techniques," reports the Institute's first year of work. It describes coordinate remote-viewing training and double-blind demonstration tests with Ingo Swann and Pat Price, including descriptions of a West Virginia site, a site in the Urals, and Kerguelen Island; electroencephalographic experiments in which unselected subjects responded physiologically to a remote flashing light; and drawing experiments with Uri Geller conducted in a shielded room and with computer-stored targets. The proposed one-year programme would measure the accuracy of remote viewing against viewing time, distance, and target complexity, use client-supplied targets unknown to the experimenters, and test whether electroencephalographic measures could gauge reliability and provide feedback.

## Content Account

### The decision memorandum

The memorandum, signed for the Deputy Director for Science and Technology and marked as approved on 20 December 1973 in the recognised text, sets out four objectives in the proportions given in the abstract above. It states that a previous Office of Technical Service project, summarised in the attached proposal, had indicated the existence of paranormal perception phenomena, and it describes the demonstrated reproducibility of remote perception under controlled conditions as the reason the subject would remain of interest to the intelligence community for offensive operational exploitation or defensive threat assessment. It recommends Stanford Research Institute on the grounds of its investigators' competence and responsiveness, recommends a contract at SECRET level while remaining flexible on security arrangements, and addresses the security risks candidly: embarrassment if the work were fraudulent, adverse propaganda about psychic mind-altering or control techniques, and the possible instability of some subjects. The memorandum argues that the investigators' and the Institute's dependence on government support made involvement in fraudulent or poorly conducted work damaging to them, and therefore unlikely.

The same memorandum appears twice in the portion of the file converted to text, separated by pages withheld as exempt, which are marked in the release with the notation that the next pages in the document are exempt. That repetition is a feature of the released file, not an error introduced in this assessment.

### The proposal's background evidence

The proposal's background section reports three bodies of preliminary work. First, in a training mode, Swann was given the latitude and longitude of one hundred targets on the earth's surface, ten per day for ten days, and responded immediately to each set of coordinates; the proposal presents a figure of hits, lesser correspondences, and misses across the ten runs and a table for the final run. It offers as a striking example the coordinate chosen to represent the middle of Lake Victoria, where Swann insisted on land to the right of a large lake, a perception the proposal says a detailed map later confirmed. The proposal itself cautions that training-mode results are indicative only.

Second, in demonstration-of-ability tests described as double-blind, with client-supplied coordinates, Swann and Price produced descriptions and maps of a West Virginia site, a Soviet site in the Urals, and Kerguelen Island. Third, the proposal reports electroencephalographic experiments in which ordinary, unselected subjects were exposed to a remote flashing light, and the resulting frequency spectra are presented as objective, non-cognitive evidence of perception of the remote stimulus. It also reports remote perception of graphic material, including the series of ten drawing targets produced with Geller, among them a firecracker, grapes, a devil, the solar system, a camel, the Golden Gate Bridge, a flying seagull, a kite displayed on a computer terminal, a church stored in the memory of a text-editing computer, and an arrow through a heart stored in a computer graphics programme. The proposal records both claimed correspondences and passes, and for the computer-stored targets it notes two hypotheses that further work would be needed to separate: perception of information stored in the computer, and perception of the mental contents of the people who knew the target.

### The proposed programme

The proposed programme's objective is stated plainly: to investigate and develop techniques to enhance human perception of remote stimuli, using independent measures such as electroencephalographic correlates to evaluate and enhance performance. The statement of work has five parts. The Institute would measure the accuracy of remote viewing as a function of viewing time, target distance, and target complexity, and attempt to characterise the information channel in terms of bit rate and resolution. For some experiments the client would supply geographical targets unknown to the experimenters, to exclude an unknown leakage channel between experimenter and subject. Pictorial targets would be presented on a decreasing density scale, from clearly visible to invisible, to measure accuracy against target density. The electroencephalographic work would test whether a shift in the alpha peak could gauge the reliability of a perception, and whether such measures could be fed back to the subject. Finally, the Institute would continue work with subjects whose electroencephalographic patterns correlated with the flicker frequency of a remote stimulus, to determine whether that channel was reliable enough to serve for communication and how shielding and distance affected it.

## Errors and Anomalies

The Drive filename, "one year resch CIA responds," does not describe the document. The file is not a one-year research report and not, in the portion read, a response by the Agency to an outside enquiry; it is an internal 1973 project file containing a funding recommendation and the contractor proposal that supported it. The one-year element is the duration of the programme proposed, not the span of a completed study.

The recognised text is heavily affected by optical character recognition errors, including garbled release stamps, routing-sheet entries, and individual words throughout, and the names of the project officers and the estimated cost in the opening memorandum are replaced in the release by redaction codes, so neither is stated in this assessment. Those defects belong to the scan, its recognition, and the release redactions, not to the 1973 documents.

The proposal's own claims are reported above as the proposal's claims. Its training results are expressly described by the proposal itself as indicative only, and its demonstration results rest on the judging procedures the proposal describes; this assessment does not adopt any of them as established.
