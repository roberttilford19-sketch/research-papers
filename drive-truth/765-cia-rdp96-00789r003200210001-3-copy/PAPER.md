## Premise

The paper was produced to test a theory against existing data. Decision Augmentation Theory holds that anomalous cognition information enters human decision-making and biases decisions toward desired outcomes, so that anomalous results arise from informed selection rather than from a mental force perturbing a physical system. The paper sets out to determine whether the accumulated random number generator database, and several individual studies, fit that informational account or the causal, force-like account it competes with.

## Abstract

This paper applies Decision Augmentation Theory, which holds that anomalous cognition biases human decisions toward desired outcomes rather than exerting a force on physical systems, to the historical random number generator database and related studies. For 128 binary random number generator studies, the authors report that a weighted least squares fit of squared Z-scores against sequence length gives an intercept of 1.036 and a slope statistically indistinguishable from zero, the pattern the theory predicts and a force-like model does not. In a two-sequence-length analysis of Princeton Engineering Anomalies Research data, they report that a force-like prediction misses the observed value by 8.6 standard errors while the data fall within 1.1 standard errors of the theory's prediction. They review six published discussions of the theory's earlier form, answer timing-based criticisms, and reject one published likelihood-ratio analysis on technical grounds. A reanalysis of Braud's hemolysis study is the single case they find favoring a causal model. Six circumstantial arguments against a force-like account of the generator data follow. The authors conclude that the generator anomaly is informational, while urging that future studies be designed so the theory's tests can be applied validly.

## Content Account

The paper first states the theory and its competitor in statistical form. Under Decision Augmentation Theory, the parent distribution of a physical system remains unperturbed, but measurements are systematically biased by an anomalous-cognition-mediated informational process, appearing as small shifts in the mean and variance of the sampling distribution. Under the perturbational model, a putative force shifts the mean of the parent distribution itself. The two models make different predictions for how the expected value of the squared Z-score depends on n, the number of observations averaged in a single trial after a single decision: the force model predicts a slope equal to the square of the effect size, while the theory predicts a zero slope with an intercept interpretable as an anomalous cognition strength parameter. Monte Carlo simulations with a published pseudorandom number generator are reported to confirm the formulation. The paper also states the criteria for a valid retrospective test: participants should be blind to n, and the distribution of n should contain no extreme outliers.

Applied to the historical binary random number generator database, drawn from the studies catalogued by Radin and Nelson, the authors identify 128 studies for which the needed quantities could be computed, with sequence lengths from 16 to 10,000. Their weighted least squares fit gives an intercept of 1.036 and a slope of (1.73 ± 3.19) × 10⁻⁶, with t = 0.543 on 126 degrees of freedom, which they describe as not significantly different from zero. Because the sequence lengths are unevenly distributed, they analyze the two median halves separately; the lower half, which is nearly uniform, again gives a statistically zero slope, while the upper half gives a slightly negative slope that they attribute to outliers. They note that even a linear model is not a good fit to the scattered data, and they conclude, within the limits of the retrospective analysis, that all causal models proposing a shift of the mean of the parent distribution must be rejected for these experiments.

The Princeton Engineering Anomalies Research data are treated separately because their volume exceeds the whole historical database. Working from raw data supplied under a 1987 cooperative agreement, for a single operator at sequence lengths of 200 and 100,000, the authors compute what a force model calibrated at one length predicts at the other. They report that the observed value at the longer length fails the force prediction by 8.6 standard errors in one direction and by more than 2.4 standard errors in the other, while the two lengths are statistically equivalent as the theory predicts. They urge caution: the operators were not blind to sequence length, and the data are only a fraction of the Princeton database. A prospective test using a pseudorandom generator, in which two operators were blind to nine possible sequence lengths, is reanalyzed and reported to give a slope encompassing zero; the authors also correct what they describe as the original experimenters' erroneous conclusion about timing, explaining that the sharp peak at zero seed shift is the expected behavior of a pseudorandom generator rather than evidence of millisecond timing ability.

The paper then reviews six published discussions of the theory's earlier form, Intuitive Data Sorting: two criticisms by Walker, answered on the ground that his constructed sequences are not of the kind the model addresses and that his timing argument misreads the pseudorandom results; a test by Bierman that found no anomaly and so, the authors say, permits no conclusion; an electrodermal study by Braud and Schlitz, which its authors read as favoring an influence mechanism but which the present authors reinterpret through the timing structure of its epochs and describe as interpreted prematurely; a timing study by Vassy, answered with a reaction-time calculation; the hemolysis study by Braud treated below; and an analysis by Dobyns, whose likelihood-ratio method the authors reject on two technical grounds, that the rows of his scoring table are not independent and that his method favors the influence model even on constructed effect-free data.

Braud's 1990 study, in which participants attempted to slow the hemolysis of human red blood cells, is the exception the paper concedes. Reanalyzing all of Braud's raw data, the authors find a significant difference between the two sequence-length endpoints (t = 2.033, p < 0.023) that selection alone cannot explain, and they concur with Braud that the results indicate a possible causal relationship. They stress that this is a single study of thirty-two trials, that it is the only study they have found that supports anomalous perturbation, and that more than one such study would be needed before perturbation could be declared a valid mechanism.

The closing argument assembles six circumstantial points against a force-like account of the generator data: results are independent of the internal complexity of the device; independent of the physical mechanism producing the randomness, whether radioactive decay governed by the weak nuclear force or electronic noise governed by the electromagnetic force; pseudorandom generator effects are statistically equivalent to hardware effects although the generated streams were later shown to be unaltered; reasonable force models do not fit the data; in one study, bits one millisecond from a significantly deviating target bit were themselves unperturbed; and a detailed quantum-mechanical model of a noise diode could not be adjusted to mimic the observed data. The authors add a derivation showing that the total change of Shannon entropy for a biased binary sequence is independent of sequence length when effect size falls as one over the square root of n, which they offer as a fundamental argument for the informational account. Every result, figure, and conclusion in this account is the paper's own, reported as the authors state it; this assessment adopts none of them.

## Errors and Anomalies

The recovered text is an optical character recognition rendering of a typed paper, and its mathematical content degrades in the usual places: formulas for the expected values and for the entropy derivation survive only in part, several tables, including the table of predictions for the three mechanisms and the scoring table reproduced from Dobyns, are present only as headings with their cell values lost, and figures survive as captions and axis labels. Character-level noise is frequent in the running heads and release stamps. None of this prevents the paper's argument, methods, and reported statistics from being followed in the prose, which is where this assessment takes them from; but no figure in this assessment was read from a table or formula, and exact symbolic statements should be checked against the scan. One citation varies within the paper itself: the prospective pseudorandom generator study is dated 1985 in the abstract and 1986 in the body and references. This assessment reports the study without settling the year.
