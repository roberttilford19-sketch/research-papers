## Premise

The report is the final deliverable under contract MDA908-93-C-0004. It sets out to summarize a contract period of laboratory research on anomalous cognition — the laboratory's term for anomalous mental phenomena, encompassing what the wider program called remote viewing — covering biophysical measurements, data patterns and parameter correlations, theoretical issues, applied research, and research methodology, with the stated aim of advancing understanding of how such phenomena might be applied to practical problems and of moving toward a comprehensive theoretical model.

## Abstract

This final report, dated 29 September 1994, by Edwin C. May, Wanda L. W. Luke, and Christine L. James of the Cognitive Sciences Laboratory at Science Applications International Corporation, summarizes research under contract MDA908-93-C-0004. Its executive summary reports that all experiments that year produced what the authors characterize as highly significant evidence for anomalous mental phenomena, attributes this to expanding understanding of the protocols, mechanisms, and psychology of high-level functioning, and states that using anomalous cognition to send messages was clearly demonstrated not to be productive. The biophysical section describes remote observation, in which a volunteer's galvanic skin responses were monitored while another person, in a separate room precluded from sensory contact, viewed the volunteer's image on a monitor, testing whether people detect being stared at from a distance; and electroencephalographic work using event-related desynchronization in the alpha band as a possible central-nervous-system correlate, with three experienced receivers, a National Geographic target pool, and visually undetectable pseudo-stimuli as a within-run control. Apart from an early Stanford Research Institute alpha-blocking experiment, the authors report, central-nervous-system correlates had not been observed, which they attribute to methodological shortcomings their protocol was designed to correct. A theoretical section considers probable futures and precognition, the Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen paradox, and general relativity as candidate frameworks, while cautioning that the brain at room temperature does not appear to be a quantum system. Appendices include a target-and-sender analysis reporting that a sender is not necessary for free-response anomalous cognition and that dynamic targets outperformed static ones.

## Content Account

The report is best read as a laboratory's account of its own program, written for its government sponsor at a contract's end. Its structure is consistent throughout the portions read by this desk: an executive summary stating the laboratory's conclusions; technical sections on biophysical measurement, data patterns, theory, applied research, and methodology; a glossary and references; and appendices carrying the detailed studies behind the summary claims.

Two substantive positions recur. The first is negative and practical: whatever anomalous cognition is, the laboratory reports that it does not work as a messaging channel, and the report states this as a demonstrated result of the contract period. The second is methodological: the authors repeatedly explain null or weak results — in particular the failure to find central-nervous-system correlates — as failures of earlier methods rather than of the phenomenon, and they present their improved protocols, including blind analysis, automated rank-order and rating procedures, and computer-locked data files, as the remedy. A reader should note that this framing is the laboratory's own; the report is a self-assessment by the performing organization, and its statistical claims, including the executive summary's statement that the magnitude of its effects exceeds the value considered robust by the psychology research community, are the authors' characterizations, reported here as such and not verified by this desk.

The theoretical section is explicitly speculative. It canvasses probable-futures accounts of precognition, observational theories built on the Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen paradox, and general-relativistic treatments of time, and it includes its own caution that brain functioning at room temperature appears not to be a quantum system, so that care must be exercised before such models are claimed as mechanisms for anomalous mental phenomena. The appendix on target and sender dependencies, sampled by this desk, reports a 1992–1993 pair of non-ganzfeld experiments and a meta-analytic background drawn from the ganzfeld literature, concluding that a sender is not necessary for free-response anomalous cognition and finding a significant increase for dynamic targets; these are the authors' reported statistics, quoted in the appendix with chi-square and p-values as printed.
