## Premise

The volume was produced to gather, in one translated collection, the working record of a research movement: its commemorative and organizational proceedings, its experimental reports, its methodological self-criticism, and its practice literature. Its premise, stated by its opening speaker, is that the field's claims will stand or fall on whether its records meet generally accepted scientific standards, and the volume's contents are arranged to show the field examining itself as well as its subjects.

## Abstract

This item is a translated Chinese compilation, Research in Human Paranormal Capabilities, volume two, issued in an English translation as document LN734-85 and released as a CIA CREST document. Its table of contents lists a commemorative speech, a regional board meeting report, experimental papers on psychic writing through sealed envelopes, on breaking and restoring needles, on sensation transmission along acupuncture channels in twenty-five children, and on a psychic magnification effect, a methodological paper on the reliability of psychic-vision experiments, a chapter on posture in a health practice, and further items that lie beyond the portion of the volume available here. The commemorative speech marks five years since a newspaper report of a child distinguishing characters by ear, and urges rigorous recording, statistical standards, and solidarity among researchers. The experimental papers report, in their authors' terms, characters and stamp marks appearing on paper sealed inside envelopes, needle fragments rejoined, and measurable changes in skin temperature, channel current, blood pressure, pulse, and blood flow when children recognized test cards. The reliability paper argues that an experiment counts as reliable only when blocking, elimination of guessing, and monitoring are jointly satisfied, and sets out five strictness requirements for test materials. All results summarized are the respective authors' reported results.

## Content Account

The volume opens with its table of contents, and the contents matter because they define what this package can and cannot assess. The text available here reaches only partway through the volume, and the account below covers the items actually read, in the volume's order.

The first item is a commemorative speech delivered on 11 March 1984, the fifth anniversary of the Sichuan Daily's report that a schoolboy, Tang Yu, could distinguish Chinese characters by ear. The speaker treats that report as the founding event of the field in China, reviews five years of difficulty, and makes three practical demands: that case records, including records of healing claims, be kept to standards generally recognized by modern medicine, with statistical records rather than individual instances; that academic exchanges be more frequent and deeper; and that researchers maintain solidarity under criticism. The speech also concedes weaknesses in the record, noting that one named subject had recently lost his special abilities and that the developmental record for Tang Yu himself was not properly kept. The speaker's name is not legible in the extracted text and is not given in this package. A companion piece lists suggested Chinese renditions for English technical terms, including psi field, parapsychological phenomena, morphic resonance, and altered states of consciousness, which shows the volume's translators and readers working to fix a bilingual vocabulary.

The organizational record follows: a report of the first board of directors meeting of the Neimenggu human research commission on 10 March 1984, summarizing several years of work with young people claimed to have psychic abilities, repeated experiments and examinations, exchanges with other provinces, and arrangements and finances for 1984, together with an account of the fifth-anniversary ceremonies and the decision to date the field's beginning to 11 March 1979.

The experimental papers begin with He Qin-Nian's report on psychic writing and seal affixing through the obstacles of space. In the experiment described, a subject designated Z wrote a greeting and signature on one sheet while, the author reports, the same writing appeared in ballpoint on a second sheet that had been folded, placed in an envelope, glued shut, and stamped across the seam with the author's personal seal in the observers' presence; stamp marks corresponding to stamps applied to the outside of the envelope were found on the paper inside. The author further reports watching, on another occasion, a sheet move upward inside a sealed envelope held against the light and emerge without the envelope being altered, and a color photograph pressed against a sealed envelope being found inside it when it was cut open. The author notes the subject's reddened face and quickened pulse, and that the subject could not perform at will.

A shorter paper reports an experiment on breaking needles and making them whole again, in April 1981, using a measured steel sewing needle broken into two parts, one part retained by the experimenters as a check and the other used as the test object, with the rejoined result matched against the retained part under a magnifying glass. Its design conditions, that the test object have unique characteristics, that the container be sealed irreversibly, and that more than one person monitor closely, anticipate the volume's methodological paper.

That methodological thread is taken up directly by Li Guan-Jun and colleagues in a physiological study, and by Bo Jin-Hua and colleagues on reliability. The physiological paper reports tests on twenty-five children, three boys and twenty-two girls aged seven to fifteen, conducted from April to July 1981 at several locations. A test card drawn double-blind was placed on the laogong acupuncture point on the wrist, and the children's reported sensations, a tingling or heat traveling up the inside of the arm, are set beside measurements taken before and after recognition: skin temperature at points along the heart channel, current along the channel, blood pressure, pulse rate, and blood-flow graphs for arm and brain, including tests in which the channel was blocked by an inflated cuff on the upper arm. The authors report that of one hundred thirty-six skin-temperature readings, about two-thirds rose, with the largest average rise, 1.78 degrees, at the laogong point, and they present the pattern as evidence that recognition involves transmission of sensation through the channels.

The reliability paper is the volume's most self-critical item. It argues that seeing something with one's own eyes is often unreliable and of no scientific value, that reliability in this field has three components, observation and measurement, data processing, and statistics, and that psychic-vision experiments carry three special requirements: the subject must obtain no information through known sensory channels, none through a third party's channels, and no advantage from logical inference or guessing. It organizes its remedies as blocking, elimination, and monitoring, all three necessary and complementary, and sets five strictness requirements for test materials: opacity, irreversibility, uniqueness, double blindness, and unguessability. It then prescribes a manufacturing sequence for test materials running from design through simulation, in which the experimenters themselves attempt to defeat their own safeguards, through improvement, pretest, final determination, and evaluation after every experiment.

Two further items were read. The psychic magnification paper, which also circulates as a standalone document assessed separately at census 1172, reports acupuncture-triggered magnification of internal tissue structures as described by five treated subjects against five untreated controls. And the volume's later pages, as far as the available text reaches, begin a chapter on basic posture requirements from a work on health through a practice the translation renders as Jinggong, setting out principles of head, neck, chin, eyes, and face as the foundation of the practice.
