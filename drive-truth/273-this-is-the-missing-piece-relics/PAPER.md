A woman bleeds for twelve years. She's spent everything on doctors. Only gotten worse.

She thinks: "If I just touch His clothes, I will be healed."

She touches the fringe of His cloak. Immediately the bleeding stops. Jesus feels power go out from Him.

Notice the sequence:

1. She touches
2. She's healed
3. Then Jesus speaks to her: "Daughter, your faith has healed you."

The healing happened at contact. Jesus affirmed her faith afterward.

And later, in Mark 6, we read: "All who touched even the fringe of His cloak were healed." Not some. All. Not those with perfect faith. All who touched.

This is the pattern that includes all the others. The source is Christ Himself. Physical contact with Him—or with things connected to Him—mediates divine power.

Not because the fabric is magic. Because He is who He is.

Post #12: "That Sounds Like Magic"

The Difference Matters

Every time I post about this, someone says: "That sounds like magic. You're treating wood and water like they have power."

Fair objection. Let me clarify.

Magic says: The object has power. Use it correctly and you control the outcome. The ritual matters. The formula matters. Your precision matters.

Incarnational theology says: God has power. He chose to work through matter. The object is holy because He touched it. The protection comes from Him, not the wood. But He honors the connection He Himself established.

The woman with the hemorrhage didn't wave Jesus' cloak like a wand. She touched it. Power went out from Him. Not from the fabric.

The baby under the pillow isn't protected by wood. The baby is protected by God, who chooses to honor the Gethsemane connection present in that wood.

The difference is everything.

Magic is about control. Incarnation is about surrender.

Magic says "I make this work." Incarnation says "God works through this."

Belief has nothing to do with it—not because the object is magic, but because God is faithful, and He doesn't need our permission to honor what He touched.

Post #13: "What About Fakes?"

The Question of Authenticity

Someone always asks: "But what if the relic is fake? What if the wood isn't really from Gethsemane?"

Fair question. Here's my answer:

First: I've done the research. The crucifix bears a stamp from Tre Fontane, a legitimate Trappist monastery with a 1,600-year history. The wood is labeled Lignum M. Olivarum. The era matches. Second: Even if it's fake, I've lost nothing. A few dollars. A little time. No harm.

Third: But if it's real? I'm holding a tangible link to Gethsemane. A piece of the ground where Jesus sweat blood. A connection to the most important night in salvation history.

Fourth: And here's the liberating part—I don't have to be certain. I just have to carry it. The protection doesn't depend on my certainty. It depends on what the object is. If it's real, it works whether I'm sure or not. If it's not, my certainty wouldn't make it real.

Faith isn't about having no doubts. It's about acting on what you have.

I have the object. I have the research. I have the logic. I have the peace that comes from following that logic where it leads.

That's enough.

Post #14: "But the Church Says..."

What the Church Actually Teaches

Another objection: "The Church requires authentication. Without paperwork, it's just an old object."

True. And not true.

The Church does require authentication for public veneration. You can't put an uncertified relic in a church altar or process with it on feast days. That's correct.

But the Church has always permitted private devotion. The faithful may keep and venerate relics in their homes without official certification. The Church trusts us to exercise prudence.

More importantly: The Church doesn't teach that authentication creates holiness. It just recognizes it. A relic doesn't become holy because a bishop stamps it. The bishop stamps it because it's already holy.

The distinction matters:

· Certified = The Church publicly guarantees this object
· Authentic = The object is what it purports to be

These are not the same. An object can be authentic without being certified. It can be certified and later found fraudulent (it's happened). And it can be authentic but never certified—which is where many relics from past centuries sit.

The Church's caution is wise. But caution isn't denial. And the absence of a document doesn't erase history.

Post #15: The Incarnation Changes Everything

Why Matter Matters

Christianity is unique among world religions in one crucial way: We believe God became matter.

Not just appeared as matter. Not just used matter as a temporary vessel. Became matter. Flesh and blood. Skin and bone. Real, physical, tangible human body.

That changes everything about how we view the physical world.

If God can become matter, then matter can mediate God. If the Word was made flesh, then flesh can carry the Word. If Jesus touched things, those things carry His touch.

This is why relics make sense. Not because wood is magic. Because God touched wood. Because water that touched His body at the Jordan participated in the Theophany. Because ground that held His weight in Gethsemane witnessed the agony.

The Incarnation doesn't just save souls. It redeems matter. And matter that participated in that redemption carries that participation forward.

That's not superstition. That's just taking the Incarnation seriously.

Post #16: The Burning Bush Principle

Once Holy, Always Holy?

When God told Moses to remove his sandals because the ground was holy, what made it holy?

God's presence. That's it. Not a ritual. Not a blessing. Not a ceremony. God was there, and that changed the ground permanently.

The text doesn't say the ground was holy while God was speaking and then ordinary again afterward. It says "the place where you are standing is holy ground." Present tense. Ongoing.

This is the loca sancta principle: Places where God acted remain marked by that action.

The same logic applies to Gethsemane. Jesus was there. He prayed there. He sweat blood there. He surrendered there. That ground participated in salvation history. It carries that participation forward.

Wood from that ground carries that history. Not magically. Really. Because God honored that place, and He doesn't revoke His honors.

The burning bush didn't stop being holy when Moses left. And Gethsemane didn't stop being holy when Jesus rose.

#BeliefHasNothingToDoWithIt #BurningBush #LocaSancta #HolyGround

Post #17: The Elisha Principle

Death Doesn't End It

Elisha died. His body decayed. His bones were just bones.

But when a dead man touched them, he came back to life.

This is remarkable: The prophet's power didn't end with his death. Something remained associated with his physical remains. Something that could, when contacted, become the occasion for resurrection.

This is the biblical foundation for first-class relics of saints. The holiness of the person doesn't disappear when they die. It remains, in some way, connected to their physical remains.

Now apply that to Christ.

If Elisha's bones could raise the dead, what can Gethsemane wood do? Not because the wood is greater than Elisha. Because the One who was in Gethsemane is greater than Elisha. And the wood touched ground that He touched.

The pattern is consistent. The source is always God. But He chooses to work through matter—even matter connected to those who have died.

Elisha's bones worked after death. Gethsemane wood works across distance and time. Not because of magic. Because God honors His own history.

Post #18: The Hierarchy of Relics

Why Gethsemane Outranks Saints

In Catholic tradition, relics are classified:

· Third-Class: Objects touched to a saint or relic
· Second-Class: Items owned or worn by a saint
· First-Class (Saints): Physical remains of a saint
· First-Class (Christ): Objects directly connected to Christ's life
· Loca Sancta: Objects from places where Christ walked

Most people don't realize there's a hierarchy within first-class relics. A bone of St. Peter is precious. But a piece of the True Cross? Water from the Jordan? Wood from Gethsemane?

These are different.

Because St. Peter pointed to Christ. But Gethsemane touched Christ. The Jordan held Christ. The Cross carried Christ.

Saints are friends of God. These places and objects participated in the events of God Himself.

That's not to diminish the saints. It's to recognize that the Incarnation creates categories that don't exist anywhere else.

Your Gethsemane wood isn't competing with St. Peter's bones. It's in a different league entirely.
