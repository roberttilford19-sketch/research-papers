## Premise

The report was produced to test, on a gradient of increasing difficulty, whether a subject given only the geographic coordinates of a place could describe that place in ways that corresponded to it. Its final stage was designed to move the test out of the laboratory's own judgment: the decisive coordinates were supplied by the sponsoring agency under double-blind conditions, and their evaluation was reserved to that agency.

## Abstract

This document reports Project SCANATE, an SRI exploratory study of remote viewing by coordinate, in which subjects were given only latitude and longitude and asked for immediate descriptions of the place. It first describes a training series with Ingo Swann: one hundred global targets, ten per day over ten days, scored as hits, lesser correspondences, or misses, with the report itself warning that training results are indicative only because memory or information from a knowledgeable experimenter could in principle account for them. It then reports demonstration tests run on coordinates supplied double-blind by the sponsoring agency. Swann and a second subject, identified only as Pat, independently described a Virginia site in late May and early June 1973; Pat's account included an underground storage area, named personnel, and labels he reported seeing on papers and cabinets, and he volunteered a second site in the Urals. Swann described an island site in July 1973 over two sessions, including buildings, tanks, an airstrip, and a coastline survey. The report states that evaluation of the sponsor-coordinate results rested with the sponsoring agency, and concludes that a channel for obtaining remote information exists, that it is imperfect and noisy, and that its signal-to-noise ratio could not yet be determined.

## Content Account

The report opens in mid-argument. Earlier work on what it calls micro-abilities had led Swann to suggest that the work had strengthened a macro-ability, the viewing of remote locations, which he had researched before joining the SRI program. The laboratory's first step, reported in an appendix, was a series in which another subject received target pictures while separated from the material by a shielded room or by the distance between the two coasts of the United States.

The training series followed. One hundred targets on the Earth's surface were chosen at random, ten per day for ten days, and for each target Swann was given latitude and longitude only and asked for an immediate response, after which he received brief feedback on whether any correspondence existed. A full run took under thirty minutes. Results are charted as hits, lesser correspondences, and misses per run, and the report prints the detail of the final run: ten coordinates, from ocean targets and Lake Victoria to the Sea of Okhotsk, Iceland, Hudson Bay, the Gobi, and Lake Eyre, with Swann's responses scored against each. The report is explicit about the limits of this stage. Training results, it says, can be taken as indicative only, because a subject might in principle succeed from memory, or might obtain the information from an experimenter who knows the target. Final evaluation must rest on the double-blind demonstration tests.

Those tests used coordinates requested from a scientist of the sponsoring agency. The first set, called the Virginia Site, was worked by Swann on 29 May 1973 and again the following morning. He described mounds or rolling hills, a city to the north, an area resembling the lawns around a military base, possible old bunkers or a covered reservoir, a flagpole, and highways to the west, and in his second reading cliffs, a fence, and a circular building, with a persistent impression of something underground. The same coordinates were given independently to a second subject, a businessman who wished to remain anonymous and is referred to simply as Pat. His written response, dated 2 June 1973, is far more detailed: a mountain peak, a roadway, a Civil War battleground, and, at a stated time, large underground storage areas with record storage, file cabinets, computers, and communications equipment. Asked later for codeword information, Pat supplied labels he reported seeing on papers and in a file cabinet, a site name he gave only vaguely, and three named personnel. He also volunteered a site on the other side of the globe, in the Urals, which he described as an underground installation with helipads, light rail tracks, and a radar installation thirty miles north. The desk notes that these details are Pat's reported impressions as printed in the report; the report contains no independent confirmation of them, and the sponsoring agency's evaluation is not included.

The second sponsor set, called the Island Site, was worked by Swann on 21 July 1973 and again the next day. Given coordinates in the southern ocean, he described an island with rocky terrain, buildings laid out in an orderly pattern, one of them orange, a radar-like antenna, two large white cylindrical tanks, a small airstrip, and trucks, and on the second day he followed the coastline point by point, drawing segments that were assembled into a single map.

The analysis section is short and candid about where judgment sits. For security and logistical reasons, the report says, the sponsor-coordinate results were being evaluated separately at the sponsoring agency, and SRI personnel had not been informed of the target series parameters or the hit-miss profile, before, during, or after the tests. What SRI had been told, and what its three conclusions rest on, is that in each experiment some categories of information exceeded any possible bounds of coincidental correlation, in the report's wording, while some of the data possibly constituted noise. On that basis the report concludes that a channel exists, that it is imperfect, and that a signal-to-noise ratio could not yet be determined from the sponsor-controlled targets.
