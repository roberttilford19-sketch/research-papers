## Abstract

This document is a classified-documents bibliography for the Grill Flame program, pairing progress reports with a bibliography of Stanford Research Institute International publications on psychoenergetics. Each entry gives a classification marking, a title, a date where known, the authors, and a short unclassified abstract of the underlying report. The entries run from the Perceptual Augmentation Techniques reports of 1974 and 1975 through the program reviews of 1989, and they cover remote viewing protocols, training and screening, evaluation methods, database management, target search, geophysical effects, and proposed countermeasures. Several entries are progress reports for fiscal years 1981 through 1984. A second section lists unclassified publications, and a third lists sanitized documents for which the Star Gate facility did not hold the classified version. An asterisk marks entries whose copy at Science Applications International Corporation was destroyed. The bibliography is a finding aid rather than a research report: it states what work was done, by whom, and under what classification, but it presents no experimental data of its own. Its abstracts summarize the sponsoring program's own descriptions of its reports, including claims about remote viewing capability that the bibliography itself does not test or evaluate.

## Content Account

The bibliography is arranged in three sections. The first and largest lists classified documents. It opens with the program's foundational reports: Perceptual Augmentation Techniques, Parts One and Two, covering January 1974 to February 1975, by Harold Puthoff and Russell Targ, whose unclassified abstracts describe a study of a human information-accessing capability termed remote viewing and present examples of four experiments. There follow the Advanced Threat Technique Assessment reports of 1977 and 1978, a scientific evaluation committee report of December 1979, and proposed protocols of 1979 for sponsored remote viewing investigations and for research on remote perturbation techniques, the latter defined in its abstract as the perturbation of an event outcome by mental processes alone.

The middle of the list is dominated by the working reports of the early 1980s. A series of program progress reports runs from April 1981 through the fiscal 1983 end-of-year report of October 1983, with a fiscal 1984 continuation dated February 1985; the abstracts describe a multi-year evaluation program assessing the potential of remote viewing for intelligence applications. Around them sit the technique studies: work on the reliability, enhancement, and evaluation of remote viewing; a remote viewing evaluation protocol; coordinate remote viewing training; audiolinguistic correlations with session quality; targeting requirements; a proposed data-base management system for operational remote viewing data; a survey of countermeasures against psychoenergetic intrusions; and an investigation of remote viewing as a location aid for anti-submarine warfare. Two entries step outside the laboratory's own work: a survey of free world psychoenergetics research drawing on 233 articles, and a summary of psychoenergetics research in Hungary from 1983 to 1988.

The later entries, from 1984 to 1989, show the program's concerns narrowing to method and measurement. They include personnel identification and selection, a geophysical effects study of ambient electromagnetic factors, target search techniques (which one abstract notes are also called dowsing in Western literature), computer-assisted search experiments, fuzzy set applications to remote viewing analysis, a meta-analysis of forced-choice precognition experiments published between 1935 and 1987, neurological and neuromagnetic correlates of remote stimuli, hypnosis as a means of improving remote viewing quality, a photon production investigation replicating a Chinese claim, and screening of 256 candidates for remote viewing talent. The section closes with a review of the psychoenergetic research conducted at the institute from October 1973 to September 1988, whose abstract states that the database comprises 117 documents and over 25,000 trials, and with a February 1989 summary of government-sponsored research in psychoenergetics from 1972 through 1988.

The second section lists unclassified documents, among them a 1978 statement of suggested research approaches, the standard remote-viewing protocol for local targets reported in the Proceedings of the IEEE, and the unclassified versions of several studies also listed above. The third section lists unclassified sanitized documents for which, its heading states, the Star Gate facility does not have the classified version; it includes a bacterial mutation study, psi communications experiments, a photon detection experiment conducted during a remote viewing test, and a personality assessment system for screening and selection. Many entries throughout carry the note that the document has been sanitized to unclassified. Every abstract in the bibliography is the program's own summary of its own report; the claims they contain, including claims of anomalous perturbation in 214 electronic-system experiments, are the underlying reports' claims, recorded here as the bibliography states them and not evaluated by it or by this assessment.

## Errors and Anomalies

The recovered text is an optical character recognition rendering of a typed list, and tabular material of this kind degrades in predictable ways: author names are occasionally garbled, as with "Ingo Swan" for Ingo Swann and "Wanda Lake" for Wanda Luke; dates are occasionally misread, most clearly in one entry that describes experiments "extending the work reported in December 1994" in a document itself dated December 1986, where the year is plainly misrendered but its correct value cannot be settled from this text alone; and classification codes and redaction marks, including several "SG1J" strings, intrude into the entries. The entries for the program progress reports carry no named authors, several being attributed only to the institute or left under redaction marks, so the bibliography cannot support an authorship claim for those items. None of these defects affects the document's identification or the overall shape of the list, but individual entries should be checked against the scan before being cited in detail.
