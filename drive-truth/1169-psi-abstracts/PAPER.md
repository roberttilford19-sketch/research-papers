## Content Account

Because the page is a sequence of independent summaries, its content is best accounted entry by entry, in the order the numbering gives, while noting that the optical character recognition of this two-column page interleaves the columns and the entries do not read in simple sequence.

Entry 01462, by Persinger, reports a study in Perceptual and Motor Skills, 1984, correlating the number of different types of paranormal experience a respondent reported with the number of temporal lobe signs reported. In one group of one hundred eight university students the correlation was .60; in a second group of forty-one it was .72. Partial correlations holding constant affirmative responses to mundane statements did not alter the relationship, and the abstract presents the result as supporting a hypothesis that spontaneous paranormal experiences and the psychological components of complex partial epilepsy lie along a continuum of temporal lobe sensitivity.

Entry 01463, by H. H. Price, from the Hibbert Journal, 1949, is philosophical. It considers what the existence of psychic phenomena would mean for conceptions of human personality, finds telepathy difficult for both materialist and traditional dualist accounts, and reports an alternative interpretation under which apparent telepathy is the coincidental experience of the same pattern of thought by closely similar minds working under comparable conditions. The entry closes by treating clairvoyance as a legendary concept that may nonetheless have bases in fact, instancing diviners whose skill the abstract attributes to subconscious reading of geological features, local knowledge, and human cues.

Entry 01464, by Rawson and Hartline, from Science, 1964, is not about psi at all. It reports telemetry of homing behavior in the deermouse: miniature transmitters weighing 2.5 to 2.7 grams implanted under the skin, radiating a pulsed signal detectable at forty-five meters, used to follow movements, activity, and nest locations, including one mouse traced returning to its nest over three hundred meters in one hour. Its presence on the page shows the compilation's range extending to animal-navigation research of possible relevance to its readers.

Entry 01465, by Schwarz, from the Journal of the American Society of Psychosomatic Dentistry and Medicine, 1968, presents a psychotherapy patient with many reported telepathic experiences, including a telepathic death dream, possible precognition of death and telekinesis, and an apparition, and discusses the psychodynamic factors the author associated with them. The entry between Price and Rawson, on extraordinary beliefs among students in Singapore and Canada, reports a thirty-four-item questionnaire completed by one hundred thirteen Canadian and seventy-six Singapore students; the Canadians were significantly more skeptical, particularly about religious beliefs and spirits, while views of psychic phenomena were quite similar, and differences among Singapore students of Chinese, Indian, and Malay descent lay mainly in religious views.

Entry 01466, by Shewmaker and Berenda, from Philosophy of Science, 1962, examines what problems psi poses for a philosophy of science, arguing that they vary with one's conceptions of both science and psi, and suggesting that psi may be a product of scientific concepts being abstractions and oversimplifications. Entry 01467, by Ian Stevenson, from the Journal of Nervous and Mental Disease, 1983, analyzes seventy-nine American children who claimed to remember previous lives and compares them with a larger number of Indian cases. Few American children made verifiable statements, and those who did spoke almost always about deceased members of their own families, whereas Indian children usually spoke of persons in another family or community and often made verifiable statements; in age of first speaking, content, and related behavior the two groups closely resembled each other, and the abstract notes that many American cases may derive from fantasy.
