## Premise

The compilation gathers the paper trail of a single scientific effort inside NASA in 1962 and 1963: taking the Mercury astronauts' visual observations seriously as scientific data. Again and again the same questions recur. What were the luminous particles, the "fireflies," that John Glenn reported on his Mercury-Atlas 6 flight? What was the luminous band he saw standing above the horizon, and how high was it really? What could astronauts see of the Earth, the airglow, the stars, and artificial lights, and how should later flights be equipped and questioned so that such observations would be more precise? The documents were produced by the Office of Space Sciences at NASA Headquarters and its correspondents, and they show working scientists interviewing astronauts, checking their reports against physics, and recommending instruments, procedures, and organization to do the job better.

## Abstract

This compilation documents how NASA scientists processed the Mercury astronauts' visual observations in 1962 and 1963. It opens with internal memoranda on John Glenn's Mercury-Atlas 6 flight of February 1962, including an interview memorandum concluding that the luminous particles Glenn saw moved with his spacecraft on all three orbits and were therefore associated with the spacecraft or its booster rather than extraterrestrial. A second memorandum, by Maurice Dubin of the Office of Space Sciences, urges that Glenn's horizon observations be captured precisely, since the band he reported might have been the scattering limit of the atmosphere, the airglow, or dust. A Los Alamos analysis addressed to Jocelyn Gill works through the physics of an absorbing layer near one hundred kilometers. A draft scientific note on Scott Carpenter's May 1962 flight measures the luminous band against a reticle and a star, and identifies a strong illusion that exaggerates angles near the horizon. The bulk of the volume is two full debriefing transcripts: Gordon Cooper questioned for fifty pages on the flashing-light experiment, horizon observations, and visual acuity of his 1963 flight, and Walter Schirra questioned on his flight by a panel chaired by Eugene Shoemaker. A press clipping and a reprinted 1955 journal article by Dubin on meteor ionization complete the volume.

## Content Account

The first substantial document is a rough-draft memorandum to the Director of the Office of Space Sciences recording considerations and recommendations following an interview on February 27 with Lieutenant Colonel John H. Glenn, after his Mercury-Atlas 6 flight. Its longest passage concerns the luminous particles. Glenn described their velocity field, their brightness and yellow-green coloring in sunlight, and their shape, consistently across all three orbits. The memorandum reasons that because the particles moved at precisely the same speed and in precisely the same direction as the spacecraft, they cannot have been extraterrestrial; they were associated with the spacecraft itself or shed by the booster. It notes a suggestion attributed to O'Keefe connecting the particles with the life support system, observes that the coloring suggests the particles became fluorescent in sunlight, and remarks that Glenn could see them despite improper dark adaptation, which showed what a properly prepared astronaut might accomplish. The memorandum also takes up Glenn's observations of the star field, the Moon, and the brilliant white, corona-free appearance of the Sun, and his report of a band standing some six to eight degrees above the horizon. Its recommendations are practical: the astronaut-scientist's observations should be published under his own name in a widely distributed scientific journal; additional instruments should be added to the spacecraft within engineering limits; follow-on discussion with Glenn is strongly recommended; proper dark adaptation methods should be built into the spacecraft system; and the physics of the particles, including an "artificial comet" experiment an astronaut might perform, should be investigated. It further recommends a headquarters program chief for scientific exploration in the manned space flight program and a scientific support team at one of the centers.

The second memorandum, dated February 21, 1962 and signed by Maurice Dubin, Head of the Aeronomy Program in the Office of Space Sciences, is titled "Possible Scientific Visual Information Obtained by J. H. Glenn." It identifies two observations reported in the press as being of vital scientific interest, the particles and the extent of the atmosphere visible at the horizon, and works through what Glenn's reported eight-degree limb might mean: he may have seen the limit of the atmosphere by Rayleigh scattering, which would place it above one hundred fifty kilometers; he may have seen the airglow; or he may have seen Mie scattering from dust. It recommends obtaining from Glenn, as soon as possible, the precise lighting conditions, the true limiting angle of his observations, and a graph of the intensity drop-off with angle from the Earth.

Next comes scientific correspondence from the Los Alamos Scientific Laboratory addressed to Dr. Jocelyn Gill, dated July 13, 1962 with a cover letter of July 17, 1962. It is a quantitative treatment, with figures of vertical and horizontal light paths, of an absorbing layer in the region of one hundred kilometers altitude. Working from values reported in the journal Science for the layer Glenn described, it derives attenuation figures for candidate absorbers and discusses nitrogen peroxide in detail as a constituent whose known absorption coefficients might account for what was seen, including whether detectable structure in the absorption should have been visible to the astronaut.

A draft note on the scientific observations follows, centered on Lieutenant Commander Scott Carpenter's flight of May 24, 1962. Toward the end of that flight Carpenter made a timed series of observations of the luminous band around the horizon, measuring its height by several methods, including the passage of the star Phecda and the geometry of a reticle set diagonally. The note records that the band remained visible even against a strong twilight band and argues for attempting to observe it in daylight. It then identifies a strong illusion that exaggerates angles near the horizon, evidently present in Glenn's flight as well, since Glenn also reported seven to eight degrees as the height of the band. The same note examines the particles reported on the Mercury flights: a fiberglass-insulator origin and a Mylar-confetti comparison are considered and set aside on grounds of size and behavior, and the note supports instead the idea that particles lodge on the outside of the capsule and are pushed outward by expanding steam from the capsule before streaming backward. Appended observational material, including temperature records spanning two days, follows in the same section; the extracted text does not make its flight attribution clear.

The longest single component is the scientific debriefing of Gordon Cooper, dated June 1, 1963, following his flight of that year. It runs to fifty numbered pages and proceeds by experiment and topic rather than by chronology. It opens with the flashing-light experiment: Cooper's estimates of the beacon's distance, his first sighting of it as a steady glow lit by the sun, and the panel's checking of the beacon's flash rate against his reports. From there the panel moves to rendezvous lighting, where Cooper judges that a beacon for rendezvous should be of about second or third magnitude and recommends a patterned series of flashing lights; to horizon definition, including examination of his photographs by an MIT participant for the benefit of Apollo guidance; to the airglow layer, which he found sharp at the horizon by day and by night, beneath a bright blue band; to window contamination, a patterned scum on the inside and outside of the window; to thunderstorms, whose lightning lit whole cumulus masses rather than showing distinct patterns; to the lights of cities seen beneath and through cloud layers; and to visual acuity, where Cooper insists he could see remarkably small objects and the panel tests the claim against his experience flying a jet at altitude. He also describes small particles drifting away from the spacecraft over a matter of seconds, determining wind direction from smoke over Tibet, the overlap and tilt of cirrus cloud layers, the sensation of being stationary at the center of a moving field of view, and the kicks in yaw he felt at retrofire.

The second transcript is the scientific debriefing of Lieutenant Commander Walter Schirra, held at NASA Headquarters on Tuesday, March 12, 1963. A transmittal memorandum from J. R. Gill dated November 15, 1963 states that the document is a literal transcription from the tape recording, issued to participants only, for their comments or recommended deletions by December 20, 1963. The session was chaired by Dr. Eugene M. Shoemaker, substituting for the chief scientist, and the participants listed include Jocelyn Gill, Maurice Dubin, John O'Keefe, Larry Dunkelman, and John Nordberg. The questioning begins with the airglow and with Schirra's identification of the planet Mercury and its color. It ranges across star visibility, where Schirra remarks that he could see more stars from an airplane cockpit or from the ground than in orbital flight, qualifying a similar report by Carpenter; the color of the sky, which he describes as going from blue to black, "not an appealing blue by any means"; the smoke-like scum on the window and the multiple reflections it produces, discussed at length with Dunkelman as a problem of instrument design; and meteors, which Dubin asks about directly and which Schirra says neither he, Glenn, nor Carpenter saw, though all were anxious to. The panel also discusses reconstructing his flight path in a planetarium, and Schirra argues for the value of the continuous tape recording in settling questions after the fact.

Two closing components are not debriefing records. The first is a contemporary press article, extracted in garbled two-column form, describing scientific interest in Glenn's fireflies and in the luminous haze belt he reported seven to eight degrees above the horizon over the dark part of the Earth, and noting that the Soviet cosmonaut Gherman Titov reported seeing much the same thing; the article quotes a speaker identified as Douglas. The second is a reprinted journal paper, "Meteor Ionization in the E-region," by Maurice Dubin of the Air Force Cambridge Research Center, from a 1955 symposium supplement to the Journal of Atmospheric and Terrestrial Physics. It reviews the theory of meteoric interaction with the atmosphere, computes the electrons produced per centimeter of path for meteors of given visual magnitude at several heights, and discusses long-duration meteor echoes and the sputtering of surfaces by ion impact. Its presence in the volume is consistent with Dubin's role throughout the compilation as the office's aeronomy specialist and as a questioner in the debriefings.

## Errors and Anomalies

The optical character recognition of this volume is highly uneven, and several distinct anomalies follow from that. The first thirty-three page segments yield almost no extractable text at all; they appear to be image-bearing pages, and their content cannot be identified from the extracted text. One page of the February 21, 1962 Dubin memorandum appears twice, at two points in the volume separated by a NASA routing slip and a government printing card, with the memorandum's second page following the second copy; the duplication is a feature of the compilation as extracted, reported here rather than resolved. The rough-draft Glenn memorandum carries a date line that the extraction renders as January 13, 1962, although its own text describes an interview held on February 27; the date as extracted is internally inconsistent and is most plausibly a misreading, but it has not been corrected here, only flagged. Several pages near the end, apparently handwritten notes, are wholly illegible in extraction. The press clipping asserts that Glenn's maneuvering of his spacecraft proved the luminous particles were independent of the spacecraft; the interview memorandum in this same volume concludes the opposite, that the particles were associated with the spacecraft itself. Those are the claims of two different documents, and this assessment reports the disagreement without adopting either side. Speaker and place names are frequently garbled in the transcripts, and quotations in this assessment have been confined to passages whose wording is legible beyond reasonable doubt.

## Limits

This assessment rests on a structured sample, executed as follows. The opening lines of every one of the 217 page segments were examined, which is how the compilation's component boundaries were mapped. Complete reads were made of the Glenn interview memorandum, the February 21, 1962 Dubin memorandum, the opening of the Cooper debriefing, the Schirra transmittal memorandum and participant list, and the press clipping. The Cooper transcript (fifty pages) and the Schirra transcript (eighty-six or more pages) were read through their segment openings across their whole length, supplemented by fuller reads at their openings and at regular intervals; their exchanges are lengthy and repetitive question-and-answer on individual observations, and fine detail deep inside either transcript, including exact figures in exchanges not sampled, has not been verified. In total roughly 55,000 of the text's 365,376 characters were read closely, in addition to the segment-opening survey of the whole. The thirty-three nearly textless opening segments and the illegible handwritten pages near the end could not be assessed at all, and it is possible that the opening segments contain a further component of the compilation that this assessment does not describe. All factual statements are drawn from the documents themselves or from the item's ledger card; each document's claims are attributed to that document. No external source was consulted, and no claim is made that this compilation is complete, unique, or the earliest record of these debriefings.
