## Abstract

This document is the record of an AI-assisted analysis of a video interview in which Dr. Mary Baxter describes her pendulum practice to Jeffrey Mishlove. As the session reports it, Baxter explains the pendulum through the ideomotor response — subconscious muscle movements that swing it — and describes a disciplined method: beginning by "connecting in light," a prayer or blessing to open oneself to truth; entering a relaxed, meditative state she associates with the theta brainwave frequency; training the nervous system by associating clockwise movement with "yes" and counter-clockwise movement with "no"; asking clear questions with a detached attitude and not repeating the same question; and acting on the guidance received for the highest good of all. She uses the pendulum, on this account, to prioritize tasks, to verify information when reading the Akashic records for a client's soul profile, and to inquire into clients' activities, desires, and possible future events. Her path to the practice ran from an interest in the Akashic records more than twenty years ago, through self-teaching and mixed early results, to a consulting session in which a client's direct question opened the method's everyday use. The session's second half changes register entirely. Prompted by assertions that Baxter violated professional codes, the assistant generates successive lists of potential ethical concerns, an assessment of her competence, and two lists of potential legal issues. These later sections are hypothetical constructions built on the session's premises, heavily repetitive, and are reported here as such.

## Content account

Baxter's method, as the session assembles it from the interview with timestamps, rests on the ideomotor response: she states that the pendulum works by subconscious muscle movements, so the instrument is a readout of the user's own nervous system rather than an external oracle. Preparation has two elements. The practitioner "connects in light," which Baxter describes as a blessing — "it's my way of opening and light" — and which she calls essential: "it's very important always to connect in light to ask for truth." She reports that when she skipped this step in her muscle-testing work, her readings were less effective. The practitioner also enters a relaxed, meditative state, which she associates with the theta frequency, using breath, closed eyes, or a singing bowl.

Training is conditioning. The practitioner holds the pendulum between thumb and forefinger, elbow supported, mentally fixes on "yes" while deliberately swinging the pendulum clockwise, and repeats until the pendulum moves clockwise at the thought of "yes"; the same is done counter-clockwise for "no." Baxter describes this as training "the nervous system to do what you want it to do," and identifies the practitioner's own disbelief — "choking off" the nervous system — as the chief obstacle.

Questioning follows rules the session draws out in several answers: frame questions specifically and without emotional charge; maintain detachment ("The more removed you are from the results, the more truth I think you can receive"); do not ask the same question repeatedly; record the answer; weigh it in the full context of the decision — finances, career, relationships, well-being — rather than obeying it mechanically; and act. Her stated ethical frame is that decisions should serve "the highest good of all," not the practitioner's gain alone.

Applications named in the interview include prioritizing tasks, direct questions from clients, inquiry into what a person wants to be doing and what challenges may lie ahead, muscle testing as a cognate technique, comparison with the I Ching, and verification work in Akashic record readings, where a standard question set builds a soul profile and the pendulum checks each item. Her biography in the interview: she came to the pendulum over twenty years ago through a system for reading the Akashic records, taught herself from online resources with, by her own account, a "bungled" start and mixed results, and expanded the practice after a consulting client asked a direct question. She is the author of a book titled in the interview "Getting Started With Your Pendulum."

The session's latter half addresses ethics and law. Asked whether Baxter asked clients' questions with their permission, the assistant first answers that she works "always with their permission" and emphasizes informed consent and transparency. The questioner then asserts that she violated professional codes, and the assistant produces five categories of potential violation (professional integrity, accurate information, client welfare, confidentiality, conflicts of interest), then, on successive prompts for more, ten further concerns, then eleven, then eight, then nine — forty-three entries in all, with substantial repetition of the same categories (transparency and disclosure, cultural sensitivity, resource management, client safety, and professional boundaries each recur several times). A competence assessment follows, arguing that use of a method lacking scientific evidence raises serious questions about competence and professionalism, while carrying the assistant's own disclaimer that it cannot provide legal opinions. Two lists of potential legal issues follow (twelve, then eleven more): negligence and malpractice, negligent infliction of emotional distress, fraud and misrepresentation, breach of fiduciary duty, privacy violations including HIPAA, licensing violations, false advertising, and practice without a license, among others. A closing answer applies inductive and deductive reasoning to list potential negative outcomes for clients — misdiagnosis, worsened symptoms, unhealthy dependency, financial exploitation, and loss of trust in evidence-based care — while noting that no actual client's experience is documented in the video.
