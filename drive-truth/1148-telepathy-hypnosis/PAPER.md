## Premise

The extract preserves two adjacent parts of a July 1972 United States government assessment of Soviet parapsychology research. The first and longer part, Part D, "Telepathic Hypnosis," surveys reported Soviet work on putting people to sleep and waking them telepathically, and asks why the Soviet Union would pursue telepathic control of consciousness, including its possible use as an instrument of influence and espionage. The second part opens Section II of the parent report, "Conditioning Through Suggestion," with Part A on hypnopedia, or sleep-learning, and the Soviet research on memory and subconscious retention that the report says grew out of it. The extract was produced as intelligence background reading; its consistent method is to relay Soviet claims, largely through the secondary source Ostrander and Schroeder and the report's numbered references, and to mark the military questions those claims raised for its readers.

## Abstract

This three-page extract from the July 1972 government report ST-CS-01-169-72 contains the end of a passage on V. Raikov's work and a device the report calls CCAP, the whole of Part D, "Telepathic Hypnosis," and the opening of Section II, Part A, on hypnopedia. Part D, relying on Ostrander and Schroeder, reports that the ability to put people to sleep and wake them telepathically, over distances from a few yards to over a thousand miles, became a leading topic in international parapsychology; it credits the work's beginnings to K. O. Kotkov of Kharkov University in 1924, describes L. L. Vasilev's documentation of telepathic sleep and waking, and reports E. Naumov's claim that a telepathic "wake up" succeeded in six of eight attempts with a hypnotized subject. It asks why the Soviets pursue telepathic control of consciousness, relays Stefan Manczarski's prediction that telepathy could become a means of spreading propaganda and influencing people, notes reported Soviet use of mescaline and psilocybin in psychic tests, and quotes Milan Ryzl's warning that the Soviet Union could keep such research secret and use extrasensory perception, in his words, "to make money, and as a weapon." The hypnopedia opening summarizes a decade of Soviet sleep-learning literature and a booklet by L. I. Kuproyanovich on improving memory through the subconscious. All statements are the report's, attributed to it here.

## Content Account

The extract begins mid-discussion. Its opening passage closes a part of the parent report concerned with V. Raikov's work and a device rendered in the text as CCAP. The report states that where the Soviets would go with Raikov's work was open to conjecture, that there was some indication the Soviets believed the work and the device might unlock many of the mysteries behind extrasensory perception and other psi phenomena, and that the device might have a much wider use than charting the mental states of the "reincarnated artists" discussed in the preceding part — a subject this extract does not contain.

Part D, "Telepathic Hypnosis," then follows in full. It states, citing Ostrander and Schroeder, that the ability to put people to sleep and wake them up telepathically from a distance of a few yards to over a thousand miles became, in the report's phrasing, the most [discussed topic in] international parapsychology — the sentence is partly garbled in the recognition text and is paraphrased here rather than quoted. It reports that the ability to control a person's consciousness with telepathy was being further studied and tested in laboratories in Leningrad and Moscow; that the work was started in the early 1920s but not publicized until the early 1960s; and that it was begun by K. O. Kotkov, a psychologist from Kharkov University, in 1924, who is said to have been able to telepathically obliterate an experimental subject's consciousness from short distances or from the opposite side of town. The work, the report states, was documented by Vasilev, who conducted research of his own but could not reveal it under Stalin's regime, and whose experiments in mental suggestion it calls backed by columns of data.

The report then describes current work. Parapsychologists in Leningrad and Moscow, it states, were involved in the telepathic manipulation of consciousness and were recording successes with the electroencephalogram. V. Raikov was involved in this research as well as E. Naumov. Naumov is reported to have said that mental telepathy woke a hypnotized subject six of eight times, and that as soon as the telepathic "wake up" is sent, trance becomes less and less deep, with full consciousness returning in twenty to thirty seconds. In the Leningrad laboratory of Paul Gulyaiev, at the Bekhterev Brain Institute, friends of subjects are reported to have been trained to put them to sleep telepathically.

The report next asks its governing question directly: why are the Soviets again hard at work on the telepathic control of consciousness? It suggests that I. Kogan, like Vasilev, was probably pursuing theoretical reasons — still trying mathematically to prove that an electromagnetic carrier of telepathy is possible — but that why other scientists were delving into control of consciousness by extrasensory perception was another question. It notes that during telepathic sleep it is unknown whether an individual is simply dreaming private dreams or whether someone else holds sway, and that the Soviets had not divulged the psychological details of their telepathic manipulation of consciousness; Vasilev described some revelations in his book, but little else had been reported. It relays the prediction of Stefan Manczarski of Poland that this new field of telepathy would open new avenues for spreading propaganda: Manczarski held that the electromagnetic theory is valid and believed telepathy could therefore be amplified like radio waves, becoming a subtle new method for the "influencers" of the world. The report itself remarks that Manczarski's wave ideas were still very debatable, then asks what about telepathy someday becoming a tool for influencing people.

The part continues with cultural and pharmacological context as the report presents it. Hypnotizing someone telepathically, it states, probably comes across as a more eerie, mystifying, almost diabolical act in the United States than in the Soviet Union; the United States was only just becoming adjusted to some aspects of hypnotism, while since the turn of the century the Soviets had been exploring and perfecting its advantages, hypnotism being a common tool there, like X-rays, in medicine, psychotherapy, physiology, psychology, and experimental pedagogy. It reports that the Soviets had been working on the effects of drugs used in combination with psychic tests: Vasilev used mescaline in the early days, and more recently M. S. Smirnov, of the Laboratory of Vision at the Institute of Problems of Information Transmission of the USSR Academy of Sciences, had been obtaining psychic success with psilocybin.

Its closing passage turns to application. The report states that the tests Vasilev perfected might have a more interesting future than he imagined, and that the ability to focus what it calls a mental whammy on an enemy through hypnotic telepathy had surely occurred to the Soviets: in espionage, one could telepathically hypnotize an individual with the post-hypnotic suggestion to steal classified documents or detonate important military equipment, the mission accomplished without the individual even knowing he had done anything. It quotes Ryzl, identified through an appendix reference, stating in Psychic that the bulk of recent telepathy research in the USSR concerned the transmission of behavior impulses, or research to subliminally control an individual's conduct, and recounting that, visiting Soviet psi laboratories in 1967, he was told by a Soviet that with suitable means of propaganda cleverly used it is possible to mold any man's conscience so that he may misuse his abilities while remaining convinced he is serving an honest purpose. Ryzl is quoted as continuing that the USSR had the means to keep the results of such research secret, and as answering the question of what extrasensory perception would be used for with the words: "To make money, and as a weapon."

The extract's final page opens Section II, "Conditioning Through Suggestion," Part A, "Hypnopedia." It states that hypnopedia, or sleep-learning, had been openly discussed in the Soviet literature for the past decade, citing a thorough Soviet report by Bliznichenko in 1966 and a published report by Dodge and Lamont covering Soviet hypnopedia through 1968, and it judges further general treatment redundant apart from trends since 1969. It states that the last decade of Soviet hypnopedia research had led into new concepts of memory improvement, and that areas such as subliminal perception and subconscious learning with hypnosis were believed to have been born from the basic research involved in hypnopedia training. It introduces a booklet by L. I. Kuproyanovich describing equipment and technical means for improving memory and the prospective uses of cybernetics for memory retention, including a discussion of subliminal acquisition of facts — an area the report calls a concern when speaking of conditioned behavior or mental alteration, and one seldom discussed in open Soviet literature. The extract ends as the report begins its summary of Kuproyanovich: that the subconscious is one of the unused reserves for the retention of facts, and that memory operation on the subconscious level takes place without our realizing it.
