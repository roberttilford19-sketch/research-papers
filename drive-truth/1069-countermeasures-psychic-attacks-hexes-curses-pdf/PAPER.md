## Premise

The document is a final report dated January 1982, covering the period October 1980 to September 1981, prepared by SRI International for the Defense Intelligence Agency under contract MDA903-81-C-0292, as SRI Project 3279-3, within the special access programme for Grill Flame. Its authors are G. Scott Hubbard, listed as a consultant, and Edwin C. May. The report was produced to determine, from a search of the pertinent literature, whether countermeasures might exist against psychoenergetic intrusion, and, if so, which of them were most likely to yield results and should be outlined for further investigation. It was originally classified SECRET and marked not releasable to foreign nationals, and it was released in 2000 under CIA-RDP96-00788R001300260001-0.

## Abstract

The report divides psychoenergetic intrusion into two categories: the obtaining of information from a remote location, and the affecting of material objects. It surveys the most recent ten years of the parapsychological literature in five journals, identifying sixty-five papers on remote perturbation other than random-number-generator studies, which together with an earlier survey give a database of approximately one hundred laboratory publications. It arranges the reported phenomena in a hierarchy from data gathering, through perturbation of transient, biological, and stable systems and of static objects, to rare large-scale effects, and observes that credibility, judged by methodological soundness and replicability, runs in the opposite direction to the size of the claimed effect. Its central practical findings are that remote viewing, the best-evidenced form of intrusion in its account, is accompanied by no reported perturbation and therefore admits no physical countermeasure; that almost all reported perturbation occurred over distances of a few metres or less, often with the subject touching the object, so that distance is the simplest countermeasure; and that some reported effects might be explained by unusual but ordinary human abilities, against which standard shielding would serve. It recommends a limited hardware programme using a random number generator, temperature sensing elements, and film detectors.

## Content Account

### Objective and approach

The introduction explains that countermeasures, if they exist, would be accomplished by physical or by mental processes. Mental countermeasures are set aside as difficult to define and still more difficult to investigate; the report notes that, like jamming a radar signal, a mental countermeasure would most likely use the same process as the intrusion, and it gives "psychic attack" and "hexes" from the occult literature as examples of potential mental countermeasures. The report focuses on physical countermeasures. Its reasoning is sequential: to judge the feasibility of a physical countermeasure device, it must first assess whether simple intrusion detection is possible, since the mechanism underlying such a detector would then serve as the basis for developing a countermeasure device.

The survey was deliberately bounded. The authors limited the period surveyed to the most recent ten years in order to keep the threat assessment relevant to modern facilities and to make it chronologically compatible with a previously completed survey of random-number-generator remote perturbation. The five journals surveyed are named in the report: the Journal of Parapsychology, the Journal of the American Society for Psychical Research, the Journal of the Society for Psychical Research, the European Journal of Parapsychology, and Research in Parapsychology.

### The hierarchy of intrusion

Table 1 of the report arranges the material in three tiers. The first tier is data gathering with minimal effect, comprising remote viewing, out-of-body experiences, and psychoenergetic data selection with pseudorandom and true random number generators. The second tier is perturbation with some physical effect, divided into transient systems such as dice throwing, random number generators, thoughtography, and a spinning coin; biological systems from paramecia and small animals to human physiology and movement; stable systems including strain gauges, thermometry, magnetometers, and bubble chambers; and static objects, including metal bending, compass needle deflection, moving plastic tubes and small jars, and table levitation. The third tier is unusual or rare remote perturbation with large-scale effect, comprising materialisations and large-scale levitations, with D. D. Home named as an example.

The report states the governing caution explicitly. Most of the papers claiming evidence of physical effects are not subject to evaluation, because the descriptions of the experiments are incomplete in one or more aspects; to consider the possibility of physical countermeasures at all, the authors are forced to assume that all reports are true, and to exercise caution in drawing conclusions from the database. That assumption is the report's own, stated on its face, and it conditions everything that follows.

### Findings

On data gathering, the report finds what it calls ample evidence in both the open and the classified literature that remote viewing may be a valuable information-gathering technology, and states that because no reported perturbation occurs at the time of remote-viewing data acquisition, it is unable to recommend a physical countermeasure against it. It notes evidence from two out-of-body studies suggesting that a concomitant remote perturbation may be detectable at the time of the experience, and that whether remote viewing produces similar detectable effects remains to be determined.

On perturbation, the report's most concrete observation is about distance. Virtually none of the papers discussed effects at distances greater than a few metres, and almost all of the reported metal bending occurred with the subject actually touching the object. The authors therefore suggest that the simplest form of countermeasure in these cases may be distance between sensitive equipment and a putative remote-perturbation agent. They add that some forms of remote perturbation may be accomplished by unusual but not psychoenergetic human abilities, instancing the suggestion that effects on static objects might be explained by a subject's ability to generate electric fields, and that in such cases standard shielding techniques would serve as an adequate countermeasure.

### Theoretical considerations and recommendations

The report's theoretical chapter states that understanding of psychoenergetic processes is still in its infancy and that, with one possible exception, all suggested mechanisms must be categorised as plausibility arguments. Table 2 lists six: an extremely low frequency electromagnetic mechanism, for which standard shielding procedures are the possible countermeasure; the EPR paradox; hypergeometry, in which the distance between points in ordinary space vanishes in hyperspace; general quantum mechanics and the collapse of the wave function; advanced waves propagating backward in time; and other exceptional but non-psychoenergetic human functioning, for which standard physical shielding techniques are listed. For the middle four, the table gives the possible countermeasure as unknown, whether physical or mental.

The recommendations follow from design constraints the report states: devices must be chosen that have demonstrated susceptibility to remote perturbation, must be sensitive yet isolated from the environment, must keep engineering to a minimum, and the approach should be systematic and follow earlier reported efforts. Three types of hardware are proposed for study as an initial step toward intrusion detection and physical countermeasures: a random number generator device, requiring a modest redesign of an existing device; temperature sensing elements; and film detectors. The remainder of the report is a bibliography by category, covering thoughtography, the Raudive voices, miscellaneous remote-perturbation experiments, remote perturbation on biological systems, and remote perturbation of stable systems including bubble chambers, temperature measurement, metal bending with strain gauges, and magnetometers.

The filename's terms do appear in the report, but in a limited place: "psychic attack" and "hexes" are given in the introduction as examples, drawn from the occult literature, of potential mental countermeasures, a category the report expressly sets aside in order to focus on physical countermeasures. These annotations are part of the released scan. Their author is not identified in the document, the handwriting is only partly legible in the recognised text, and this assessment records their presence without attributing them to any person or treating them as part of the report's findings.
