## Abstract

This paper, by Nai Chi-Jing of the Wuhu Municipal College of Chinese Medicine, in an English translation issued as document IN734-85, reports an experiment on what the author calls the psychic magnification effect. Five sensitives, all men between thirty-two and fifty-eight with chronic diseases, who had previously received acupuncture treatment, were needled at acupuncture points and asked to concentrate on the part of the body being tested; five sensitives who had never undergone acupuncture served as a control group. The author reports that the treated subjects described their internal tissue structures as magnified, comparing a Krause globule to a mung bean, a Meissner touch sensor to a soybean, a hair-follicle receptor to a sesame seed, and a nerve cell to a red bean, and that they could describe the location, size, and number of nerve clusters in the cerebellum. No such effect is reported for the control group. The author argues that the agreement among the five subjects' descriptions excludes prompting and fabrication, that the effect requires prior acupuncture experience together with intense, undistracted concentration, that it persists while the needle remains in place and fades as needle sensation fades, and that it is best explained through the channel system and the brain's role in selectively concentrating on a single stimulus.

## Content Account

The paper begins from the author's earlier reports. Certain psychics, when triggered by acupuncture, could magnify the cells at an acupuncture point to the size of cherries, feel the three-dimensional structure of a line, determine whether a cell membrane existed, and watch material exchange into and out of cells. The present experiment was designed to explore that claim systematically.

The method is described in some detail. The experimental group was five sensitives, all male, aged thirty-two to fifty-eight, all with chronic diseases, all meeting criteria the author attributes to a 1975 national conference on sensitives, and all having already had two to three courses of acupuncture treatment, each course ten days with three days between courses. Two had a general knowledge of anatomy and physiology, a fact the author reports without treating it as a difficulty. The control group was five sensitives, three male and two female, aged twenty-one to forty-three, who had never undergone acupuncture. Subjects were seated. A number thirty acupuncture needle, half an inch or one and a half inches, was inserted with high-speed shallow piercing at internal-organ or other acupuncture points, twisted by hand, and left in place; when the sensation weakened, the needle was turned again. Subjects were prepared beforehand to ensure cooperation, rested in a quiet place, and instructed to concentrate all thought on the part of the body being tested, and all reactions were, the author says, objectively recorded.

The reported results are those summarized in the abstract. Under acupuncture, subjects described internal structures at enlarged scales, with sizes consistently expressed by comparison to seeds and beans, and described the location, size, and number of nerve clusters in the cerebellum. The phenomenon could be produced from various acupuncture points and did not occur in the control group. The author draws three conclusions from this. First, the effect is real: he argues that the subjects affected him with their descriptions while he did not affect them, which he takes to eliminate prompting, and that the common characteristics of five independent descriptions will not support fabrication. Second, the effect has necessary conditions: prior acupuncture experience and intense concentration on the tested part, for which he quotes Ming dynasty and other classical sources on gathering thought to one place; when attention was diverted, the magnified structure disappeared. Third, the effect has a material basis: it continues while the needle remains, fades as needle sensation fades, returns when the needle is turned, and is not clearly manifested until the sensation transmitted along the channels reaches the part being concentrated on. From this he argues for an explanation in terms of the channel control system together with the brain, which he describes as a receiving unit with an amplification function, drawing on the traditional statements that energy in the head stops with the brain and exits through the brain.

The paper's evidentiary character should be stated plainly. Its results are the subjects' verbal descriptions, reported by the author; no independent anatomical verification of any description is presented, no measurements by instruments are reported, and the control comparison is between subjects who differed in acupuncture history, which is the very variable the author treats as the condition of the effect. The paper is therefore an author's report and interpretation of his subjects' statements, and this package presents it as exactly that.
