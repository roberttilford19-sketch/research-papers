## Premise

The file holds pages from a parapsychology journal containing Sybo A. Schouten's paper “A Test of Heymans' Theory on Paranormal Phenomena,” from the Parapsychology Laboratory of the University of Utrecht. Gerard Heymans, the Dutch philosopher and psychologist, had built a theory of paranormal phenomena on psychic monism, the view that reality is fundamentally mental. Schouten sets out to explain the theory, derive its predictions about telepathy, survival, and ghost experiences, and then test one central prediction: that telepathic success should be higher when the target is an experience the target person shared with the percipient, because shared experience creates the associative connections the theory requires.

## Abstract

Sybo Schouten expounds Gerard Heymans' theory of paranormal phenomena and reports a free-response experiment designed to test it. Heymans, following Fechner, held a position Schouten calls psychic monism: reality is basically mental, individual consciousness is part of a wider world-mind, and mental structures in what Heymans called peripheral consciousness are linked by associations formed through experience. On this account telepathy occurs when an emotionally important mental complex in one person reaches another person's consciousness through shared associative connections, which yields testable predictions. Telepathic experiences should be rare, should occur most readily when sensory stimulation is weak, should be more common between people who are related or who share experiences, should concern important and serious events disproportionately often, and should arrive with the character of a vague memory rather than a perception. The theory also explains apparent survival and ghost experiences as access to the weakening mental structure of a recently deceased person. Schouten tested the association prediction with twenty-one pairs of related subjects, using as targets either important experiences the target person had shared with the percipient or important events in the target person's life that had not been shared. The paper reports the result plainly: no extrasensory perception was observed in the data, and therefore no conclusion could be drawn about the importance of associations for ESP. The theoretical exposition, not the experiment, is the paper's substance.

## Content account

The pages contain more than Schouten's paper. At the top of the first page is the closing portion of a preceding article about observational theory in parapsychology, arguing that correlations between subject traits and ESP scoring might be produced by the observer or checker rather than the subject, and reporting that the study it concludes offered no result favoring either the traditional or the observational paradigm. At the end of the second page begins a further article, James C. Carpenter's exploration of quasi-therapeutic group process as a vehicle for facilitating ESP, describing a twelve-trial pilot series. Schouten's paper sits between these, on journal pages 61 to 63 as printed, and only Schouten's paper is the subject of this assessment.

Schouten introduces Heymans as the founder of the scientific and experimental approach in parapsychology in the Netherlands, internationally best known for the telepathy experiment with the subject van Dam carried out with his assistants Brugmans and Weinberg at Groningen, and notes that his theoretical views are less well known. The theory section explains psychic monism: all phenomena are basically mental, the world-mind contains all the mental as space contains physical matter, and each individual's consciousness, divided into present awareness and a peripheral consciousness of memories and knowledge organized into complexes of related images, is part of that whole. Images compete to enter awareness under the laws of association, with perceptions, recent events, strongly associated images, and strong emotions favored, and weak images reaching consciousness mainly under favorable conditions such as the absence of sensory stimulation.

From this the paper derives the predictions summarized in the abstract, including the account of ghosts: a deceased person's mental structure stops developing and gradually weakens, so a living person might by association gain access to it shortly after death, creating an impression of survival and communication, while a ghost, being only the representation of that structure as it was, could only behave passively and could not converse. The test then operationalized the association concept. Two sets of targets were used with twenty-one pairs of related subjects: important experiences shared between target person and percipient, and important events in the target person's life not shared with the percipient. Heymans' theory predicts higher scoring in the shared condition. The paper's report of the outcome is one sentence in substance: no ESP was observed in the data, and hence no conclusion could be drawn regarding the importance of the concept of associations for ESP. The theory therefore survived its test untested, a result the paper states without embellishment.
