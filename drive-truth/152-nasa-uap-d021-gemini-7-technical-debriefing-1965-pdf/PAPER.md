## Premise

The document is the preliminary transcript of the Gemini VII flight crew's technical debriefing, conducted from December 19 through December 21, 1965, at Crew Quarters, Cape Kennedy, Florida, immediately after the fourteen-day flight. Its purpose is to place the crew's account of the mission — systems performance, the rendezvous with Gemini VI, the experiment program, and the training that preceded the flight — on the record from the voice tapes, section by section, for the use of the program. The transcript runs to 208 printed pages and fifteen sections, from countdown to concluding comments.

## Abstract

The transcript records command pilot Frank Borman and pilot James Lovell describing the fourteen-day Gemini VII mission to a representative of the Flight Crew Support Division, in a document dated December 23, 1965 and described in its own preface as a rough-edited preliminary transcript. The crew reports windows that were clean at launch but filmed over in flight, difficult station-keeping with a venting and bending spent booster, and a first orbit-adjust burn that overshot its target. The defining operational decision of the flight, in Borman's stated judgment, was flying without pressure suits for most of the mission, which he called the best decision of the flight. Systems problems included yaw thrusters that lost authority, fuel-cell stacks that failed in the second week, and a water boiler whose venting repeatedly disturbed the spacecraft's attitude and once froze. The rendezvous with Gemini VI is described as wholly dependent on sun angle for visibility. The experiment program was limited chiefly by weather, fuel, and two equipment failures, while the crew praised the D-4/D-7 experiment as the most valuable flown. The debriefing closes with detailed criticism of simulator coordination and a strong endorsement of the support organization behind the flight.

### Flight narrative and spacecraft systems

The crew reports that the windows were clean at launch — heated, a fix the transcript credits to the problems experienced on Gemini V — but became dirty in flight under a greenish, greasy film, worse on Lovell's side than on Borman's. Station-keeping with the spent Stage II booster was, in the crew's account, an order of magnitude harder than with a stable vehicle, because the booster was venting and bending; its four lights were judged useless for estimating distance without illumination. The first perigee adjust maneuver overshot, reaching a perigee of about 117 to 120 miles against about 102 planned, after a trailing primer cord or wire slapped the spacecraft during the burn.

The crew began suitless operation at about 45 hours into the flight (Lovell) and both were out of their suits by 191 hours 48 minutes. Borman called the decision "the best decision in the whole flight" and suitless operation "1,000 per cent better"; those are his words in the transcript and are reported as such. On thrusters, Nos. 3 and 4 (yaw right) lost authority: the crew heard solenoid clicks and a "whishing" sound without ignition, and the transcript records ground analysis suggesting valve seats as the cause. On fuel cells, Section 1 performed ideally throughout, while Section 2 stacks 2A and 2C were lost about day 11 to 12, leaving 2B; the crew judged the delta-P caution lights unreliable after operating and purging with the lights on under ground guidance. The water boiler's venting produced recurring left yaw and left roll, with rates the crew measured at about 7 degrees per second on day 13, and two thruster pulses fired at every ACME power-down. A frozen water boiler at about 266 hours 16 minutes was cleared by radiator bypass, evaporator heat, and a 10-degree-per-second roll, leaving an ice glob about ten inches across at the vent.

For the rendezvous, the crew reports acquiring Gemini VI at about 5 miles in the final phase and finding visibility wholly dependent on sun angle. During reentry, the ground updated the backup bank angle from 50 degrees to 35 degrees after retrofire tracking, a change Borman credited with avoiding a large landing shortfall. Maximum reentry acceleration was 3.9 g. The crew disagreed on the size of the drogue parachute oscillations, estimating them at more than 20 degrees. When the snorkel opened at 26,000 feet, it drew in a contaminant that burned the crew's eyes; the crew did not identify it. The spacecraft landed about 8 to 9 miles from the carrier. The crew also remarked that the digital clock never varied by a second in 339 hours.

### Experiments

Weather was the dominant limit. Of nine logged Apollo landmark sequences, most failed because of cloud, and only one primary target was photographed. The D-5 star occultation equipment never worked: its reticle would not change from red to green, and the crew learned only in flight of a possible radio-frequency sensitivity. The D-9 sextant had a 20-to-80 light split rather than the 50-to-50 split the crew had trained with, and its overly bright white angle lighting destroyed dark adaptation; in the later sextant discussion the crew adds that the green and blue filters erased the horizon entirely, that no usable horizon reference independent of the Moon existed, and that stars could never be observed in daylight despite repeated attempts. The M-9 visual acuity work went well, although the crew questioned whether the head brace and bite board were necessary. The S-5 and S-6 photography programs completed everything called up, plus uncalled sequences over Brazil, Mexico, North Africa, and Cuba, although window film degraded the results. The MSC-4 optical communications experiment was limited by telescope light gathering and a green filter that faded out the terrain features needed for acquisition. The M-1 cardiovascular experiment ran continuously for fourteen days; the crew called it a waste of time, although they kept it running so the experiment could not be called invalid. The M-8 sleep analysis headgear proved impractical: Borman tore off all four leads removing the helmet after two days. Urine collection drew the crew's sharpest equipment criticism, and they recommended a simple overboard relief tube instead. The crew counted meteors on dedicated night passes without seeing the expected Gemini shower, observed a brilliant southern aurora over Australia, counted 206 discrete lightning flashes in a single pass, and praised D-4/D-7 as the most valuable experiment flown, citing equipment worth about $3.5 million in Borman's figure.

### Planning, mission control, and training

The crew states that adding the Gemini VI rendezvous to the mission without reducing the experiment program cost results, because about 110 pounds of propellant went to the rendezvous; Borman asked that this be put on the record. They praised real-time flight planning and the flexibility of the mission rules, and they objected to ground requests that spent scarce fuel on systems exploration, including a proposed three-second thruster burst and a suggestion to fire the OAMS squib to see whether it could be heard; Borman refused both, and the ground accepted the refusals. Training sections record 126.5 hours of systems briefings and 200.5 hours on experiments, high praise for the McDonnell engineering simulator week and for the scheduling work of Mike Brzezinski, and blunt criticism of the simulator network sessions between Houston and the Cape as nearly a total waste of time because of interface failures. In concluding comments the crew praised the Flight Crew Support Division task-force organization and the responsiveness of the configuration control process, and advised future crews to attend management meetings and to engage the flight-plan process early.
