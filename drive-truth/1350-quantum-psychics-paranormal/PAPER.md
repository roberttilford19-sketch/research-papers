## Premise

The report examines whether quantum physics, whose accepted features already depart from common sense, can supply a physical basis for parapsychological phenomena, whose features also depart from common sense. It does so by attending a conference convened on exactly that question, studying the written papers, and judging each contribution by whether it offers a testable physical idea or only a philosophical one.

## Abstract

This report, dated 28 October 1974, covers the twenty-third annual international conference of the Parapsychology Foundation, held at the Hotel La Reserve in Geneva on 26 and 27 August 1974 on the subject "Quantum Physics and Parapsychology," a topic chosen at the instigation of Arthur Koestler. Ten papers were presented. The report's author states at the outset that the reality of extrasensory perception was an accepted fact among those present, that discussion of it concerned only how to secure public acceptance, and that no adequate theory of paranormal perception existed which could furnish a physical basis for the phenomena. The report divides the participants into two broad schools, those who expected progress from extending existing scientific methods and those who favoured a philosophical or mystical approach requiring a revolution in thought, and it confines detailed discussion to the first group. It judges the paper by O. Costa de Beauregard, linking information theory to entropy, causality, and advanced and retarded waves, to be the most promising point of departure, and the detailed theory of Evan Harris Walker, resting on hidden variables in quantum theory, to be the most explicit and quantitative while resting on unsubstantiated assumptions. An appendix summarises and criticises each paper in turn.

## Content account

The general conclusions are the report's own, and they are more restrained than the conference's shared assumption might suggest. Knowledge of normal perception and of neural data processing in the brain is, the report argues, still limited, so a physical theory of paranormal perception would not by itself produce understanding; advances in cybernetics and neurophysiology would also be needed. Empirical work should not wait for theory. The report observes that a phenomenon can be used effectively, or its use evaluated, without deep understanding of its nature, as fire was used for thousands of years without chemistry, and it calls for the identification and quantification of the physical and psychological conditions that inhibit or enhance the effects. It then raises what it treats as the fundamental practical problem. Information obtained by paranormal means is incomplete, subject to error and distortion, and sometimes completely erroneous, while a percipient may be unable to estimate his or her own accuracy reliably. Unless output can be assessed for completeness and reliability, the report argues, such information cannot serve a useful function.

The appendix gives the substance of the individual papers. Walker's theory proposes computed data rates for subconscious, conscious, and attention processes in the brain, and assigns to so-called hidden variables the role of fixing the outcome of quantum events identically for all observers, which would provide a communication channel independent of space and time. The critique replies that hidden variables form no part of accepted physical theory, that no compelling evidence requires them unless psi phenomena themselves are taken as that evidence, that the claim that nerve synapse triggering is a quantum process requires more substantiation, and that the theory has not yet predicted anything. Gerald Feinberg's paper proposes that precognition is a memory of a future state of the percipient's own mind, by analogy with time-advanced solutions in electromagnetic theory; the critique calls it basically a trivial paper containing one idea. C. T. K. Chari's paper, read in his absence by Harold Puthoff, postulates a fourth, non-Boolean information network, and is judged to offer no theory clear enough for experimental verification. Costa de Beauregard's paper receives the fullest approval, because it stays close to established physical understanding, supplies a framework rather than a mechanism, and places the phenomena in the general context of information theory. V. A. Firsoff's paper is described as a philosophical essay expressing awe rather than testable hypotheses. The two experimental papers by Harold Puthoff and Russell Targ, on psychokinesis experiments with physical instruments and on remote viewing of natural targets, are summarised briefly, with the critique noting that belief in the results depends on confidence in the integrity and skill of the experimenters. J. H. M. Whiteman's paper is recorded as unintelligible to the report's author. Helmut Schmidt's paper proposes an empirical measure, a psi source strength applied as a multiplicative factor to ratios of prior probabilities, and is judged valuable for testing the logical consistency of models. Ted Bastin's paper is judged too confusing to evaluate effectively, with its relation to parapsychology described as extremely tenuous, although a note records a circulated paper on experiments with Uri Geller. The conference programme and participant list close the document.

## Errors and anomalies

The Drive filename reads "Quantum Psychics," where the document's title is "Quantum Physics." This assessment follows the document and records the discrepancy, because the difference changes what a reader would expect to find. The OCR text is noisy throughout, with frequent misreadings of ordinary words and several mangled passages in the programme pages, but the body of the report is legible and its argument is continuous. Names are misread in places, including those of several participants, and have been given here in the forms the document's own participant list supports. One internal feature should be noted as a matter of method rather than error. The report evaluates a conference at which the existence of the phenomena was treated as settled. Its judgements are therefore judgements about proposed explanations, made from inside that assumption, and they should not be read as an independent assessment of whether the phenomena occur.
