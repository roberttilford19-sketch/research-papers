## Premise

This file assembles Central Intelligence Agency papers from September and October 1952 in which the Intelligence Advisory Committee and the Office of Scientific Intelligence considered whether unidentified flying objects posed a national security problem and what research should follow.

## Abstract

This staged assessment describes a collected Central Intelligence Agency file from 1952, released under the reference CIA-RDP81R00560R000100020012-7, concerning unidentified flying objects, then commonly called flying saucers. The file contains a draft cover sheet for the Intelligence Advisory Committee, a draft letter to Secretary Lovett, and memoranda from the Office of Scientific Intelligence dated September and October 1952. Together the papers ask three questions: whether the sightings carry national security implications, whether the study then under way is adequate, and what further research should be instituted, by whom, and under what authority. The documents record that the Air Technical Intelligence Center held primary responsibility, that about fifteen hundred official reports had been received since 1947, and that a substantial minority remained unexplained in the Air Force accounting quoted here. The writers' concerns are practical rather than speculative. They worry that unidentified reports could mask an air attack, that public credulity could be exploited in psychological warfare, that Soviet silence in print is itself a datum, and that poorly understood atmospheric phenomena may explain many reports. Their recommendation is an expanded, coordinated scientific research programme conducted alongside intelligence research. This assessment reports the file's contents and reasoning without adopting its period assumptions or its unexplained residue as established fact.

## Content Account

The file opens with a draft Intelligence Advisory Committee cover sheet recommending concurrence in a proposed letter to Secretary Lovett and asking the Watch Committee to attend to indications of Soviet action connected with flying saucers or with American public opinion about them. The draft letter argues that intelligence cannot estimate an enemy's ability to create and use such phenomena until scientific research establishes whether they can be generated and controlled by human beings. The longer Office of Scientific Intelligence memorandum of 2 October 1952, from the Assistant Director, Office of Scientific Intelligence, states the problem formally, reviews the Air Technical Intelligence Center's case-by-case work and its arrangement with Battelle Memorial Institute for machine indexing of reports, and recommends improved identification of visual and electronic phantoms, study of possible psychological warfare uses, and a national policy on what the public should be told. Briefing notes dated from August 1952 record the Air Force position quoted in the file: that most sightings were explained as known objects or atmospheric phenomena, a small share were hoaxes, and about twenty percent remained unexplained. A closing scientific discussion considers atmospheric electricity, auroral and refractive effects, radiation, and other poorly understood phenomena as candidate explanations, while conceding that some reports from credible observers resisted the explanations then available.
