## Abstract

This file is a nine-page clipping bundle assembled around Russell Targ and Harold Puthoff's 1977 book Mind-Reach. Its principal item is Robert Ornstein's review in the New York Times Book Review, which argues that the book is pleasantly written but slim in hard evidence: the experiments it reports, he writes, provide very little evidence, in the book or in published journals, that they can be repeated, and the authors habitually claim more than their preliminary results support. Also present are a congratulatory letter of 1 January 1977 from Gertrude Schmeidler, Charles Tart's favorable review, Library Journal review pages that include a second, favorable notice of the book, and a letter from Charles Panati praising it. The bundle closes with Jeffrey Klein's review of Ornstein's own book The Mind Field, which criticizes Ornstein's scolding tone toward the human potential movement, an inclusion that gives the file a critical voice on both sides. Much of the later material survives only in degraded form, with several pages reduced to scattered fragments. The file is a reception record for the book, not the book itself, and its contents are the reviewers' and correspondents' opinions, attributed to them throughout.

## Content Account

The first page is legible and complete, and it carries the bundle's center of gravity: Robert Ornstein's review, "A Case for Parapsychology," of Mind-Reach, whose bibliographic heading the review gives as 230 pages, published by G. P. Putnam's Sons at $8.95, with the reviewer identified as teaching at the University of California Medical Center and directing a forthcoming magazine. Ornstein's argument has three parts. First, he holds that parapsychology's marginal standing is partly self-inflicted: its publicizers counter skepticism by proclamation, claiming a significant body of incontrovertible evidence and attributing disbelief to prejudice, and Targ and Puthoff, he writes, are no exception. Second, he applies the standard of repeatability. The experiments the book reports — remote viewing of places visited by others, guessing target drawings, attempts to influence a machine's random events, and sittings with Uri Geller — are, he writes, reported as successes by the authors, but they provide very little evidence either in the book or in published journals that any of them can be repeated by anyone else. He invokes William James's white crow and answers it directly: one bit of evidence ought to be enough, but in practice a single unrepeated instance can be dismissed as statistically insignificant, and much more evidence is needed to overthrow an accepted world-view. He adds a personal datum: he had himself attempted, in collaboration with the authors, with the same subject they used and their full cooperation, to repeat one of their experiments published in Nature, and was unable to do so. Third, he judges the book against its own stated hope that parapsychology become a firmly grounded science: in their writing, he concludes, the authors almost always go beyond the evidence and claim to have demonstrated what they have not, and have thereby done more harm, perhaps, than help to their cause and their field. His summary verdict is that the book is slim in hard evidence, pleasantly written, and amusing to follow, and that its publisher's claim to have documented that some degree of psychic ability is universal is unjustified propagandizing. The review continues to a later page, as its own footnote states, but that continuation does not survive in legible form in this file.

The remaining pages are a mixed reception record, in markedly worse condition. A letter dated 1 January 1977, on City College of the City University of New York psychology department letterhead and signed by Gertrude Schmeidler, thanks the authors for her copy, congratulates them on a beautifully written book she finished in a single sitting, and tells them she revised the galleys of her own handbook manuscript to give the book two citations. A review attributed in the file to Charles Tart takes the opposite side from Ornstein: it finds the researchers' competent technique, impeccable integrity, and obviously positive results to provide, in its words, unquestionably powerful evidence for the reality of parasensory perception, and recommends the book highly. Library Journal review pages follow, dense with notices of unrelated books; among them, two notices of Mind-Reach itself are legible in substance, both favorable, describing the Stanford Research Institute experiments, the participation of Uri Geller and Ingo Swann alongside ordinary volunteers, the transcripts and juxtaposed target photographs the book provides, and Margaret Mead's introduction, and recommending the book to readers. A short letter from Charles Panati praises the book as wonderful and soberly presented, singles out its chapter on the loyal opposition, and thanks the authors for mentioning his own Geller papers. The bundle's final legible item turns the critical apparatus on the reviewer: Jeffrey Klein's review of Ornstein's own book The Mind Field, in Mother Jones, which grants Ornstein's eminence but finds the book's scolding tone toward the human potential movement unpleasant, its map of the field flat and without political dimension, and its advice reducible to a recommendation of his earlier work. Everything in this account is the opinion of the reviewer or correspondent named, reported as the file presents it; this assessment adopts none of it, about the book or about its reviewer.
