## Premise

The report was produced to answer the National Research Council's 1988 assessment of parapsychology, published within *Enhancing Human Performance*, a study commissioned by the United States Army. The NRC committee had concluded that there was no scientific justification, from research conducted over a period of 130 years, for the existence of parapsychological phenomena. Palmer, Honorton, and Utts set out to show that this conclusion was unwarranted: that the committee's review was narrow in scope, that its two principal evaluators were publicly committed against parapsychology before the review began, that its treatment of the ganzfeld and random-number-generator evidence was selective, and that its overall verdict discouraged future research on the basis of a fraction of the field's work.

## Abstract

This report is the Parapsychological Association's reply to the National Research Council's 1988 assessment of parapsychology, prepared for the Association's Board of Directors by John A. Palmer, Charles Honorton, and statistician Jessica Utts. The authors argue that although the NRC report is couched in scientific language, it is not an unbiased scientific assessment. They note that its single chapter on parapsychology is restricted to four selected research areas from the past twenty years — less than ten percent of the systematic scientific effort in the field, on their account — while its headline conclusion invokes 130 years of research. They argue that the committee's two principal evaluators, Ray Hyman and James Alcock, were publicly committed to a negative position and were members of the Executive Council of an organization known for its campaign against parapsychology, with no balancing appointment. They contend that the committee violated its own evaluation guidelines by conceding it had neither a "smoking gun" nor a demonstrated plausible alternative for the better experiments, and that it selectively omitted favorable findings from a background paper by an eminent Harvard psychologist whom the committee chairman had asked to withdraw his favorable conclusions. The body of the report re-examines the ganzfeld database, the random-number-generator meta-analysis of Radin and Nelson, and the committee's treatment of Backster's "primary perception" work, arguing in each case that the statistical results are robust to the removal of individual investigators' studies and unrelated to study quality. ## Content Account

The report opens by setting the scene: on 3 December 1987 the National Research Council convened a well-attended press conference in Washington to announce *Enhancing Human Performance* (Druckman and Swets, 1988), the product of a study commissioned by the United States Army to assess techniques of enhancing human performance, of which parapsychology was one of six areas. Committee chairman John A. Swets stated that "perhaps our strongest conclusions are in the area of parapsychology," and the report's conclusion — quoted by the authors — is that "the Committee finds no scientific justification from research conducted over a period of 130 years for the existence of parapsychological phenomena."

Against this, the Overview advances the reply's charges in compact form. The chapter on parapsychology is restricted to four selected areas of research conducted during the past twenty years; the scope of the review is, the authors state, limited to less than ten percent of the systematic scientific effort in parapsychology, which began with J. B. Rhine's Duke University work in the 1930s, and no explanation is provided for the purported 130-year history the conclusion invokes. The two principal evaluators, Ray Hyman and James Alcock, were publicly committed to a negative position at the time the committee was formed, and both are identified as members of the Executive Council of an organization the authors describe as well known for its zealous campaign against parapsychology; no attempt was made to balance the committee with scientists holding a more positive or neutral position. Even within its limited scope, the authors argue, the committee's method violates its own stated guidelines for research evaluation, which specify the identification and assessment of plausible alternatives: regarding the better parapsychological experiments, the committee admits, in a passage the authors quote, "We do not have a smoking gun, nor have we demonstrated a plausible alternative." The report is also charged with selectively omitting important findings favorable to parapsychology from one commissioned background paper while liberally citing others supportive of its position; the principal author of the favorable paper, described as an eminent Harvard psychologist, was actually asked by the committee chairman to withdraw his favorable conclusions. The authors conclude the Overview by arguing that the committee's conclusion far outstrips the scope of its investigation, implies that parapsychology has had its day and failed, and so discourages future research and funding.

The contents page shows the report's structure: an anatomy of prejudgment examining the composition of the NRC parapsychology subcommittee; evaluation issues; a treatment of the scientific evidence in three parts — psi ganzfeld research, random number generator experiments, and Backster's "primary perception"; potential applications; qualitative evidence and subjective bias; conclusions; references; and an appendix on the committee's response to the Harris and Rosenthal postscript, which became available after the main report was completed.

The random-number-generator section illustrates the authors' method. RNG research, they explain, involves attempts by subjects to bias the normally random output of an electronic device solely by intention, with randomness typically based on radioactive decay, electronic diode noise, or pseudorandom sources, and with controls including automated recording, trial-by-trial oscillation of the target definition, alternation of high and low aims, and control runs without influence attempts. They cite the meta-analytic review by Radin and Nelson (1987), encompassing all known RNG studies between 1959 and 1987 — over 800 experimental and control studies by 68 investigators — in which the overall z-score for 597 experimental series was 15.76 while 235 control series yielded −0.67, within chance fluctuation; accounting for the experimental result by selective reporting would require, on the analysis they cite, more than 50,000 unreported chance-level studies. The authors note that the NRC committee concedes the RNG research cannot reasonably be explained by chance, and admits that critics have not specified any plausible mechanism accounting for the difference between experimental and control trials. They fault the committee for discussing only the work of Helmut Schmidt and Robert Jahn, the two largest contributors, when the result does not depend on them: removing Schmidt and Jahn leaves an overall z-score of 5.48 for the remaining 66 investigators. They report that Radin and Nelson assigned each study a quality weight from sixteen criteria derived in part from published criticisms by Hyman and Alcock themselves, across RNG integrity, data integrity, statistical integrity, and procedural integrity, and found no significant relationship between research quality and study outcome, with unweighted and quality-weighted effect sizes nearly identical. Two committee arguments are answered in detail. An unnamed physicist's suggestion that the human body might act as an antenna and bias RNG output is criticized as undocumented, never followed up despite its potential relevance to the Army's interest in human interaction with delicate electronics, and irrelevant to studies using prerecorded targets, pseudorandom sources, or trial-by-trial oscillation of the target definition. And the committee's downgrading of a favorable experiment by May, Humphrey, and Hubbard (1980) for its "marginal" significance (p = .029) is called completely spurious, because that experiment used a standard sequential sampling technique designed to stop at a preset significance level in as few trials as possible — a design feature specified in advance — so that judging it by the p-value standards of a fixed-trial experiment misconstrues its method.

The appendix applies the same style of argument to the ganzfeld database, answering Ray Hyman's response to the Harris and Rosenthal postscript. The authors state that when Carl Sargent's studies are removed from the database, the median effect size is unchanged; that when the analysis is done by investigator rather than by study — the method Hyman now advocates — dropping Sargent's studies actually increases the median effect size for the remaining nine investigators, whose overall z-score is still 5.07; and that even if Honorton's studies, the other major contribution, are also eliminated, the eight surviving investigators still yield a z-score of 3.67. They answer Hyman's point about eight undisputed experiments by noting that, analyzed by investigator, those studies give an overall z-score of 2.32, and they dispute his claim about sensory leakage by pointing to five binary-coding experiments by three investigators, a methodology Hyman himself had acknowledged is not susceptible to sensory leakage, three of them independently significant with an overall z-score of 2.84. They summarize that the ganzfeld database remains significant even when more than seventy percent of its studies are removed, and is therefore, contrary to Hyman's claim, very robust. On one point they agree with Hyman against the committee's practice: Honorton's new ganzfeld series had not yet been fully reported in a peer-reviewed journal, and the authors endorse the principle that important new findings should undergo rigorous peer review before being taken seriously, noting the committee's inconsistency in considering other unpublished work.

The authors' biographies close the report. Palmer is identified as a past president of the Parapsychological Association, then Senior Research Associate at the Institute for Parapsychology in Durham, North Carolina, and author of a 1985 critical review of parapsychological research for the United States Army that the NRC committee frequently cited. Honorton is identified as director of the Psychophysical Research Laboratories in Princeton, New Jersey, formerly of Maimonides Medical Center, and co-recipient of the first major federal research grant for parapsychology. Utts is identified as Associate Professor of Statistics at the University of California at Davis, with editorial roles at the *Journal of the American Statistical Association*, and statistical editor of the *Journal of the American Society for Psychical Research*. These affiliations matter to a fair reading: the reply is an advocacy document written by officers of the field's professional association, two of them researchers whose own work the NRC report evaluated, and it announces that standpoint on its title page rather than concealing it.
