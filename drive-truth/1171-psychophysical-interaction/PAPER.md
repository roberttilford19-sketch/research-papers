## Premise

The chapter was written to argue a philosophical point by empirical means. The problem of psychophysical interaction, whether mind is produced by the brain or interacts with it, had traditionally been left to speculative philosophy or deferred to future neuroscience. Honorton's premise is that parapsychology is the one field that claims methods for producing the relevant evidence, physical detection of mental influence under conditions that exclude ordinary physiological explanation, and that its two most developed research lines therefore deserve review on that question.

## Abstract

This chapter by Charles Honorton asks whether the mind-body problem can be moved from philosophy into empirical science, and argues that two lines of parapsychological research bear directly on it. The first concerns psi-conducive states. Honorton proposes that such states are internal attention states marked by muscular relaxation, reduced sensory input, enough cortical arousal to sustain consciousness, increased vividness of spontaneous mental processes, and a communication goal. Reviewing the ganzfeld procedure, in which a relaxed subject describes a remote target picture while sensory input is held constant and unpatterned, he reports thirty-two experiments from thirteen laboratories, with more than one thousand sessions and more than five hundred subjects, in which significant effects were obtained in fifty-six percent of the experiments and by sixty-two percent of the laboratories. The second line concerns psychokinesis with electronic random generators, from Schmidt's first experiments onward; Honorton reports roughly five dozen such experiments, about sixty-five percent yielding significant departures from chance under experimental conditions and none in control conditions. He concludes modestly: that the findings justify treating psychophysical interaction as an empirical question, that dualist accounts require evidence of mental processes causing physical effects, and that no stronger claim is warranted at this stage. ## Content Account

The chapter opens with the problem itself. Mind may emerge from physical states, or it may be an independent entity interacting with them; the brain may generate mind or transmit it; and mental states may be only effects of brain states or sometimes their causes. Honorton names the two standard scientific responses, that the problem is metaphysical and outside science, and that it is premature and will be solved by future neuroscience. For the second he adopts Karl Popper's term, promissory materialism, and its criticism: the view presupposes the form of its own solution. Against it he sets J. C. Eccles and Wilder Penfield, neuroscientists who advocated dualistic solutions, and quotes Eccles's speculation that weak mind influences could modify the discharge patterns of large numbers of neurons. Honorton observes that Eccles's case rests on negative evidence, the present inability to identify mind with brain processes, and that bringing it into the empirical domain requires positive evidence of the kind parapsychology claims to supply. He defines that field plainly: the study of interactions between living systems and their environment that are anomalous with respect to currently recognized physical channels, divided into information acquisition, called extrasensory perception, and direct mental influence on external physical systems, called psychokinesis.

Two obstacles to accepting the field's evidence are stated before any results. Psi research has consistently failed to identify physical correlates or a plausible mechanism, which has led scientists to reject the phenomena in advance, a basis Honorton calls dubious while the reduction it assumes remains unproven. And its findings have been difficult to replicate, because its experiments were demonstration-oriented rather than process-oriented. His review is therefore organized around the shift to process research, and around two sample areas chosen, he says, for both methodological and substantive implications.

The first area is psi-conducive states. Spontaneous psi experiences are historically linked to dreaming, hypnosis, and meditation, and Honorton reports that experimental work through 1976 found significant overall effects in forty-nine of eighty-seven experiments using such states, a fifty-six percent success rate against a chance expectation he gives in the text. His explanatory model treats these states as internal attention states sharing five features, set out in the abstract. The reasoning is that ordinary perception is noise with respect to weaker input, so reducing sensory functioning while sustaining attention to internal imagery should improve detection, much as subliminal influences are more readily detected under similar conditions. The ganzfeld procedure is presented as the developed test of this idea, and its evaluation machinery is described in detail: blind ranking of the target among four pictures, or binary coding of target content across ten independent categories drawn from a set of one thousand twenty-four pictures. The database and its success rates are given in the abstract, and Honorton adds two process findings: success correlates with reported changes in subjective time sense and body image, and successful studies averaged thirty-seven minutes of ganzfeld exposure against twenty-two minutes for unsuccessful ones, a difference he connects to the time sensory habituation requires.

The second area is psychokinesis with random generators. These devices derive randomness from radioactive decay or semiconductor noise and record their own output automatically, while the subject watches a feedback display and attempts to bias the output in a preset direction. Honorton traces the line from Schmidt's first experiments, more than thirty thousand trials with fifteen subjects, in which control runs conformed to chance while experimental trials deviated significantly, and reports the aggregate given in the abstract. Two features receive emphasis. Effects appear goal-directed rather than mechanism-directed: switching the feedback display between two internally different random sources, unknown to the subject, made no difference to performance. And in Honorton's own laboratory's work the target state was redefined one microsecond before each trial, which he argues places the effect three orders of magnitude faster than nervous-system functioning and so resists reductionist interpretation; in seven million control trials with that arrangement, he reports, the overall deviation was within 0.03 standard deviations of chance.

The closing section, headed psychophysical reality testing, draws the limited conclusion summarized in the abstract. Honorton claims no answers. His final sentence claims only that an empirical approach to the problem has begun to be developed.
