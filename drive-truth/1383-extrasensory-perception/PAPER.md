## Abstract

This staged assessment describes a collected broadcast script from the BBC General Overseas Service series Research Project, released under the reference NSA-RDP96X00790R000100040020-2. The talk, 'A Fresh Look at Extrasensory Perception', was given by Stephen I. Abrams, identified in the script as director of the new Oxford Parapsychological Laboratory, recorded on 20 June 1962, and transmitted on 25, 26, 27, and 28 June 1962. Abrams opens with a laboratory anecdote in which hypnotised subjects appeared to distinguish a cigarette made foul-tasting by post-hypnotic suggestion from a normal one even after the cigarettes were switched, and he uses the incident to argue that involuntary, conditioned responses may reveal extrasensory perception better than deliberate card guessing. He criticises card-guessing experiments as difficult to repeat and as faint echoes of spontaneous cases, and he presents hypnosis as a favourable experimental environment because motivation, action, and attention can be controlled. He describes Oxford work on waking subjects by thought transference, on using purported extrasensory signals to trigger simple conditioned reflexes such as hand clapping or laughter, and on transmitting drawings, where responses may preserve a target's shape while changing its identity. On mechanism, he reports the older mental radio hypothesis and Soviet shielding experiments, but he concludes that psychological explanations are more likely than physical ones to predict and control the behaviour. The script closes by inviting sceptical listeners, through the White Queen in Alice, to practise believing impossible things.

## Content Account

The script's header preserves production details, including producer John Newell, tape numbers, duration of eight minutes and fifty-five seconds, and the four transmission dates. Abrams cites the Duke University work of J. G. Pratt and Jack Woodruff on symbol size as an example of a physical variable that dissolved into a psychological one, novelty. He also refers to nineteenth-century French distance hypnosis experiments and to Leonid Vasiliev's Leningrad work, in which, as Abrams reports it, hermetically sealed lead chambers did not prevent highly positive results. These references are Abrams's own framing for a general overseas audience, delivered as the final talk in the series.
