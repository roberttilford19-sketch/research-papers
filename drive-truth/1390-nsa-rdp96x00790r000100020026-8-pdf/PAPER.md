## Premise

Its purpose is documentary rather than experimental: it collects a journalist's account of the Leningrad laboratory of Professor L. L. ## Abstract

This document is a 1961 Joint Publications Research Service translation of selected articles from a symposium titled "Thought Transmission — Is It Possible?" published in the Soviet popular-science magazine *Znaniye-sila* in 1960. The longest piece, by G. Anfilov, reports a visit to the Leningrad laboratory of physiologist L. L. Vasil'yev, where experimenters claimed to induce and end hypnotic sleep in subjects by silent mental suggestion, recording the effect with a pneumatic hand-squeeze recorder. The account describes Vasil'yev's test of the "cerebral radio" hypothesis: when the hypnotist was sealed inside a screened metal chamber, including a later version built of thick lead plates, the reported effect was, according to the article, unchanged, and the authors withheld the results from publication for roughly a quarter of a century. Companion pieces survey claims about a cerebral electromagnetic field, including work attributed to P. I. Gulyayev, P. P. Lazarev, and the Italian investigator Cazzamalli, and present individual cases said to show unusual susceptibility to suggestion. The closing contributions are cautionary. I. Klyatskin argues that many apparently successful experiments can be explained by conditioned reflexes and subjective error, and the editor's note concludes that thought transmission, if it exists at all, is extremely rare and that research on it should proceed on a strictly scientific basis. (211 words)

## Content Account

The translation opens with the framing of the symposium. A letter from V. I. Us of the Institute of Power Engineering and Automation asks whether reports in the French magazine *Science et vie* about American experiments aboard the submarine *Nautilus* should be considered scientifically significant, and what comparable research exists in the Soviet Union. The editors state that they put the question to named Soviet scientists, including E. A. Asratyan, D. A. Biryukov, L. L. Vasil'yev, P. I. Gulyayev, and M. N. Livanov.

The central narrative is Anfilov's report from Leningrad. It describes Vasil'yev's seminar presentation of experiments in which a hypnotist attempted mental sleep induction and mental awakening of a subject in another room. According to the article, onset of sleep was registered instrumentally, first by changes in electrical potential between the upper and lower side of the hand and later by a simpler method in which the subject continuously squeezed a rubber bulb connected to a pneumatic recorder; when the subject fell asleep, the hand stopped and the recorded line became straight. The article reports that subjects sometimes appeared to resist a perceived suggestion, that this resistance showed on the graph, and that after repeated series the subjects developed a conditioned tendency to fall asleep without suggestion, an effect the authors themselves name as self-hypnosis entering the results.

The screening experiments are presented as the most striking part of the work. Vasil'yev is quoted reasoning that if the transmitted influence were radio waves, a metallic envelope between hypnotist and subject should block it. A screen was built, first of tin foil and then of thick lead plates forming a cabin whose lid seated in a mercury-filled groove; physicists are said to have checked its reliability, and a separate metal chamber was provided for the subjects. The article reports that the screen made no difference: subjects still fell asleep and selective sleep induction still occurred. The article states that the investigators were so surprised that they refrained from publishing for almost twenty-five years.

Later sections widen the survey. A chapter from Gulyayev's book on electrical processes in the cerebral cortex is summarized, covering Lazarev's 1922 proposal of a cerebral electromagnetic field and experiments from 1925 to 1941 attributed to Cazzamalli with physicists of the Marconi laboratory. Shorter items present individual cases, including a report of a young child said to pronounce words and scientific terms at the moment they were conceived by persons present. These are the document's claims, reported by the magazine; the translation itself offers no independent verification of any of them.

The final third of the symposium is skeptical in tone. One contributor argues that the study of parapsychic phenomena should become a rigorous branch of science, with large numbers of experiments treated by mathematical methods, precisely so that the reality of mental suggestion can be proved or refuted. Klyatskin's contribution, "Experiments Must Be Accurate," argues that many successful thought-transmission experiments may be explained by conditioned reflexes, that experimenters often cannot explain how they read one another, that there are no objective indicators for recording whatever might underlie telepathy, and that contradictory results across laboratories show the need for strict experimental design and fastidious criticism. The editor's closing note states that scientists are still arguing not only about the nature of the phenomenon but about whether it exists at all, finds the foreign sensation over the *Nautilus* reports unjustified, and recommends investigation from materialistic positions, sweeping aside what is false and sensational.

## Errors and Anomalies

The text available to this desk is optical character recognition of a scanned document, and the OCR layer is noisy throughout. Proper names are rendered inconsistently (for example, "Vasil'yev" appears in several spellings, and one Italian researcher's name appears in a form that the document's own translator already flagged as unverified in spelling). Dates and figures embedded in garbled lines, including one passage that appears to date Upton Sinclair's *Mental Radio* to 19350, cannot be trusted at face value and have not been silently corrected. Running declassification headers ("Declassified in Part — Sanitized Copy Approved for Release 2011/12/01: NSA-RDP96X00790R000100020026-8") repeat on nearly every page and are artifacts of the release copy, not content. The translation itself carries a note that a translation from the French press included in the original symposium is omitted from this English version, so the document is a selection, not the complete symposium.

## Limits

This assessment rests on a sampled read of the OCR text: the opening (characters 0–5,500), a middle section (characters 20,000–25,500), and the closing section (characters 57,000–62,977) of the 62,977-character text were read in full, and the remaining stretches were not read line by line. Statements about sections outside those ranges rest on the document's own framing and the sampled passages only. All experimental claims summarized here are claims made within a 1960 Soviet magazine symposium as translated in 1961; none has been independently verified for this assessment, and the document's inclusion of skeptical contributions means it should not be cited as a Soviet endorsement of telepathy.
