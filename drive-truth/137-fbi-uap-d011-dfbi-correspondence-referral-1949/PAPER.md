## Abstract

This file preserves a letter dated 31 January 1949 from the Reverend Charles C. Barnes, pastor, of McMinnville, Oregon, addressed to J. Edgar Hoover in Washington. In the letter, Barnes reports that one afternoon the previous May he saw four narrow beams in the sky passing from the northwest to the southeast and converging in the Cascade Mountains, with small clouds forming in the beams and a great explosion effect where the beams met against the mountains, visible for at least ten minutes. He connects the sight to a pre-war article he recalled about European experiments with radio beams to affect rainfall, and he states that the Columbia River flood of the previous summer and the unusual precipitation of the winter moved him to send the observation in the interest of national security, noting that it might already be known to military authorities or the Atomic Energy Commission. He adds that the explosion effect seemed to rise to a height of about ten thousand feet, and he explains his hesitation to pass on information that might incite warlike attitudes. The file also contains the Bureau's reply, acknowledging his letter of 31 January 1949 and thanking him for furnishing the information, together with mailing markings dated February 1949. The Bureau file records the correspondence; it contains no investigation or evaluation of the observation.

## Content Account

The principal document is the letter itself, on the letterhead of the Reverend Charles C. Barnes, pastor, Box 391, McMinnville, Oregon, dated 31 January 1949 and addressed to Mr. J. Edgar Hoover, Washington, D.C. Barnes introduces the letter as a bit of information that may be of great significance or may be merely an observation of a scientific experiment, and asks whether Hoover can pass it to the right persons.

The observation reported is as follows. One afternoon the previous May, Barnes saw four beams in the sky passing from the northwest to the southeast and converging in the Cascade Mountains. In those four narrow beams, small clouds were forming. Where the beams met, apparently against the mountains, a great explosion effect was to be seen. He states the beams were visible for at least ten minutes or longer, and in a closing addition states that the explosion effect seemed to rise to a height of about ten thousand feet. The sight brought to mind an article read before the war about experiments carried on in Europe with various types of radio beams to effect rainfall, which he guessed he had seen in Science Digest.

Barnes then gives his reason for writing. The Columbia River flood of the previous summer and the unusual precipitation of the winter forced him to feel the observation should be sent somewhere in the interest of national security. He notes he has one son in the Air Corps and another working with the Atomic Energy Commission at Hanford, that as a minister he deplores the waste and tragedy of war, and that this is one reason he had refrained from passing on information and ideas that might incite warlike attitudes.

The remainder of the file is administrative: the Bureau's reply from the Director's office, acknowledging the letter and thanking Barnes for furnishing the information with reference to the matter he mentioned, a communications section mailing stamp dated February 1949, and file pages that are illegible in the extraction.
