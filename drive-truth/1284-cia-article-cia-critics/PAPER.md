## Premise

The two articles approach the same public controversy from opposite directions. The first reports a lecturer's charge that the CIA has suppressed the bodies of creatures from outer space. The second reviews a book arguing that most UFO reports are sightings of a natural electrical phenomenon. Together they show the two poles of the UFO debate as it stood in the American press of the period: an accusation of concealment, and a physical explanation offered against it.

## Abstract

The first article, by John D. Lofton Jr. in the Philadelphia Inquirer, reports the claims of Robert S. Carr, a former lecturer in mass communications at the University of South Florida, who charged in campus lectures and broadcast interviews that the Central Intelligence Agency had covered up the recovery of a crashed craft and twelve dead occupants near Aztec, New Mexico, and that the bodies were held in cryonic suspension at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base. Lofton records that the Air Force base denied the story in a statement read to him in full, that Carr refused to name any of the five hundred people he said had examined the bodies, apart from President Eisenhower, and that Carr said he would not betray his sources. The article is openly sceptical in tone. The second article, by J. S. Butz Jr. in Air Force Magazine for April 1968, reviews Philip J. Klass's book "UFOs — Identified" and endorses its plasma theory, under which many UFOs are balls of ionised air produced by electrical discharges, while also setting out the objections readers were likely to raise against it.

## Content account

The Lofton article gives Carr's account in Carr's own terms and then tests it in the ways available to a reporter. As Carr told it, on 13 February 1948 a flying saucer crash-landed twelve miles west of Aztec, New Mexico, killing all twelve "little men" aboard, and the bodies were taken to the medical laboratory at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base in Dayton, Ohio, where they remained in cryonic suspension. Carr called the alleged suppression "the worst kept secret in America" and put the number of highly placed people who had examined the humanoids and their craft at at least five hundred, drawn from the medical profession, the academic world, and the intelligence community. He added a second incident in which, he said, local law enforcement officers caught a humanoid outside a UFO, and the humanoid died after being taken to CIA headquarters in Virginia. Lofton notes against that detail that the CIA was not in its Virginia headquarters until 1961.

Lofton's own enquiries produced a denial and a refusal. A captain at Wright-Patterson, named in the article as Jim Bowman, read him a statement which Lofton reproduces in its entirety: "There are no little creatures or craft from space at Wright-Patterson AFB now. There never have been. The report is without foundation." The base had received, Bowman said, over 130 queries about Carr's charges. Asked for proof, and for the names of any of the five hundred witnesses other than President Eisenhower, Carr refused, saying he would not betray his sources or "finger" the individuals concerned, whom he praised as people of vision and courage. The article closes by predicting, ironically, that the charges would reach the front pages and that Director William Colby would be called to Capitol Hill to prove one more negative.

The Butz article is a review, and its judgements are the reviewer's. Butz describes Klass, an electrical engineer and senior avionics editor at Aviation Week and Space Technology, as an unusually accurate and productive technical reporter, and describes the book, priced at $6.95 and 290 pages, as an exhaustive reporting job that makes the strongest case yet presented for the plasma theory. The theory, as Butz summarises it, holds that low-altitude UFOs are either hoaxes or corona-discharge plasmas, triggered by salt air, dust, high-voltage power lines, and electrical discharges, and that high-altitude reports, including wartime "foo fighter" reports, arise when an aircraft's accumulated charge discharges in its wingtip vortex. Butz cites a survey by Dr. J. Rand McNally Jr. of Oak Ridge, in which nearly 16,000 people were interviewed and 513, or 3.2 percent, reported having seen ball lightning, and he reports Dr. Martin A. Uman's view that ball lightning is a family of phenomena. He also records the limits of the argument. The theory explains one unknown with another, as a NICAP official quoted in the article puts it; no easy experimental proof had been suggested; and believers resisted it because it appeared to rule out extraterrestrial visits. A sidebar notes the University of Colorado study under Dr. Edward U. Condon, sponsored by the Air Force, with a report due the following autumn, and another records Klass's standing offer of $10,000, payable when an authentic extraterrestrial visitor presented credentials or a clearly extraterrestrial crashed craft was found, against $250 a year from the other party until then.
