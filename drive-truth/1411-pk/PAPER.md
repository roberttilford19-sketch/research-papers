## Abstract

This document is Jack Houck's own history of the PK Parties he organized, dated December 19, 1983. Houck defines a PK Party as a group of fifteen or more people meeting to bend metal using psychokinesis, and he states that the first party, held at his home on January 19, 1981, was planned as a test of his idea that a peak emotional experience would allow the mind to affect a physical object. He describes how metallurgist Severin Dahlen briefed the twenty-one attendees, how a fork held by a fourteen-year-old boy was reported to bend, and how, in his account, nineteen of the attendees then experienced metal becoming soft for periods of five to twenty seconds, a process he calls warm forming. The history goes on to describe further parties, including sessions connected with graduate teaching, and to develop the author's proposed mechanism, in which mental energy is said to be concentrated as thermal energy along the grain boundaries of the metal, with dislocations in the crystal structure playing a central role. Houck reports that annealed or cast metal was very difficult to warm form, which he presents as support for that mechanism, while stating plainly that how the mind converts a goal into thermal energy remains a mystery. The document closes with the author's claims about the parties' effects on participants and society. Every event and effect described is Houck's account, reported here as his account and not adopted by this desk.

## Content Account

The opening pages define the PK Party and narrate the first event. Houck states that the objective was to assemble a group and create a peak emotional experience, at which, he postulated, the mind could make a connection with an object and affect it. He met Severin Dahlen, a metallurgist who had been involved with psychokinesis research at the University of California, Irvine, the week before the first party, and together they prepared the plan. Twenty-one people gathered at Houck's home on the evening of January 19, 1981. His grandparents' silverplated silverware was distributed. Dahlen stood in the center of the seated circle and instructed participants to form a point of concentration, intensify it, move it down through the arm into the silverware at the intended bend point, command the metal to bend, and then release the command.

Houck reports that a fork head held by a boy of fourteen bent, that almost everyone in the room saw it happen, and that the silverware in many hands then became soft and was easily bent and twisted for periods of between five and twenty seconds. He reports that nineteen attendees experienced this during the following hour, and that the same boy put a 270-degree bend in a steel rod five-sixteenths of an inch in diameter. One attendee who did not bend metal is identified in the account as a woman who had said beforehand that she saw no sense in bending silverware.

The middle pages, sampled for this assessment, develop Houck's proposed physical mechanism and describe later parties and demonstrations, including a figure captioned Graduate School Bending. Houck proposes that the mind or subconscious concentrates thermal energy into the grain boundaries of the metal. He reports that metal sometimes becomes so hot internally that gas is formed, which he believes explains the loud pop sometimes heard when a warm-formed object breaks, and that twice the plating of silverplated forks split apart with loud pops while held in one hand. He states that annealed or cast metal is very difficult to warm form and presents this as support for the role of dislocations in concentrating thermal energy. He also states that, despite the data he believes supports this process, how the mind converts the goal into thermal energy along the grain boundaries remains a mystery.

The closing pages present Houck's claims about the parties' wider significance. He argues that as attendance grows, recognition will grow that everyone in the world is interconnected, and that people working together would make a more peaceful planet. The reference list cites his own Conceptual Model of Paranormal Phenomena of September 1982, Rupert Sheldrake's A New Science of Life, Dahlen's Remote Annealing of High Carbon Steel Parts of November 1982, D. Scott Rogo's The Poltergeist Experience, and two works by Cynthia Siegel on the experiences and belief changes of PK Party participants, a December 1983 master's thesis in parapsychology at John F. Kennedy University and a spring 1983 class paper.
