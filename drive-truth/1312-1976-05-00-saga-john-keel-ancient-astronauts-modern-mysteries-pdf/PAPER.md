## Premise

Writing in 1976, twenty-nine years after Kenneth Arnold's 1947 sighting over Mount Rainier, Keel asks what three decades of flying-saucer investigation have actually produced. His answer is double-edged. The phenomenon, he argues, transformed science and society — it made extraterrestrial life a respectable subject, helped propel the space program, and redirected large sums of public money — yet the investigation itself learned almost nothing about the objects, and the money followed prestige and profit rather than evidence.

## Abstract

Keel dates modern ufology from June 24, 1947, crediting Arnold with the sighting and the term "flying saucer," and Captain Edward Ruppelt of Project Blue Book with coining "Unidentified Flying Object." He contrasts the phenomenon's cultural weight with that of the older fairy faith, which researchers such as Jerome Clark, Loren Coleman, and Dr. Stephen Thomas had compared it to: fairy belief, he writes, changed nothing, while UFOs changed a generation. His central section, headed "Who Profits?", argues that the scientific respectability of extraterrestrial life arrived when funding did. He states that NASA was created for three reasons — to catch the Soviet Union, to give President Kennedy a substitute for war, and to search for extraterrestrial life as a public-relations justification — and that the space program spent about four billion dollars a year at its peak, mostly recycled through the aerospace industry, while the intelligence agencies ran on about eleven billion. Against those sums he sets his own figures, drawn from his 1966 inquiries in Washington: roughly 150 million dollars a year on exobiology research, against the Air Force's 300,000 dollars, later raised to 500,000, for the University of Colorado UFO study. He recounts the collapse of both enterprises in the early 1970s, the discrediting of the 1950s contactees once probes reached Venus and Mars, and closes by arguing that a small number of independent researchers made the only real contributions, that humanity knows mainly what UFOs are not, and that the answer cannot be bought.

## Content Account

The article opens as a retrospective and turns, midway, into an argument about money. The first movement covers the founding events and the sociology of belief: Arnold's sighting, the rapid spread of civilian UFO organizations, the public concern of scientists and officials especially in Europe, and the fairy-faith parallel, which Keel accepts as a comparison but rejects as an equivalence, since the fairy myth "made no tangible contribution to our progress" while UFOs, in his account, reshaped science's agenda. The second movement, under the heading "High Costs and Low Comedy," is built on Keel's own reporting. He describes trudging around Washington in 1966 trying to find anyone who knew the exobiology budget, and finding that no official could state it; his best estimate was about 150 million dollars a year, spent on calculations of planetary probabilities and on experiments exposing plants and animals to alien-environment conditions. He then draws the contrast that anchors the piece: the search for extraterrestrial life in the abstract was funded in the hundreds of millions, while the Colorado study of the objects themselves was funded at a small fraction of that, and both ended in failure — the Colorado project, in his words, "a monumental disaster," leaving "great piles of reports, all negative." The final movement records the aftermath: NASA's budget cut in the early 1970s, aerospace workers in unemployment lines, the space program's verdict on the contactees (Venus too hot, Mars apparently dead, George Adamski, who died broke in 1965, refuted by the astronauts who found only dust and rocks where he claimed trees and rivers), and the closing judgment that after twenty-nine years "we don't know much more about the phenomenon today than Kenneth Arnold knew," ending with the question of whether the lights will still be there in 2007 and whether anyone will know more about them then. All figures and judgments in this account are Keel's own, as printed; none are adopted here.
