## Premise

The file assembles newspaper reporting from the 1970s on a single question: whether people claiming psychic ability can help police solve crimes. The clippings were collected, evidently for reference, by a government reader of parapsychology material; each article was produced by its newspaper to report cases, experiments, and official opinion on police use of psychics.

## Abstract

This file contains at least three newspaper articles on psychics and police work. The first, "Psychic's Powers Astound Police" by Paul Bannister, profiles Dutch psychic Warner Tholen, crediting him with pinpointing for police where bodies of missing people would be found, locating water at an African mission from an aerial photograph, and documenting the injury of an astronaut at splashdown. It quotes Professor W. H. C. Tenhaeff of the University of Utrecht, who studied Tholen; Inspector Hendrick Schut, directed to a canal spot where a missing man's vehicle and body were found; and the Reverend Nicholas Borst, a missionary priest in Tanzania. Professor John Beloff of the University of Edinburgh recorded Tholen's prediction about the youngest of the Apollo astronauts. The second article, "L.A. Police Researching Psychics" by Mark Jones of the Los Angeles Times, reports eight months of Los Angeles Police Department research into psychic phenomena; its latest study, begun in October, tests nearly four dozen psychics, homicide investigators, and ordinary citizens with sealed envelopes containing evidence from crimes. Its leader, Dr. Martin Reiser, director of behavioral sciences, is quoted as unconvinced that reputable psychics can solve crimes, wanting a reevaluation of whether psychics have merit. The third item, from The Washington Post of Thursday, November 23, 1978, continues the Los Angeles reporting, including Reiser's evaluation of an earlier test with twelve psychics: little, if any, information elicited would materially help investigate the major crimes, alongside detective accounts where psychics appeared to supply accurate information.

## Content Account

The file's two poles are credulity and skepticism, and the reporting keeps both in view. The Bannister article is a success catalogue. Its Tholen cases are presented through named witnesses — a professor who observed the psychic's work, a police inspector who acted on his directions, a missionary whose letter of thanks is quoted — and its most checkable story, the Apollo splashdown prediction, is tied to a named academic recorder and a specific outcome: the youngest astronaut slightly wounded on one side of his head at landing, and the capsule landing off target, on November 24, 1969. The article offers no failures and no independent verification; it is advocacy journalism, and its claims are Tholen's and his supporters' as reported by Bannister.

The Los Angeles reporting is structured around an experiment rather than anecdotes. The department's design — separate teams of psychics, homicide detectives, and citizens, each person tested individually against sealed envelopes of crime evidence — was an attempt to compare psychics with non-psychics under the same conditions. The Post's continuation supplies the earlier study's outcome, in Reiser's own published conclusion: overall, little if any information of investigative value came from the twelve psychic participants. Both articles, however, also carry the counterweight of working detectives' case stories: a South Gate case in which a housewife psychic's work with a police artist produced a drawing of a man later identified as a suspect in a triple murder, and the Torrance detective Ray Gross's account of the Dutch-born psychic Jan Steers, whose telephone information about a theater murder led officers back to a set of fingerprints at the scene, at a height the psychic gave almost exactly. The case never went to trial, the Post reports, because the suspect hanged himself in jail.

What the file establishes, at the level of the record, is narrower than its subject. It shows that by the late 1970s at least one major police department was publicly and experimentally evaluating psychics, that its lead psychologist's published verdict was negative, that individual detectives nonetheless credited psychics with useful information in specific cases, and that all of this was considered worth clipping and keeping by a government reader. The file does not establish that psychic crime-solving works; its own most rigorous item concludes the opposite for the cases tested.

## Errors and Anomalies

The text available to this desk is optical character recognition of scanned newspaper pages, and it is heavily degraded in the Bannister article in particular: columns are interleaved, words are broken, and some passages — including parts of the castle-plans story — are only partly recoverable. The Los Angeles Times and Washington Post pages are more legible but still noisy, with garbled lines at column breaks. Because the clippings are excerpts gathered into a file, continuations may be missing; the Post item is itself marked as continued from earlier pages. Names, dates, and figures in this assessment are given as the articles render them and have not been independently verified. No substantive wording has been silently repaired.

## Limits

This assessment rests on the full extracted text, all 13,763 characters across the file's five pages. The file's clipping boundaries are not always clear in the extraction, so the count of distinct articles (at least three) is a floor, not a certainty. Every case claim in this assessment is attributed to the article that reports it; none of the psychic-success stories has been verified by this desk, and the file contains no follow-up record of the Los Angeles study it describes.
