## Premise

In this installment, headed "Monster Season," Keel surveys reports of out-of-place and unidentified animals in the United States — tall hairy monsters near New York City, kangaroos in Illinois, escaped chimpanzees and a reported giant penguin in Florida, a Himalayan bear shot in New Jersey, and a damaging snakebite in Ohio — and asks why such creatures, if they exist, are never caught. His recurring answer is drawn from his own field experience: even very large animals can move silently and hide effectively in settled country, so the absence of captured specimens is weaker evidence than it first appears.

## Abstract

Keel opens with 1975 reports from New Jersey and New York, where seven-foot hairy creatures with blazing red eyes were seen miles from Manhattan, and a bear-like biped appeared on Staten Island, which has no bears; searches found nothing. To explain how such animals evade capture he tells two elephant stories. In central India he walked up to what he took for a giant anthill and found a wild elephant a few yards away, which studied him and then left without snapping a twig. In the Catskills, two tame elephants escaped from the Catskill Game Farm through a fence hole, stayed hidden for two weeks despite thousands of searchers and private aircraft, and were found a few miles away. Under the heading "Mysterious Marsupials" he reports kangaroo sightings around Du Quoin, Illinois, in July 1975, including witness Kevin Luthi, who feared being thought crazy, and a police officer who gave chase and lost the trail; zoos and circuses had lost no kangaroo. He adds Florida material — chimpanzees escaped during the filming of a Tarzan picture and still occasionally reported, Ivan T. Sanderson's 1949 conclusion that a creature on Florida riverbanks was a fifteen-foot penguin, and the 1967 shooting of a Himalayan brown bear in New Jersey that no one explained — before a final section on snakes, built around the 1946 Kenton, Ohio, bite suffered by Orland Packer, whose wound, by his wife's later letter to Keel, never healed and cost him part of his heel.

## Content Account

The installment is a miscellany held together by one argument, stated plainly in the elephant passages: size is no protection against being overlooked, and elusiveness is not evidence of nonexistence. Keel supports the monster reports with the Staten Island detail — no bears live on the island, so a bear-like biped seen near the Tibetan Museum does not resolve into an ordinary animal — and he undercuts easy skepticism with the Catskill elephants, which were known, tame, and searched for by thousands, yet vanished for two weeks in a landscape "laced with roads and private homes and cabins." The kangaroo section weighs a mundane candidate seriously. Keel gives the eastern grey kangaroo's size and leaping ability in the text (about five feet tall, leaps of twenty-five feet) and notes that kangaroos are timid unless cornered, then dangerous; some leaping "monsters" seen at night, he suggests, could be renegade kangaroos living unnoticed in the countryside. The reptile section, headed "Ridiculous Reptiles," turns personal. Keel recounts his boyhood herpetology hobby, the two-headed sand boa and three-fanged cobras he brought back from India, his Times Square snake-charming act in a store window to promote a book, and the folklore he heard on lecture tours — the hoop snake and the milk snake, which he states plainly do not exist — before reaching the Packer case. Packer, riding near Kenton, Ohio, on June 9, 1946, was thrown when an eight-foot snake appeared, was bitten in the heel, and had his ankle broken as the snake coiled about his leg; the snake also bit his horse, which survived but lost a patch of hair. Keel compares the venom's effects with those of the king cobra, the black mamba, and the bushmaster, finds no match in his snake catalogs — the failure of the wound to heal over years of fever and sweating, ending in partial amputation of the heel, is the detail he cannot file away — and quotes Mrs. Packer's letter at length. The closing paragraph widens back out to the whole menagerie, from West Virginia's Mothman and reported pterodactyls to lake serpents, and ends with the direct address that these reports are not all hallucinations and that the reader might one day see one of these things. All reports and claims in this account are the article's own; none are adopted here.
