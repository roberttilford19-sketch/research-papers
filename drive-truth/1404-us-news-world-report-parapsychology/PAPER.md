## Abstract

This article, published in *U.S. News & World Report* on 31 July 1978 under the headline "A Stepchild of Science Starts to Win Friends," surveys the position of parapsychology in American science and public life at that date. It reports that new and carefully controlled research into extrasensory perception, clairvoyance, and telepathy was being carried out in government, military, medical, and academic settings, and it quotes Willis Harman of the Stanford Research Institute anticipating discoveries that could alter notions of the mind's capabilities. Against this it sets the burden-of-proof position of the scientific establishment, quoting Philip H. Abelson, the editor of *Science*, that extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence reproducible by impartial investigators. The article cites a Gallup Poll released in June of that year finding that 51 percent of American adults believed in extrasensory perception, with 37 percent believing in precognition. It recounts claimed experimental results at the Maimonides Medical Center, the University of California, and Duke University, describes public-facing exhibits and college courses, and then turns to the intelligence dimension: a declassified Defense Intelligence Agency document on Soviet capabilities, the scale of Soviet spending as estimated by the parapsychologist Charles Tart, and the Stanford Research Institute's claimed demonstrations of clairvoyant spying in 1975. It ends by noting organized sceptical opposition and judging the argument unresolved.

## Content Account

### The respectability claim and the establishment reply

The article opens with its thesis: that parapsychology was slowly but steadily edging toward scientific respectability after years on the fringes, with possible applications of psychic ability said by advocates to range from warfare and crime detection to archaeology, investment, and energy prospecting. It immediately balances that thesis with the establishment's standard reply, quoting Philip H. Abelson, editor of *Science*, as agreeing that parapsychological research had improved markedly while remaining dubious about the results, on the ground that findings questioning the basic laws of nature must be rigorously scrutinized and duplicable by impartial investigators before many scientists will be convinced. The article attributes the disagreement to differing views of the subconscious: many scholars, it says, treat it as a reservoir of acquired information whose surfacing can create an impression of paranormal activity, while parapsychologists argue that their research proves an ability to reach beyond conscious limits of communication.

### Claimed results and everyday belief

The article lists results that, it says, reputable researchers insisted had taken psychic events out of the realm of the unlikely: experimenters at the Maimonides Medical Center in New York claiming repeated success in mental telepathy between widely separated individuals, with some results said to defy chance by a ratio of 100 billion to 1; University of California investigators reporting a woman who could perceive concealed computer-generated five-digit numbers; and long-term projects at Duke University and the Foundation for Research on the Nature of Man indicating that many persons seem able to predict future events. It connects these laboratory claims to everyday anecdotes of premonitions and dreams that later came true, and to the theory of Rex Stanford of the Center for Parapsychological Research in Austin, Texas, that most people unconsciously use psychic abilities to scan the world around them. Every result in this section is presented in the article as a claim by the researchers concerned, and it is recorded here on that basis.

The article supports its account of public interest with a Gallup Poll released in June 1978: 51 percent of United States adults believed in extrasensory perception, 37 percent in precognition, and 30 percent in déjà vu. One figure in the poll passage — the percentage of belief among persons with some college education — is garbled in the recognition of the scan and is not restated here, as described in the Errors and Anomalies section.

### Institutions, exhibits, and the Soviet dimension

The article notes institutional markers of legitimacy: a Smithsonian Institution travelling exhibit called "Psi Search," based on the book of the same name, seen, it says, by more than two million Americans in two and a half years; parapsychology seminars at a growing number of colleges; and the Parapsychological Association's affiliation with the American Association for the Advancement of Science since 1969, alongside the observation that scientific journals almost never publish reports of psychic research.

Its final movement concerns national security. Advocates, the article says, contended that the United States was devoting too few resources to the field and that Soviet-bloc explorations were far ahead. It describes a recently declassified Defense Intelligence Agency document examining Russian capabilities, citing repeated examples of individuals who allegedly could will objects into motion and "psychotronic" generators said to store psychic energy, and speculating that Soviet research might someday enable the perception of top-secret documents, influence on the thoughts of military leaders, and even the disabling of military equipment. It records that formal Western contact with Soviet parapsychologists ended in 1972, when the Soviet government classified the research, and that the Soviet Union had the previous year deported a *Los Angeles Times* correspondent, Robert C. Toth, after he was given a paper on parapsychology by a Russian scientist. It then reports the Stanford Research Institute scientists' account that in 1975, working with two well-known psychics, they had demonstrated the possibility of clairvoyant spying, one describing a secret underground military installation and another sketching a location on a South Indian Ocean island from its coordinates. It closes the section with Charles Tart's figures, given as his statements: that United States funding for parapsychological research averaged about half a million dollars annually, mostly private money spread through fourteen laboratories, while Russia spent the equivalent of fifty million dollars each year. The article ends by describing the Committee for the Scientific Investigation of Claims of the Paranormal, formed the previous year to debunk parapsychology, and by judging that arguments over the field's validity seem likely to remain unresolved far into the future.
