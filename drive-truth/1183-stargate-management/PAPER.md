## Premise

The document was produced to give Congress and CIA leadership a proposed strategy for the Star Gate program after its directed transfer from the Defense Intelligence Agency to the CIA. It sets out to ground that strategy in the completed external technical review by the American Institutes for Research and in the National Intelligence Council's assessment of Russian parapsychology, and to state, element by element, what should happen to the program's operations, its research and development, its foreign assessment work, and its records.

## Abstract

This item is a 1995 CIA document, "Proposed Star Gate Management Strategy," written after Senate conferees directed the transfer of the Star Gate remote-viewing program from the Defense Intelligence Agency to the CIA, with ten civilian billets and five hundred thousand dollars, by 1 July 1995. It recounts the program's three objectives of operations, research and development, and foreign assessment, the declassification work undertaken to support an external technical review, and the review's commissioning through the American Institutes for Research with a panel pairing a leading proponent of parapsychology with a well-known skeptic. Its key recommendations are to terminate remote-viewing operations within the intelligence community, because the review found the information provided vague and ambiguous and not of sufficient quality for actionable intelligence; to terminate remote-viewing research and development, because the review found it unclear whether the existence of remote viewing had been demonstrated; to carry out a comprehensive declassification review of all Department of Defense and CIA program files for transfer to the National Archives; and to apply no resources to assessing foreign parapsychology, which the Directorate of Intelligence judged a relatively low threat. It estimates four person-years and four hundred thousand dollars in each of fiscal 1996 and 1997 for the declassification, archiving, and records work.

## Content Account

The document opens with the congressionally directed action. In early 1995, Senate conferees directed the CIA to transfer the Star Gate program from DIA, specifically ten civilian billets and $500,000 in program resources by 1 July 1995. The conferees' language also adopted an earlier directed action requesting a technical review of Star Gate going back twenty years, a declassification review, and establishment of a cooperative activity with the Russians in parapsychology. A status report on the transfer was presented to committee staff in March and April, but presentation of the management strategy was deferred to 30 September by agreement, to allow CIA to complete the technical review with an external blue ribbon panel commissioned by the American Institutes for Research (AIR), and it was agreed that the final proposed strategy would be based closely on AIR's review.

The history section defines the program. Star Gate was a DIA program involving the use of paranormal phenomena, primarily remote viewing, for intelligence collection, with three objectives: operations, using remote viewing to collect intelligence against foreign targets; research and development, using laboratory studies to improve remote viewing for intelligence use; and foreign assessment, analyzing foreign activities to develop or exploit the paranormal for any uses affecting national security. Before Star Gate in the early 1990s, DIA, CIA, and other organizations ran programs pursuing some or all of those objectives; CIA's program began in 1972 and was discontinued in 1977, and DIA's direct involvement continued until the transfer. The document notes that all government parapsychology programs had been viewed over twenty years as highly controversial and high-risk and had been subjected to various critical reviews, and it distinguishes this review on two grounds: a totally external panel of experts, operating in an open forum with no equities concerning the program's fate, conducted it; and the National Intelligence Council provided oversight during a fresh assessment of Russian parapsychology programs.

On declassification, the document states that CIA declassified its past parapsychology program to facilitate AIR's review, worked with DIA to continue declassification of Star Gate documents, and, at CIA's request, DIA officially declassified the fact that it had had a parapsychology program. CIA collected the relevant CIA and DIA files, built detailed bibliographies, and submitted them to AIR, which specified the documents it needed; all requested documents were declassified and transferred.

The Office of Research and Development asked AIR to evaluate the research comprehensively against acceptable scientific standards, to evaluate the program's utility or usefulness to the intelligence community separately from the question of scientific validity, to consider whether changes might improve results, and to advise on future strategy, basing its findings on the CIA and DIA program data rather than the whole field. AIR's commissioned reviewers, the document states, consisted of a leading proponent of parapsychology actively involved in contemporary research and a well-known skeptic who had closely followed it.

The foreign assessment was conducted largely by DIA Star Gate program officers on temporary assignment to CIA, under the supervision of the National Intelligence Officer for Science and Technology and the Office of Scientific and Weapons Research, overseen by the National Intelligence Council; it was reviewed by NIC and CIA officers but not coordinated within CIA or the intelligence community.

The proposed strategy is stated as four key recommendations. First, terminate remote viewing operations within the intelligence community: AIR, after interviewing multiple current users, concluded, as quoted, that the information provided by remote viewing is vague and ambiguous, making it difficult if not impossible for the technique to yield information of sufficient quality and accuracy for actionable intelligence. Second, terminate research and development relating to remote viewing within the intelligence community: AIR, after assessing its panel's review of over twenty years of CIA and DIA sponsored research, concluded, as quoted, that it remains unclear whether the existence of a paranormal phenomenon, remote viewing, has been demonstrated, and that the laboratory studies do not provide evidence regarding the sources or origins of the phenomenon. The document adds that this does not mean parapsychology research is unimportant or should not continue in the public domain, but that government-sponsored programs had not succeeded in proving the existence of remote viewing despite significant past investment. Third, perform a comprehensive declassification review of all Department of Defense and CIA program files to prepare them for official transfer to the National Archives, quoting the committee's view that the time had come for a re-evaluation of the classification of the effort and its results. Fourth, apply no resources to the assessment of foreign developments in parapsychology: the Directorate of Intelligence, in consultation with the NIC, stated, as quoted, that developments in parapsychology are a relatively low threat to the United States and a low priority among the threats being monitored, and that in none of the Russian research was there credible evidence of developments that could significantly add to the threat Russia posed.

The resource section states what the declassification recommendation entails. Since the transfer, CIA is responsible under Executive Order 12958 for records management of the Star Gate files as though it were the originating agency. The records consist of 43 document boxes of diverse material relating to at least five past CIA and Department of Defense parapsychology programs, named as SCANATE, Sun Streak, Center Lane, Grill Flame, and Star Gate, spanning the early 1970s to 1995, and including operations documentation, training material, internal memoranda, assessments, and program reviews, some of which remain sensitive for sources and methods of other organizations. The CIA's review and declassification team estimated that four person-years would be required for the declassification, transfer, and eventual release work, with an estimated $400,000 in each of fiscal 1996 and fiscal 1997 for contract labor and capital investment in computer hardware and software for scanning and text searching, plus reproduction of transferred records to National Archives standards for permanent records.
