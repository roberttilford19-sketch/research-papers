## Premise

The covering letter was produced to answer an Air Force request of 27 July 1966 for declassification of the panel's report, and to state the conditions under which a version could be released. The panel material itself was produced to evaluate whether unidentified flying objects posed a threat to national security and to recommend how the subject should be handled, after reviewing the best documented sighting cases presented by the cognisant intelligence agencies.

## Abstract

This item consists of a letter from the Deputy Director of Scientific Intelligence to Colonel Gerald R. Jorgensen, Chief of the Community Relations Division, Office of Information, Department of the Air Force, followed by the comments and suggestions of the scientific panel and the panel's report dated 17 January 1953. The letter states that the report as originally drafted could not be downgraded, that an unclassified version omitting the names of personnel and participating organisations had been prepared instead, that permission for release had not been obtained from the persons and organisations involved, and that the Agency was anxious that further publicity not be given to the fact that the panel was sponsored by the Central Intelligence Agency. The panel comments review in detail a group of sighting cases from 1950 to 1952, discuss the quality of the Air Force reporting system, examine the Tremonton, Utah film and reject the photo interpretation laboratory's conclusions on stated technical grounds, and consider radar interference, reported cosmic ray anomalies, and sky patrol proposals. The panel concluded unanimously that the evidence showed no direct threat to national security and no residuum of cases attributable to foreign artefacts capable of hostile acts. It recommended an integrated educational programme of training and what it called debunking, expected to run for at least eighteen months to two years, to strip the subject of its special status and aura of mystery. These conclusions are the panel's own, reported here as its findings.

## Content Account

The panel comments open with a general observation that the members were impressed by the lack of sound data in the great majority of case histories. The cases discussed in detail are named as Bellefontaine, Ohio; Tremonton, Utah; Great Falls, Montana; Yaak, Montana; the Washington, District of Columbia, area; Haneda Air Force Base, Japan; Port Huron, Michigan; and Presque Isle, Maine, with about fifteen further cases considered in less detail. The comments state that reasonable explanations could be suggested for most sightings and that, on lack of danger, the panel concluded unanimously that there was no evidence of a direct threat, citing the wartime foo fighter reports as unexplained but plausibly electrostatic or electromagnetic phenomena. A substantial section criticises the Air Force reporting system for collecting a mass of low-grade reports of little scientific value and for fostering public concern. The Tremonton section lists eleven stated grounds for rejecting the photo interpretation laboratory's analysis, among them that the objects' apparent motions, sizes, and brightnesses strongly suggested birds, and that intensity measurements were made from duplicate rather than original film. Later sections reject a nationwide optical sky patrol as not worthwhile, explain many radar reports by mutual interference between stations, reject a claimed correlation between cosmic ray counter anomalies and sightings as instrumental effects, and recommend that unofficial investigating groups be watched because of their potential influence on mass thinking. The closing report restates the conclusions and recommendations in formal form over two pages.
