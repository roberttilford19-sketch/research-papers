## Premise

The article retells a legend that Keel says flying-saucer contactees of the 1940s and afterward repeatedly passed on: that a inhabited planet called Lucifer once stood between Mars and Jupiter, destroyed itself in an atomic war, and left the asteroid belt as its debris, while some of its people escaped to Earth and became humanity's ancestors. Keel then tests the legend against what astronomers of his day said about asteroids and meteorites, and against rival catastrophe theories involving Jupiter and Venus, asking how much of the story astronomy can support and how much it undermines.

## Abstract

Keel opens with the legend in narrative form. Lucifer's people rose from caves to an atomic age, stayed warlike, wrecked Mars's cities with armed spaceships, and finally destroyed their own world with a doomsday weapon while a small stranded colony on Earth watched the flash in the night sky. The colonists, in his telling, crossbred with Earth's native hairy bipeds, founded a colony in the Andes that later perished, and handed the story down as myth. He then turns to the asteroid belt itself, noting that Ceres, the largest asteroid, measures about 437 miles across by his figure, that Pallas followed in 1802, and that estimates of the asteroid population range from a conservative 50,000 to more than a million objects a mile or more in diameter, a figure he attributes to the Soviet astronomer Dr. S. Orlov. Against the exploded-planet reading he sets composition: known asteroids total only about one-thousandth of Earth's mass, meteorites are largely iron and sometimes solid nickel, and an exploded Earth-like planet would leave mostly worthless rock. He surveys alternatives — Jupiter as a captured body or a decaying star whose arrival wrecked the system, Ganymede as a reported UFO home, and Immanuel Velikovsky's claim that Venus was torn from Jupiter — before closing with a speculative reversal in which Earth itself is Lucifer's largest surviving fragment, and a chain of unanswered questions about whether the cycle of migration and destruction repeats.

## Content Account

The article is one installment of a recurring column, and its structure is a story followed by an audit of that story. The narrative half presents the Lucifer legend as the contactees told it, including the detail that the planet's name varied between tellings — Clarion and Neoposto appear in some versions — while the structure stayed the same: a destroyed world, a warning that Earth would share its fate, and the asteroid belt offered as physical evidence. Keel reports that some farmers in remote areas said they heard the story directly from beings who climbed out of flying saucers. The analytical half weighs the belt's known contents. He gives the discovery sequence of Ceres and Pallas, the range of population estimates, and the mass problem: even a million mile-wide asteroids, plus the thousands of rocks that have drifted out of the belt and fallen to Earth as meteorites over the ages, sit awkwardly beside the claim that a planet larger than Earth once occupied that orbit. His sharpest argument is chemical. Meteorites are rich in iron and nickel, he writes, so a parent planet built from them "must have been built like an iron cannonball," whereas an exploded Earth would vaporize its oceans and atmosphere and leave mostly rock. He also wonders, in passing, whether the Moon — which he describes as at least a billion years older than Earth — and the debris layer in Earth's orbit could themselves be Lucifer's remains. The closing section, headed "Colliding Worlds," collects the Jupiter theories: Jupiter as a decaying star with twelve satellites, four larger than our Moon; Jupiter as a late arrival whose gravity rearranged the solar system and may have triggered Lucifer's explosion; and Velikovsky's Venus hypothesis, supported in the article by ancient Chinese and Middle Eastern records of a comet-like Venus. The final paragraphs entertain the theory that the largest fragment of the exploded super-planet cooled into Earth itself, then end in open questions about whether astronauts from Earth will one day watch their own world blaze and become another generation's Lucifer. All claims in this account are the article's own; none are adopted here.
