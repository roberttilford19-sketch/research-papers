## Premise

The study sets out to review and assess Warsaw Pact countries' activities in paraphysics research, including trends, current research, and potential future achievements, with its main concentration on the Soviet Union. Its preface states that it was intended for use by United States Department of Defense research facilities, the intelligence community, and other government agencies, and Change 1 updated it with research and activities that had occurred since the original 1978 publication.

## Abstract

This Defense Intelligence Agency study, first published on 30 March 1978 and reissued with Change 1 on 4 February 1980, surveys paraphysics research across the Warsaw Pact, concentrating on the Soviet Union. It defines paraphysics as the investigation of unusual mental functioning, including telepathy and extrasensory perception, together with related interactions such as psychokinesis and sensitivities to subtle physical fields. Its executive summary traces Soviet work from a beginning in the early 1920s, through interruption at the start of the Second World War, to a re-initiation in 1959, after which established scientists from various disciplines entered the field and its scope widened. The body of the study proceeds country by country, examining specific investigations in the Soviet Union and then in Czechoslovakia, Bulgaria, Poland, Romania, and other states, and it reviews theoretical models proposed to explain the reported phenomena, including an extremely low frequency electromagnetic hypothesis and a holographic model of brain functioning. A recurring judgment in the country sections is cautionary: the study repeatedly finds the available data inadequate for meaningful evaluation, the experiments poorly controlled or poorly reported, and the publicity given to them disproportionate to the experimental evidence. The copy on disk is incomplete for assessment purposes, because only the first sixty of the study's 138 pages were converted to text.

## Content Account

### Front matter and the change record

The text opens with the study's amended cover and with the Change 1 paperwork: a notice of change dated 4 February 1980 and Air Force Form 1565 entry, receipt, and destruction certificates listing exactly which pages Change 1 removed and replaced across the sections, annexes, and appendices, bringing the study to a total extent of pages i–x and 1–133. The change record is administrative, but it establishes that the 1980 text is a revised document rather than a simple reprint of the 1978 original.

### Scope and definitions

The introduction defines the study's key term. Paraphysics, in its usage, generally refers to the investigation of unusual or paranormal mental functioning, including telepathy, and also covers unusual interactions such as certain sensitivities to subtle electromagnetic fields and dynamic influences such as psychokinesis, together with related physical or biological research that might assist in understanding paranormal mental functioning. The executive summary states the study's historical frame: Soviet research began in the early 1920s, was interrupted at the start of the Second World War, and was re-initiated in 1959, with a later trend toward multidisciplinary research that the study expected to continue growing in investment and scope.

### The country surveys

The second section examines specific investigations in the Soviet Union, including telepathic-type experiments and claimed correlations in electroencephalographic patterns between sender and receiver, which the study notes had also been reported by researchers in the United States and Canada. The Czechoslovak material discusses the investigator R. Pavlita and his claimed psychokinetic effects and "psychotronic generator" devices at length. The study's treatment is sceptical in method: it records that Dr. M. Ryzl investigated Pavlita and was unable to find convincing evidence of psychokinetic ability, that considerable difficulty was encountered in separating ordinary energy transfer, such as body electrostatics or thermal radiation, from what might be considered paranormal effects, and that the popular press greatly exaggerated unofficial remarks, after which formal research with Pavlita was discontinued at that time. Of the Czech conference activity organized by Dr. Z. Rejdak from 1973 onward, the study judges that the material presented was highly speculative and theoretical, that the investigations in general appear to be poorly controlled and poorly reported, and that the data available are not adequate for meaningful evaluation of the various claims.

The Bulgarian section describes the work of G. Lozanov in Sofia, beginning with sleep-wake telepathic experiments in the 1940s judged at the time to be successful, and continuing with his 1960s experiments with blind subjects who were reported to distinguish colours and describe drawings through glass, work the study relates to Soviet "dermo-optics" research. The Polish section reaches back to telepathy experiments at the University of Krakow in the early 1920s and to the European investigations of the clairvoyant Stefan Ossowiecki in the 1920s and 1930s. The Romanian material discusses the "biophysical effect" terminology applied to dowsing-type sensorimotor responses and to ideomotor response in the so-called wedding ring test. All of these are the study's reports of other people's research and conclusions, and they are recorded here as such.

### Theoretical models

A later section reviews concepts advanced to explain paranormal processes. The sampled portion discusses a holographic model, in which the brain is treated as a hologram interpreting a holographic universe, so that information about distant targets would in principle be present throughout the hologram and accessible to paranormal processes; it connects this model to the functional division between the brain's hemispheres and to Soviet neuropsychological research on imagery and memory, while noting that other proposed concepts are rather abstract, difficult to interpret in terms of physical reality, and would not necessarily contribute to experimental investigations. These models are presented in the study as proposals under discussion, not as established explanations.
