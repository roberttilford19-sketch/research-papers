## Premise

The report was produced to account, to the program's client, for four months of work under SRI Project 3183. The program's stated objective is to determine the characteristics of those perceptual modalities through which individuals obtain information about their environment when such information is not presented to any known sense. The report divides the work into applied research — exploring experimentally the potential applications of such abilities, with special attention to accuracy and reliability — and basic research aimed at identifying the characteristics of individuals possessing the abilities, the neurophysiological correlates of the functioning, and its basic mechanisms.

## Abstract

This report is the third progress report of Stanford Research Institute Project 3183, "Perceptual Augmentation Techniques," by Harold E. Puthoff and Russell Targ, dated 31 October 1974 and covering 1 April to 1 August 1974. Its applied-research section reports remote-viewing work, including a three-phase experiment on a client-designated European research and development test facility in which only map coordinates and the facility's general designation were given to the experimenters, a remote-viewing experiment conducted with a subject traveling in Costa Rica, and local-target experiments with mid-test feedback and with azimuth bearing. Its basic-research sections describe screening procedures intended to identify gifted individuals — remote viewing of natural targets, reproduction of simple line drawings under shielded conditions with blind judging, and a four-state electronic random stimulus generator with a pass option — together with medical, psychological, and neuropsychological evaluation of subjects. Further sections report bilateral EEG measurements in a remote strobe experiment, in which alpha reduction correlated with remote stimuli appeared essentially only in the right hemisphere; physiological recording during remote viewing; a universal randomization protocol; and instrument experiments using a superconducting differential magnetometer and a laser-monitored torsion pendulum, in which the authors report statistically significant correlations for one subject and describe them as indicating a highly probable cause-effect relationship requiring further work. ## Content Account

The report states the program objective and its division of effort, then reports progress under each heading. In applied research, the principal item is the Project Atlas remote-viewing experiment on a client-designated target of interest, a European research and development test facility, carried out in three phases to determine the utility of remote viewing under operational conditions. In Phase I, map coordinates were furnished to the experimenters, the only additional information being the target's designation as an R&D test facility; a remote-viewing experiment was then carried out with Subject 1 on a double-blind basis, and the results were turned over to client representatives for evaluation. The report does not state the client's evaluation. Further applied work covers a Costa Rica remote-viewing experiment and local-target experiments with mid-test feedback and with azimuth bearing.

Basic research takes up the larger share. The first task is screening: criteria for determining gifted individuals, pursued through three tests. In remote viewing of natural targets, transcripts are matched to targets by judges, with the probability of the obtained series of matches computed conservatively assuming assignment without replacement; the report states that Subject 1 completed a series obtaining a result significant at the p = 8 × 10⁻¹⁰ level (the exponent as rendered in the OCR copy), with experimentation in progress for Subjects 2 and 4. In the line-drawings test, a pool of fifty simple line drawings of everyday objects is randomized and secured; during a trial the subject is isolated in a shielded room, visually, acoustically, and electrically separated from the target location, a target is selected by the universal randomization protocol, and the subject attempts to reproduce the drawing, with the option to pass; drawings are collected before the subject sees the target, and two SRI scientists not otherwise associated with the research judge the matches blind, without replacement. The report notes that drawings had been obtained from several subjects but not yet submitted for judging. The third screening test uses a four-state electronic random stimulus generator: one of four art slides is chosen randomly by the machine but not revealed until the subject registers a choice, whereupon the slide is illuminated with a bell for a correct choice; the machine records choices automatically, and a PASS button lets the subject decline a trial without score, so the choice of target is not forced.

The second basic-research task is the identification of measurable characteristics of gifted subjects, through medical evaluation, psychological evaluation, and neuropsychological evaluation, the last including administration of the Halstead-Reitan Neuropsychology Test Battery under a consultant from the Palo Alto Veterans Administration Hospital, scheduled for the first half of September 1974. Raw medical data and preliminary psychological notes are relegated to appendices.

The third task concerns neurophysiological correlates. Two EEG facilities are described: a standard facility under SRI's Sleep Studies program director, with two sound-isolated rooms, an eight-channel polygraph, and computer processing producing five-second averages of eight channels, monitoring broad alpha and beta components from left and right occipital regions together with galvanic skin response and plethysmograph data; and a smaller semiportable four-channel polygraph. In the bilateral EEG remote-strobe experiment, repeated with Subject 4, reduction of alpha activity correlated with the remote stimulus was observed essentially only in the right hemisphere — an average alpha reduction of sixteen percent in the right hemisphere against two percent in the left during stimulus trials as compared with no-flash trials — although the trial-to-trial variation was larger than in previous work and the system was being modified before further work. A parallel series recorded galvanic skin response, blood volume and pulse height, unfiltered EEG, and percent-time in alpha while a subject performed remote viewing, with a correlation then attempted between the physiological record and the subject's taped descriptions.

The fourth task addresses the nature of the phenomena and possible energy mechanisms. It describes a universal randomization protocol used across the program to generate target selections and trial commands. In experiments with a Develco superconducting differential magnetometer (gradiometer), 130 trials comprising 64 activity and 66 no-activity periods produced 63 events of signal-to-noise ratio greater than one, of which 42 fell in activity periods and 21 in no-activity periods, a correlation the report gives as significant at the p = 0.004 level; two other subjects produced low, nonsignificant activity. The authors conclude that for Subject 1 the results indicate a highly probable cause-effect relationship, that it appears a gifted subject can interact with the instrument from a distance of four meters, and that further work would be required to determine the precise nature of the interaction, although generation of a magnetic field is considered the most probable mechanism. In the torsion-pendulum experiments, a pendulum of three 100-gram balls inside a sealed, shock-mounted bell jar is monitored by a laser readout; the subject attempts to increase or decrease its oscillation on commands determined by the randomization protocol, first from one meter and later from a room twelve meters away with feedback by closed-circuit video or a parallel chart recorder. The report states that in pilot studies considerable evidence was observed indicating that a gifted subject in the same room could increase or decrease pendulum motion on command, with change-to-baseline ratios often five to one or better, and that decreases below environmental-noise levels were especially interesting as indicating energy coupled out of the pendulum motion. Experiments with a Geiger counter are also listed. Four appendices cover randomness tests of the stimulus generator, personal observations on its use, sample raw medical data, and preliminary notes on psychological testing.

All results summarized above are the report's own reported results, stated by its authors in a client progress report. They are recorded here as the document's claims; this desk has not verified any of them, and the report itself qualifies several as pilot work or as awaiting judging, analysis, or further experimentation.
