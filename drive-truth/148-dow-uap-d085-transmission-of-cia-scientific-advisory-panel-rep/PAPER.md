## Premise

The file was produced to carry a finished piece of scientific advice from one part of the national security establishment to another. A panel of scientific consultants, convened at the direction of the Director of Central Intelligence following a recommendation of the Intelligence Advisory Committee, had examined the evidence on unidentified flying objects and reported on 17 January 1953. The packet preserved here is the administrative vehicle that delivered that report to the Secretary of Defense: a routing slip of the Office of the Secretary of Defense, a formal transmittal letter from the Central Intelligence Agency, and the first two pages of the panel's report itself. Its purpose was not to argue a case but to place the panel's conclusions and recommendations before the Secretary, noting that they identified certain potential dangers to national security related to the subject and suggested ways of eliminating them.

## Abstract

This file is the Department of Defense copy of a short 1953 transmission packet. It opens with a file-folder cover and an Office of the Secretary of Defense routing slip, and its core is a letter dated 13 March 1953 from Richard D. Drain, Secretary of the Intelligence Advisory Committee at the Central Intelligence Agency, to the Secretary of Defense. The letter furnishes a copy of the report prepared by a panel of scientists on unidentified flying objects, convened at the direction of General Smith following a recommendation of the Intelligence Advisory Committee. Drain writes that the panel's conclusions and recommendations point out certain potential dangers to national security related to the subject and suggest ways of eliminating them, and he adds that the Agency does not consider problems arising from sightings of flying saucers primarily its concern, while offering assistance in any appropriate action. Enclosed with the letter are the first two pages of the panel's report, dated 17 January 1953. In them the panel concludes that the evidence shows no indication that the phenomena constitute a direct physical threat to national security, and it recommends that national security agencies strip unidentified flying objects of the special status and aura of mystery they had acquired.

## Content Account

The packet has four components, in the order the scan presents them. The first page is a file-folder cover carrying a declassification sticker (declassification authority NND 823015, as read in the optical character recognition text), a handwritten tab notation reading "1953," and a National Archives footer; it is nearly blank by nature. The second and third pages are the front and reverse of a Secretary of Defense routing slip, form SD 32. The front records the item received from the Central Intelligence Agency, names Richard D. Drain as the sender in his capacity as Secretary of the Intelligence Advisory Committee, gives the date received as 17 March 1953, and summarizes the subject as a report prepared by a panel of scientists on the subject of unidentified flying objects. The reverse carries the printed instructions to recipients governing the handling of classified material, including the rules for material in the highest categories, which may not be sent through the mail and may be handled only by specifically authorized personnel.

The fourth page is the substantive heart of the packet. It is the transmittal letter of 13 March 1953, on Central Intelligence Agency letterhead, signed by Richard D. Drain as Secretary of the Intelligence Advisory Committee. The letter states that the Director of Central Intelligence has asked that the Secretary of Defense be furnished a copy of the attached report. It explains that the panel was convened at the direction of General Smith, following the recommendation of the Intelligence Advisory Committee. It characterizes the report's conclusions and recommendations as pointing out certain potential dangers to national security which are related to the subject, and as suggesting ways of eliminating them. It adds the Agency's institutional disclaimer: although the Agency does not consider problems arising from sightings of flying saucers primarily its concern, it will be pleased to assist in any appropriate action the Secretary may deem advisable. It closes by noting that copies of the report are also being sent to at least one further recipient and to the Chairman of the National Security Resources Board, although the name of the other recipient is not reliably legible in the recovered text. The fifth page is the reverse side of the letter sheet showing through the paper; it carries no text of its own, only a faint received stamp of the Office of the Secretary of Defense dated 17 March 1953, read visually rather than by character recognition.

The sixth and seventh pages are the first two pages of the enclosed document, headed "Report of Scientific Panel on Unidentified Flying Objects" and dated 17 January 1953. The report states that the panel of scientific consultants met at the request of the Assistant Director for Scientific Intelligence to evaluate any possible threat to national security posed by unidentified flying objects and to make recommendations. It states that the panel received the evidence as presented by cognizant intelligence agencies, primarily the Air Technical Intelligence Center, and reviewed a selection of the best documented incidents. The panel's first conclusion is that the evidence presented shows no indication that the phenomena constitute a direct physical threat to national security. The panel states its firm belief that there is no residuum of cases indicating phenomena attributable to foreign artifacts capable of hostile acts, and that there is no evidence that the phenomena indicate a need for the revision of current scientific concepts. Its further conclusion is of a different kind: that the continued emphasis on reporting these phenomena does, in the panel's words, result in a threat to the orderly functioning of the protective organs of the body politic. The report supports that conclusion with three examples: the clogging of channels of communication by irrelevant reports, the danger of being led by continued false alarms to ignore real indications of hostile action, and the cultivation of a morbid national psychology in which skillful hostile propaganda could induce hysterical behavior and harmful distrust of duly constituted authority. The recommendations follow from that reasoning. The panel recommends that the national security agencies take immediate steps to strip unidentified flying objects of the special status they have been given and the aura of mystery they have acquired, and that the agencies institute policies on intelligence, training, and public education designed to prepare the defenses and the morale of the country to recognize and react to true indications of hostile intent. It suggests that these aims may be achieved by an integrated program designed to reassure the public of the total lack of evidence of inimical forces behind the phenomena, to train personnel to recognize and reject false indications quickly and effectively, and to strengthen regular channels for evaluating true indications of hostile measures. The page closes with the panel's signatures and institutional affiliations, among them the California Institute of Technology, Brookhaven National Laboratories, and Johns Hopkins University. These findings and recommendations are the panel's own, reported here as the document states them; this assessment adopts none of them.

## Errors and Anomalies

The recovered text is uneven in quality, and the unevenness follows the physical character of the pages. The transmittal letter and the two report pages are clean, with only minor character errors, such as "Plying Saucers" where the report plainly means "Flying Saucers." The routing slip pages are noisier: the two-column printed layout interleaves in places, so individual lines must be read with care, and the handwritten entries and initials on the slip were not reliably captured at all. The fifth page is an anomaly of the scan rather than of the document: it is the mirror-image show-through of the letter on the preceding page, and any text recovered from it is a garbled duplicate of that letter rather than independent content. The cover page carries almost no text by nature, so its thin yield is not a defect. One substantive gap sits inside the letter itself: the sentence listing the other recipients of the report is only partly legible, and the recipient named alongside the Chairman of the National Security Resources Board cannot be stated from this text. Finally, the packet contains only the first two pages of the panel's report. Whether the enclosure originally ran longer cannot be determined from this file, and nothing beyond those two pages should be attributed to the enclosure on this file's evidence.

## Limits
