## Premise

The paper was produced to move a claim from earlier, less controlled demonstrations onto what its authors regarded as strict ground. Earlier workers, the authors say, had reported objects removed from sealed canisters, envelopes, and tubes. Their stated purposes were to use samples that, once damaged, could not be restored, combined with visual records, to test the reality of the ability further, and to observe where and over what span of time the barrier was penetrated.

## Abstract

This paper, by Song Kongzhi, Li Xianggao, and Zhou Liangzhong of the Aerospace Medicine Engineering Institute, in an English translation issued as document LN190-91, reports experiments in which the subject Zhang Baosheng was said to remove objects from sealed containers without damaging the containers. The authors designed unique, coded test samples: sealed glass bottles containing counted, color-coded tablets or plexiglass pieces lettered so as to form words, medicine bottles in their original seals, and sealed glass tubes containing lengths of wire fixed at one end. Samples were not given to the subject beforehand, were watched continuously, and were recorded on videotape, with high-speed photography at up to one thousand frames per second in some trials. Of fifty experiments, the authors report twenty-five as successful, seventeen of them videotaped. They report tablets and plexiglass pieces removed with the counts outside and inside the bottle reconciling exactly and the bottles undamaged, photographic paper passed half in and half out of a bottle wall across successive video frames, and wire drawn out of tubes in sections. From frame timing they estimate passage speeds from about thirty centimeters per second to about 2.8 meters per second. They conclude that the passage point is the container wall itself, and note that every successful sample had a slit, hole, or stopper, whose significance they leave open.

## Content Account

The introduction surveys the prior work the authors build on: reports that persons with paranormal abilities removed nuts, nails, and matches from a sealed plastic film canister, target paper from a sealed kraft envelope, insects from sealed glass tubes without discernible effect on the insects, and a sponge dipped in one chemical from a double-layer sealed test-paper bag without the color change the authors say contact would have produced. The authors present these as the preliminary basis that made stricter testing worthwhile.

The samples are the paper's center of gravity, and they are described at length. One design used sealed transparent glass bottles, four centimeters in diameter and twelve to thirteen centimeters long, holding thirty tablets, five and a half millimeters in diameter, colored red, green, or purple in ratios that differed by bottle; each bottle was individually numbered and its contents recorded on its label in a binary coding system, and the mouth was heated and closed to a slit through which, the authors state, even a tablet broken in half could not be poured out. A second design used the same bottles with fifteen plexiglass pieces, five millimeters square, in three colors, each piece bearing a letter so that each color formed a word, with serial numbers marked on the bottle. Other samples were unopened commercial medicine bottles with cork, wax, and screw lids, each holding one hundred tablets; hundred-milliliter bottles holding fifty tablets and capsules of five types under a rubber stopper and aluminum cap; sealed glass tubes, twenty to twenty-five centimeters long, containing two meters of spiraled enameled wire with one end sealed into the tube; similar tubes containing one and a half meters of shielded wire; and a five-hundred-milliliter bottle containing a packet of unexposed photographic paper on a string.

The stated principles were continuous close observation and video recording of the whole process, no prior access to the samples by the subject, no removal of samples from the site, collection and storage of all samples afterward, and a double blind on the coded labels for both the person administering the test and the subject. The subject is identified as a man of twenty-six, with a history of tuberculosis, fully recovered, and normal physical development.

The results are reported as totals and as cases. Of fifty experiments, twenty-five are reported successful, seventeen videotaped, six recorded with high-speed photography, and twenty-one successful samples retained in storage. In two videotaped bottle experiments, tablets were removed, in one case one and a half tablets, the halves explained by the authors as breakage when the subject shook the bottle; the counts of each color inside and outside reconciled to the starting thirty, and the bottles were undamaged. In three experiments with the lettered plexiglass pieces, five, three, and three pieces were removed from three bottles, matching the pieces found missing in number, color, letter, and code. The authors argue that the coding, the double blind, the subjects' lack of prior contact, continuous watching, videotape, and the impossibility of reassembling broken glass together exclude switching or opening.

The paper then turns to the passage point, which is its distinctive claim. High-speed photography of the medicine-bottle experiment at four hundred frames per second is described as showing a tablet absent in one frame, two-thirds outside the glass at the bottom of the bottle in the next, and three millimeters clear in the third. Videotape of the photographic-paper packet is described frame by frame at forty-millisecond intervals: the packet appears outside the bottle, is half in and half out, parallel to the table and cutting through the side of the bottle, and is then fully removed. With the wire tubes, the authors describe the subject pinching the end of the wire outside the glass while the wire inside followed the movement of his fingers in a shearing motion, leaving no visible mark, and report the wire and an attached iron washer appearing outside in sections. From these timings the authors compute the passage speeds given in the abstract and note the spread, from slow enough to follow by eye to a few milliseconds for small objects through thin walls.

The discussion concedes one condition the authors cannot explain. In every experiment the container had to have a slit, a small hole, or a stopper for the experiment to succeed; objects could not be removed from a completely closed container. Yet the passage point, they maintain, was not at the slit, hole, or stopper, but in the wall itself. Whether that opening is a psychological or a physical requirement is left as a question for further study. The conclusions restate the authors' position that the existence of the ability, the wall as passage point, and passage over a period of time were proved by the twenty-five successful experiments. That conclusion is the authors', reported here as theirs; the underlying videotapes, photographs, and retained samples are not part of this item, and this desk has not examined them.

## Errors and Anomalies
