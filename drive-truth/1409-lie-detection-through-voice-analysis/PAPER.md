## Premise

The article sets out to test the popular claims then being made for voice analysis as a lie detection method against the evidence available from validation studies, congressional testimony, and the statements of the devices' own manufacturers and developers. Its stated purpose is to explain why no military law enforcement agency was using voice analysis for lie detection at the time of writing, while all of them used the polygraph where appropriate.

## Abstract

This article by Frederick C. Link, a United States Army chief warrant officer and polygraph instructor, examines whether voice analysis could detect deception, as popular coverage in magazines, film, and television had suggested. Link explains the claimed basis of the technique: that the human voice combines a basic sound produced at the vocal cords, formant sounds produced by resonances in the head, and an inaudible microtremor, a frequency modulation in the range of eight to twelve hertz, which devices such as the Psychological Stress Evaluator treated as an index of psychological stress. He then reviews the validation evidence available to him, including an Army-commissioned comparison of voice analysis and the polygraph by Joseph P. Kubis, and notes that a further validation study at Michigan State University had announced no findings when he wrote. The article's longest section asks whether voice analysis can determine whether public figures told the truth in public statements, prompted by published analyses of statements connected with the Kennedy assassination and the Patty Hearst case. Link quotes device manufacturers and developers telling Congress that their instruments could register stress but could not determine deception from routine recordings, broadcasts, or surreptitious taping, because lie detection requires control questions, a structured examination, and a post-test interview. His conclusion, stated in the article's own terms, is that voice analysis had not been developed to the point of usefulness for military lie detection, whatever it might become in the future.

## Content Account

The article opens by listing the sources of the public impression it disputes: articles in Playboy and Penthouse, the film The Trial of Billy Jack, and television presentations claiming that voice analysis showed what really happened in the Kennedy assassination. Link states that these presentations were not unbiased and that citizens had formed an unrealistically high opinion of the method's present value.

The technical section explains the three components the manufacturers described. The basic sound is formed by air forced over the vocal cords and lies generally between 100 and 300 hertz. The formant sounds are resonances created by the cavities of the head, especially the mouth. The microtremor is an inaudible frequency modulation, normally between eight and twelve hertz, superimposed on the other two. Voice analysis devices, of which the Psychological Stress Evaluator is the article's main example, claimed to detect stress through changes in the microtremor.

The article then turns to validation. Link discusses the published claims, the congressional hearings on the use of polygraphs and similar devices by federal agencies, and the Kubis comparison study conducted for the U.S. Army Land Warfare Laboratory. He notes that a new validation study was being conducted at Michigan State University but that no findings had been announced.

The longest section addresses the analysis of public figures' recorded statements. Its central case is the article by George O'Toole, a trained voice analyst, published in the April 1975 issue of Penthouse under the title Lee Harvey Oswald Was Innocent, which concluded from voice analysis that Oswald was probably telling the truth when he denied shooting President Kennedy. Link also cites press reporting on the tapes made by Patty Hearst. Against these claims he sets the testimony of the voice analysis community itself. A manufacturer of the Psychological Stress Evaluator told Congress that stress indications without controls, a pre-test, a structured examination, and a post-test interview remain only indications of stress. The developer of the Mark II Voice Analyzer stated that the instrument could provide data on stress in dialogue but that no instrument then available could analyze routine dialogue and determine deception. A professional society president, also quoted, stated that from clandestine or broadcast recordings no truth evaluation could be undertaken. Link closes by observing that these statements to Congress conflict with the claims that had reached the popular press.
