## Abstract

This six-page file is an excerpt of the Defense Intelligence Agency report *Soviet and Czechoslovakian Parapsychology Research* (ST-CS-01-169-72), prepared by the U.S. Army Medical Intelligence and Information Agency and dated July 1972. The excerpt opens with a cover in DST form, then presents passages on Soviet telepathy research under L. L. Vasilev, including his laboratory for biocommunications research at the University of Leningrad, his claimed long-distance experiments between Leningrad and Sevastopol, and reports that the Soviets trained cosmonauts in telepathy as a backup to electronic equipment. Its central passage concerns apports and luminous bodies: it discusses speculation that such bodies, reported to travel unlimited distances and pass through solid material, might be controlled and used against military and civilian officials and equipment, and it reasons from reported cases that organic matter might be disintegrated and reintegrated by faculties of the subconscious mind. The excerpt closes with a statement attributed to a researcher named Pullman predicting that Soviet diplomats would use an apport-related form of extrasensory perception, through astral projection, to steal secrets, and with a closing section quoting Oliver Caldwell and astronaut Edgar D. Mitchell on the importance of the field. All of these statements are the report's own, and are attributed to it here.

## Content Account

The first page is a cover in DST form, spliced in this file to body sheets of the July 1972 report (see Errors and Anomalies). It names the Defense Intelligence Agency, gives the report title *Soviet and Czechoslovakian Parapsychology Research* and the preparing agency, the U.S. Army Medical Intelligence and Information Agency, Office of the Surgeon General; its own report number is garbled in the recognition text and is not the number carried on the July 1972 body sheets that follow. The remaining five pages are body text from the report, headed ST-CS-01-169-72 and dated July 1972; they are not continuous — the excerpt jumps between passages, and the visible parent pagination (pages in the high twenties and pages 39 and 40) confirms that this file holds selected pages rather than a complete section.

The first body passage concerns telepathy. It states that although the United States Navy subsequently denied reports of telepathic testing on atomic submarines, the Soviet hierarchy apparently heeded Vasilev's advice and supported his view that, in the quotation the report gives, "the discovery of the energy underlying telepathic communication will be comparable to the discovery of atomic energy." It states that since 1962 Vasilev had headed a special laboratory for biocommunications research at the University of Leningrad, whose work included developing machines capable of monitoring and studying telepathic communication, and that in 1953 Vasilev claimed successful long-distance telepathic experiments between Leningrad and Sevastopol, a distance the report gives as 1,700 miles, conducted with an ultra-short-wave radio transmitter. It records theorizing by unnamed Soviet scientists — that telepathic impulses might be radiated as bits of information in a cybernetic system, or that transmissions might propagate through a supposed time-energy system rather than the electromagnetic field. It quotes K. E. Tsiolkovsky, described as the father of Soviet rocketry, on the necessity of telepathic abilities in the coming era of space flights, and reports that the Soviets were training cosmonauts in telepathy to back up their electronic equipment, one scheme involving coded signals.

The second passage is the one the Drive filename points to. It begins from Soviet seriousness about luminous bodies, evidenced, the report says, by Kirlian photography of the human aura, and considers the prospect that a hand which appears out of nowhere and can grasp "with the firm pressure of an old friend" might have first-rate military possibilities. It reports discussion of controlling the apport technique to a point of sophistication where individuals could control these "luminous clouds," and it states the claimed properties on which the speculation rests: that these bodies can travel unlimited distances and pass through solid material such as walls, and so might be used to produce instant death in military and civilian officials or to disable communications equipment. The report is explicit that this material is questionable: it introduces its deductions with the condition "if any of this highly questionable material is true." On that condition, it infers that organic matter can be transformed into "ectoplasm," rendered invisible and impalpable, and converted into something amounting to force, and that a physical object so converted could travel through solid matter. It then states as certain, in its own words, that parapsychological phenomena are due to little-known faculties of the subconscious mind and that the powers of the subconscious are vastly superior to those of normal consciousness, citing the subconscious mind's memory and mathematical ability. It suggests that these faculties may have something to do with disintegrating and reassembling objects, and concludes that the only way to learn more is through intensive study and experimentation, since "the stakes seem high enough."

A following passage extends the same reasoning. It states that while the process by which matter is converted into "force-matter" and back may not be understood, experiments are said to show that a human body which has lost about half its weight can be reintegrated without loss of normal functions, and that it therefore does not seem safe to exclude, without further investigation, the possibility that inorganic matter might undergo similar disintegration and reintegration. It states that apport phenomena, in which physical objects have passed through solid matter, have been observed and attested by eminent scientists and other responsible witnesses, and it concludes, "in view of the very real Soviet threat in this sector," that parapsychology should be investigated to its fullest potential, perhaps to the benefit of national defense. The passage then turns to a prediction attributed to Pullman, identified as director of the Southeast Hypnosis Research Center in Dallas, Texas: that before the end of the 1970s Soviet diplomats would be able to sit in their foreign embassies and use extrasensory perception — in this case a form of the apport technique — to steal the secrets of their enemies. The mechanism described is astral projection: a spy would be hypnotized, his invisible "spirit" ordered to leave his body, travel to a foreign government's security facility, read top-secret documents, and relay back their information. The report states Pullman's further claims that such astral projection had already been accomplished in laboratory settings, that the Russians were probably trying to perfect it, and that the Soviets were at least twenty-five years ahead of the United States in psychic research. It names other scientists and mediums interested in this work — H. A. Cahn of Northern Arizona University, Charles Tart, and V. Inyushin of Alma-Ata among them, as read in the recognition text — and quotes Sybil Leek's warning of a danger that within ten years the Soviets would be able to steal top secrets by out-of-body spies.

The final body page begins a closing movement. Its partly legible opening lists four areas of possible hostile use — knowing the contents of top-secret United States documents and the movements of troops, ships, and military installations; holding the thoughts of key United States military and civilian leaders at a distance; causing the instant death of any United States official at a distance; and disabling United States military equipment, including spacecraft, at a distance. It concedes, in its own words, that these four areas "sound like science fiction," while stating that the literature appears to support the second, thought-holding, as the most possible use of psi phenomena during the study's time frame. It quotes Oliver Caldwell, identified as an expert on Soviet affairs and past acting commissioner for International Education in the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare, expressing amazement at American skepticism toward Soviet parapsychology experimentation and warning that if the United States did not make a serious effort on this frontier, "in another ten years it may be too late." It closes the section with a quotation from astronaut Edgar D. Mitchell, Jr., drawn from his Apollo 14 experience, stating that extrasensory perception is not a matter of belief, that serious scientific work has established its probabilities beyond chance over thirty years, and that he considered the work important. The file's last sheet is a release notice stating that the next page in the document is exempt, so the excerpt ends there.
