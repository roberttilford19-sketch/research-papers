## Premise

The report was produced to give the sponsoring agency a full final account of a twelve-month study of "remote viewing," the term its authors use for the claimed ability of individuals to describe remote geographical or technical targets by mental imagery alone, with no sensory access to them. It sets out to document both the applied work, in which the ability was tested against sponsor-designated and operationally styled targets, and the basic research intended to screen for gifted subjects, identify their measurable characteristics, and look for neurophysiological correlates and possible physical mechanisms.

## Abstract

This document is the final research report of Stanford Research Institute Project 3183, "Perceptual Augmentation Techniques," by Harold E. Puthoff and Russell Targ, dated 1 December 1975 and covering January 1974 through February 1975. Its applied-research section reports long-distance remote-viewing experiments on sponsor-designated targets, including two subjects' descriptions of a West Virginia site given only by coordinates, an SRI-designated series built around a subject traveling in Costa Rica, a short-distance test using an abacus as an analogue for a cipher machine, a technology series, and an experiment on detecting secret-writing target material. Its basic-research section reports screening tests in which six subjects, three described as experienced and three as learner controls, viewed natural targets under a standard protocol and were evaluated by blind rank ordering, together with tests on a four-state electronic random generator. The contents also list sections on medical, psychological, and neuropsychological evaluation, electroencephalographic experiments, and instrument experiments, which fall largely beyond the pages available as text here. Throughout, the results reported are the authors' own reported results, stated in a contractor's final report to its sponsor, and this assessment records them as the document's claims rather than as findings verified by this desk.

## Content Account

The report opens with the authors' own abstract, which states that visiting personnel of the sponsoring organization, with no previous exposure to the concepts, performed well under controlled laboratory conditions, in that independent judges could blind-match their descriptions to targets, and that subjects trained over two years performed under operational conditions and provided data later verified by independent sources. Those are the authors' summary claims for their program, and the body of the report presents the experiments offered in support of them.

In the applied research effort, the first experiments concern a West Virginia site whose coordinates were supplied by the sponsor's threat-analysis group and relayed to two subjects on a double-blind basis. The first subject's recorded responses, from 29 and 30 May 1973, describe mounds or rolling hills, a possible former military installation, a circular building, and something underground. The second subject's written response describes mountain terrain, a roadway, and a distant city. The report presents drawings by both subjects and treats the general location as correctly spotted. A further SRI-designated series used a subject traveling in Costa Rica; the report describes twelve submitted descriptions, blind matching of five correct matches against seven target locations by the returning experimenter, and a stated probability of p = 0.02 by exact binomial calculation. A short-distance experiment used an abacus purchased in New York as an analogue for a cipher machine of interest; the subject, who did not know the target, drew the object and described it as a game box with little balls. The applied section continues with a technology series and with an experiment on the detection of secret-writing target material, in which card-sorting runs by sequential sampling produced, in one series, a stated probability of 0.09 for the result obtained, and in a second shorter series, chance results.

The basic research effort, which the report states took approximately half the program effort, begins with screening. Six subjects were studied, three considered gifted or experienced and three acting as learner controls, matched in pairs by age, sex, or background. Two screening paradigms were used: remote viewing of natural targets under a standard protocol, and determination of the state of a four-state random target generator. The report states its own thresholds in advance: a result at p below 10 to the minus eight power is considered unambiguously paranormal, a result at p below 0.01 is taken as strongly indicative, and a result at p below 0.05 is treated as circumstantial evidence requiring further exploration. In the sponsor-subject series reported near the end of the text available here, two sponsor staff members took part in five experiments between them; blind rank ordering gave a stated significance of p = 0.017, with three direct hits and one second rank. The report's summary of the standard-protocol experiments states that the descriptions, although containing inaccuracies, were accurate enough for judges to differentiate among targets to the degree tabulated.

The table of contents lists the remaining basic-research sections: a four-state electronic random number generator study; medical, psychological, and neuropsychological evaluation of gifted subjects; identification of neurophysiological correlates, including a remote strobe experiment and physiological monitoring during remote viewing; and experiments with physical apparatus, including a Geiger counter, a laser-monitored torsion pendulum, and a superconducting differential magnetometer, followed by discussions of possible mechanisms, a communication-theory approach, Soviet efforts, and conclusions, with appendices. Because the text available for this assessment ends partway through the report, those sections are identified here only as the contents list presents them, and no statement is made about their contents.

All results summarized above are the report's own reported results. The report is a contractor's final report written for its sponsor, several of its experiments are described by its authors as exploratory or pilot work, and the sponsor's independent evaluation of the work is not contained in the document. This desk has not verified any of the reported results.
