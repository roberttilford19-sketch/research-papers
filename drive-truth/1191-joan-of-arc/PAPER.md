## Premise

The principal article sets out to present Joan of Arc as a documented case of precognition and clairvoyance. Its author, a physician writing as a parapsychologist, argues that Joan's prophetic gifts are unusually well evidenced because her life is attested both by contemporary chronicles and by the records of her trial and rehabilitation proceedings, and he offers the article as a short glimpse of a book he intended to write on Joan and parapsychology.

## Abstract

This file contains "Joan of Arc's Prophecies," by Jean Barry, M.D., of Bordeaux, France, who writes that Joan of Arc is not a myth: her feats are attested by chronicles of both sides and the instruments of the Inquisition trial led by Cauchon and Bedford, and the rehabilitation trial initiated by Charles VII preserves testimony of men, women, priests, and warriors. Barry, a parapsychologist, is interested in her moral nature, cleverness, military talent, and gifts of premonition and clearsightedness, and intended a book on Joan and parapsychology. He divides her clear-sightedness into near-future cases, called certain and testified by history, and furthest-future cases, called arguable and often transmitted by family tradition. Among the first he recounts her foretelling at Vaucouleurs the defeat at Rouvray before news could arrive; where a rusted sword would be found behind an altar; two predictions she would be wounded, followed by an arrow wound above the breast; and her words to Sir Glasdale shortly before he drowned at the burning drawbridge. Among the second he recounts, as family traditions, predictions attached to descendants of La Hire and Georges de La Trémoille, the latter said to have been confirmed as recently as 1933. Barry closes by rejecting coincidence and vengeful haunting, attributing the testimony to an extraordinary gift of clearsightedness and precognition. Interleaved by scanning, the file also carries an unrelated journal passage arguing that state-vector reduction in quantum mechanics is not mysterious when state vectors represent potential interactions with observing apparatus.

## Content Account

The article's structure is a brief: documented cases first, traditional cases second, conclusion last. Its near-future cases rest on named episodes from the historical record as Barry presents them — the Rouvray prediction made to Robert de Baudricourt at Vaucouleurs, the sword found behind the altar at Sainte-Catherine-de-Fierbois (rendered in the article as Sainte Marie de Fierbois), the wound predicted the day before it occurred, and the exchange with Glasdale at the Tourelles. Barry asserts these cases are historically true; that assertion is his, and this desk has not checked the episodes against the trial records or chronicles.

The long-range cases are presented more cautiously, and the article itself marks the difference. The La Hire tradition — that Joan predicted the last of La Hire's line would die ruined if he returned to pillaging, and that a descendant, Jean de La Hire, died impoverished some fifty years before the article — is offered through named intermediaries, including a Mrs. Michelle Nahon and a Dr. Giry who is said to have prepared detailed documentation of Joan's psychic feats. The La Trémoille tradition — that Joan, imprisoned at Rouen, predicted the last of that line would die burned alive, and that the young Duke died in a hunting-box fire in Scotland in 1933 — is likewise offered through named intermediaries, including the late Duchess's physician and the physicist Olivier Costa de Beauregard. Barry's own closing words frame the choice for the reader: "Believe them or not, you shall not be damned for that." His conclusion nonetheless adopts the precognition explanation, and the article should be read as advocacy by an interested parapsychologist, not as a neutral historical study.

The interleaved quantum-mechanics passage is not part of Barry's article. It argues, with references to Bohr, Jammer, Lawden, and Villars in the Journal of Psychophysical Systems, that state-vector collapse seems obvious rather than occult once a state vector is understood as representing a system's potentiality for interacting with observing apparatus. Its presence in the file appears to be an artifact of how the source journal issue was scanned into the release, and it is reported here only so that a reader of the file is not misled about its contents.

## Errors and Anomalies

Two anomalies dominate. First, the file interleaves two distinct texts — Barry's Joan of Arc article and an unrelated journal passage on state-vector collapse with its own reference list — in alternating column fragments, a scanning or compilation artifact noted in the ledger card's description of the file as research notes and comments. Second, the optical character recognition is moderately noisy, with broken words and substituted letters throughout; proper names and dates in this assessment are given as the article renders them and have not been independently verified. Barry's placing of the found sword "behind the Sainte Marie de Fierbois altar-table" differs in the church's name from the form most commonly encountered, but that is the article's own wording as extracted, and this desk has not corrected it.
