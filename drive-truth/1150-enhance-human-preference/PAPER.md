## Premise

The volume is the report of a committee that the Army Research Institute asked the National Academy of Sciences to form in 1984. According to its preface, the committee was asked to examine the potential value of a class of techniques that had been proposed to enhance human performance, techniques the preface describes as extraordinary because they were developed outside the mainstream of the human sciences and were presented with strong claims for high effectiveness. The committee was also asked to recommend general policy and criteria for the Army's future evaluation of enhancement techniques. The report therefore sets out both to judge the evidence for the particular techniques examined and to state the standards by which any such claim should be judged.

## Abstract

This staged assessment concerns the 1988 National Research Council volume Enhancing Human Performance: Issues, Theories, and Techniques, edited by Daniel Druckman and John A. Swets. In 1984 the Army Research Institute asked the National Academy of Sciences to form a committee to examine techniques promoted as capable of enhancing human performance, many of them developed outside the mainstream of the human sciences and presented with strong claims. The committee's report evaluates those claims against the available scientific evidence and sets out criteria for judging future proposals. Its general conclusions require adequate scientific evidence, or a compelling theoretical argument, before a technique is implemented; they reject personal testimonials as a substitute for rigorous evidence, and they call for independent field testing, analysis of how a program would be implemented, and explicit attention to the costs of drawing incorrect conclusions. Its specific findings support some mainstream directions, including mental practice for motor skills and the reduction of stress through information and a sense of control, while finding no supporting evidence for learning during verified sleep, for hemispheric synchronization, or for neurolinguistic programming as a strategy of influence. The chapter on paranormal phenomena concludes that the best available evidence does not justify concluding that extrasensory perception or psychokinesis exists, and it recommends monitoring selected research rather than direct Army involvement. Within the recognised pages, the scan contains the front matter, the preface, all of Part I, and Chapter 9; Part II does not appear in this sequence.

## Content account

### The committee and its charge (front matter, preface, and Chapter 1)

The committee was chaired by John A. Swets. Its membership as printed in the volume comprises Robert A. Bjork, Thomas D. Cook, Gerald C. Davison, Lloyd G. Humphreys, Ray Hyman, Daniel M. Landers, Sandra A. Mobley, Lyman W. Porter, Michael I. Posner, Walter Schneider, Jerome E. Singer, Sally P. Springer, and Richard F. Thompson, with Daniel Druckman as study director. The preface states that the committee first met in June 1985, that its fourteen members were appointed for expertise in areas related to the techniques examined, and that over the following two years it met six times, conducted interviews and site visits, and commissioned ten analytical and survey papers.

Chapter 1, "Introduction," states the committee's task as the preface frames it and adds two further questions the Army Research Institute posed: what criteria are appropriate for evaluating claims for such techniques in the future, and what research is needed to advance understanding of performance enhancement in the areas the techniques address. The chapter describes the Army's needs, including the demands of an all-volunteer force and of complex new technologies, and observes that the Army's ambitious goals, combined with what the chapter calls a reluctance to deal with the complexities surrounding human performance, could make the institution susceptible to claims made by technique developers. It characterises the market for such techniques as a seller's market, quoting an estimate, attributed in the chapter to the Wall Street Journal of 5 August 1986, that companies were spending about thirty billion dollars a year on formal courses and training programs. The chapter then describes several techniques in the promoters' own terms, among them Suggestive Accelerative Learning and Teaching Techniques, neurolinguistic programming, hemispheric synchronization as marketed under the name Hemi-Sync, and the SyberVision videotape system, noting that support for the last of these rests on testimonials rather than experiments. The chapter argues that skill learning is complex and incremental, that effects obtained by many techniques may be short-term effects of the experience itself, and that claims should be evaluated within the frameworks of existing theory and method. It adopts, as guidelines for its evaluations, a standard set of questions drawn from a paper by Hegge, Tyner, and Genser of the Walter Reed Army Institute of Research, asking what changes a technique will produce, what evidence supports its claims, what theory stands behind it, who will be able to use it, what its implications for Army operations are, how it fits with Army philosophy, and what its cost-benefit factors are.

### Findings and conclusions (Chapter 2)

Chapter 2 presents the committee's findings and conclusions in two parts. The general conclusions state that techniques and commercial packages proposed to the Army should be shown to be effective by adequate scientific evidence or compelling theoretical argument, or both; that a technique's utility should be judged in relation to alternatives designed for similar purposes; and that testing should be carried out by investigators independent of a technique's originators or promoters. The chapter states that personal experiences and testimonials are not an acceptable alternative to rigorous scientific evidence. It states that two kinds of evidence should support a decision to implement a technique, successful field tests and an analysis of implementability, and it recommends that the Army Research Institute formalise the ways in which it receives and provides advice about specific techniques and that purchases of commercial packages follow an open-bid procedure based on a standard presentation of objectives, procedures, evidence, and the vendor's record.

The specific findings, as the committee states them, are mixed. The committee finds no evidence that learning occurs during verified sleep, while concluding that presenting material during lighter stages of sleep might alter later waking perception and recall and that the question deserves renewed basic research. It finds little scientific evidence that so-called superlearning programs derive their benefits from elements outside the mainstream of research and practice, while observing that such programs do integrate well-known instructional, motivational, and practice elements. It concludes that mental practice is effective in enhancing the performance of motor skills, that programs purporting to enhance skills by improving visual concentration have not been shown to be effective, and that the effects of biofeedback on skilled performance remain to be determined. It states that its review refutes claims linking differential use of the brain hemispheres to performance, and it finds no scientifically acceptable evidence for the claimed effects of techniques intended to integrate hemispheric activity, giving Hemi-Sync as the example. On stress, it finds that stress is reduced by giving an individual knowledge and understanding of future events and a sense of control, and it finds that biofeedback, while it can reduce muscle tension, does not reduce stress effectively. On influence, it finds no scientific evidence that neurolinguistic programming is an effective strategy for exerting influence. On group cohesion, it finds few scientific studies addressing the relationship between cohesion and performance, notes the possibility of negative effects, and endorses the evaluation studies of the Army's COHORT system. On parapsychology, it finds no scientific justification, from research conducted over a period of 130 years, for the existence of parapsychological phenomena, concludes that there is no reason for direct Army involvement at the time of writing, and recommends that research in certain areas be monitored, including work in the Soviet Union and named work in the United States, with attention to psychokinesis with random event generators and to Ganzfeld effects.

### Evaluation issues (Chapter 3)

Chapter 3 sets out the committee's philosophy of evaluation. It distinguishes two levels of evaluation: examination of the scientific justification for a technique's effectiveness, which the chapter states can come only from basic research, and field tests of a pilot program, which assess feasibility and usefulness. For basic research, the chapter reviews standards for drawing inferences about novel concepts, about causation, about alternative explanations of causal relations, and about the generalisability of causal relations, discussing threats to validity, random assignment, double-blind procedures, placebo control groups, replication, and systematic review including meta-analysis. For field tests, it states that an adequate appraisal requires attention to the organisational context of a program, to a description and explanation of the program's consequences including side effects, and to an explicit assignment of value, which it breaks down into whether the program meets a demonstrable Army need, how likely its results are to transfer to other settings, and how it compares with alternatives, with cost-effectiveness analysis preferred to cost-benefit analysis for comparing programs. The chapter then records the committee's impressions of current Army evaluation practice, which it stresses are impressions rather than conclusions from systematic research: that decision making about sponsorship is diffuse, that pilot programs are not generally implemented well or closely monitored, that little evidence exists of aspirations to state-of-the-art evaluation methods or of requirements for evaluation by persons independent of the program under review, and that weaker evaluations based on testimony will sometimes be preferred to stronger methods because the weaker methods are easier and less disruptive. The chapter closes by considering qualitative approaches that rely on personal testimony, reviewing research on cognitive biases, including belief perseverance, selective memory, errors of attribution, and overconfidence, and concludes that experimental methods facilitate causal inferences better than the alternatives, while not guaranteeing them.

### Paranormal phenomena (Chapter 9)

The scan's sequence passes directly from Chapter 3 to the Part III divider and Chapter 9, "Paranormal Phenomena," at printed page 169; the structure of the scan is described in the errors and anomalies section below. The chapter states its purpose as evaluating the scientific evidence on parapsychological techniques in selected areas. It describes the military interest in such phenomena, including presentations made to the committee by military officers, and records claimed applications ranging from intelligence gathering and communication with submarines to psychotronic weapons and a proposed "First Earth Battalion." The committee states that it restricted its detailed assessment to the areas with large literatures: remote viewing, experiments with random number generators, and Ganzfeld experiments, together with an experiment the committee itself witnessed at Cleve Backster's laboratory.

On remote viewing, the chapter describes the program begun at SRI International by Harold Puthoff and Russell Targ and the striking results originally claimed for it, and then sets out the committee's assessment that the case is, in its words, not just very weak but virtually nonexistent. The chapter explains that the successive trials in the early experiments were not independent of one another, which exaggerated the reported levels of significance; that recalculation by Kennedy, cited in the chapter, left only two of six experiments significant where five had been reported; that the combination of dependent trials and immediate feedback to the subject makes sensory cueing in the transcripts virtually impossible to prevent, following criticisms by Hyman and Kennedy that the chapter endorses; and that the one experiment not open to that objection, by Schlitz and Gruber, is negated by a different flaw, since a member of the target team translated the subject's descriptions for the judging process.

On random number generators, the chapter surveys a review of 332 experiments, of which 71, about twenty-one percent, reported statistically significant results against the five percent expected by chance, and it records the agreement of both a parapsychologist reviewer, Palmer, and a sceptical reviewer, Alcock, that the results cannot be accounted for by chance alone, together with their disagreement about what follows. The chapter emphasises the smallness of the effects: about 50.5 percent hits against a fifty percent baseline in Schmidt's program, and 50.02 percent across seventy-eight million trials in the Princeton program, where the bulk of the significance was due to one subject who contributed twenty-three percent of the data. It records methodological reservations about randomisation, controls, and documentation in both programs, and it describes one experiment by May, Humphrey, and Hubbard as the only near-flawless random number generator experiment known to the committee, noting that it was conducted only once, was reported only in a technical report, and yielded results that were just barely significant.

On the Ganzfeld experiments, the chapter reports Hyman's review of forty-two experiments, which found flaws including multiple testing, inadequate controls for sensory leakage, inadequate randomisation, and statistical errors, and Honorton's rebuttal in the same journal issue; it records that Hyman and Honorton afterwards issued a joint communiqué agreeing that the existing data base was insufficient to support either the conclusion that psi exists or the conclusion that the results are due to artifacts. It also reports Akers's survey of fifty-four experiments selected as the best current evidence, in which eighty-five percent were considered flawed and none methodologically ideal.

The chapter then describes the committee's visit on 10 February 1986 to the Backster Research Foundation in San Diego, where it witnessed a demonstration of procedures for detecting a correlation between the electrical activity of oral leukocytes and the emotional state of the donor. The committee states that what it read and observed does not justify Backster's claim. It records doubts among its members about whether the leukocytes were alive at the time of recording, and it argues that Backster's method, which looks for polygraph deflections first and then seeks an emotional response in their vicinity, with the subject's own confirmation sought afterwards, is subject to illusory correlation and demand characteristics, and that twelve examples of co-occurrence drawn from seven sessions provide no information about whether a correlation exists, since the full record of deflections and of emotional responses would be needed for that judgment.

The chapter's remaining discussion concerns qualitative evidence. It argues that personal experience and testimony are subject to strong biases and that beliefs formed under such conditions tend to resist later disconfirming data, giving two examples: the conditions proposed by a group of British physicists for testing Uri Geller, which the chapter argues amount to giving the alleged psychic control of the experiment, and the spoon-bending or PK parties originated by Houck, whose conditions the chapter describes as precisely those known to create heightened suggestibility. The chapter's conclusions state that the best scientific evidence does not justify the conclusion that extrasensory perception exists, and does not support the existence of psychokinesis; that if the Army wishes to evaluate further experiments, the Army and outside scientists should first arrive at a common protocol, the research should be conducted under that protocol by both proponents and sceptics, and attention should be given to whether any effects found are manipulable and practically applicable; and that the discrepancy between the lack of scientific evidence and the strength of many individuals' beliefs in paranormal phenomena is itself a cause for concern.
