## Premise

The sponsor had asked whether elements of high-technology targets could be sensed by anomalous cognition. ## Abstract

This paper, dated 26 May 1995, presents two exploratory anomalous cognition trials in which a single receiver, identified only as receiver 372, attempted to describe complex high-technology directed-energy systems. In the first trial, conducted in May 1987, the targets were an advanced technology accelerator near Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, a windmill farm at Altamont Pass, and the laboratory's west gate. In the second, conducted in August 1988, the target was a high-power microwave generator housed in a trailer in the New Mexico desert. The author analyzes both trials after the fact with a fuzzy set method that scores a response for accuracy, the share of the target correctly described, and reliability, the share of the response that was correct. He reports weighted accuracy and reliability of 0.67 and 0.63 for the accelerator, perfect reliability for the windmill farm, and reliability rising to 0.80 in the microwave trial. The paper stresses that the analyses were post hoc, that no statistical calculations were performed, and that the trials are offered as hypothesis-forming exploration. Its stated purpose is to explain how these qualitative correspondences, in which large, rapid changes of energy and entropy characterized both targets, inspired the laboratory's later Shannon entropy experiments.

## Content Account

The paper opens by defending exploratory, hypothesis-forming research against a literature that rewards hypothesis testing alone, and it states its limits plainly at the outset: the analyses were all done post hoc, no statistical calculations were performed, and no meaning should be ascribed to the trials beyond the points they are used to illustrate. A footnote records the laboratory's terminology: anomalous mental phenomena for psi, anomalous cognition for extrasensory perception, and anomalous perturbation for psychokinesis, chosen because the terms are descriptive and neutral about mechanisms.

The first trial ran in May 1987. Receiver 372 and a monitor, blind to the sponsor, the target system, and its details, worked four sessions at roughly eight-hour intervals across a twenty-four-hour period beginning on 7 May 1987. They were told that a named individual from the sponsoring organization was in the target area and that two members of the laboratory staff known to them would serve as a beacon at the specific target of interest overnight. The author, as project director, was fully informed. Three targets figured in the analysis, weighted by the sponsor's interest: the Advanced Technology Accelerator, about fifteen kilometres from Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory, at weight 1.0; the windmill farm at Altamont Pass at 0.50; and the laboratory's west gate at 0.25. The paper explains the fuzzy set method at length. Target and response are each treated as fuzzy sets over a universal set of elements, with membership values between zero and one and weighting factors set post hoc by the sponsor and the author; accuracy is the weighted share of the target described correctly, reliability the weighted share of the response that was correct, and their product is the figure of merit used in the laboratory's formal work. For the accelerator, sixty-seven elements were grouped under functions, physical relationships, and objects, weighted 1.0, 0.75, and 0.5 respectively.

The reported results are these. For the windmill farm, everything the receiver said was correct, a reliability of one, and almost all of the sponsor-designated target elements were perceived, an accuracy of 0.94. For the accelerator, the weighted averages were accuracy 0.67 and reliability 0.63; the author notes that the receiver's correct statement about electrons coming down a tube was embedded in a substantial amount of incorrect material, and that accuracy and reliability for physical relationships, 0.36 and 0.31, were at about the level the laboratory's cross-match experience associates with chance. He also observes that responses to the technical targets were technical and the response to the architectural target was architectural, and he concedes that the quality responses to the lesser targets inflated the combined averages beyond what the qualitative correspondence alone would support.

The second trial ran on 24 and 25 August 1988, with the team blind to all details except that an event would take place within the continental United States; a monitor flew to the receiver's East Coast home. The single target was a high-power microwave generator encased in a truck trailer in the New Mexico desert, scored over seventy-two elements. The author reports that accuracy and reliability for functions and objects were consistent with the accelerator trial, that the totals rose because the values for physical relationships roughly doubled, an increase he states he cannot account for, and that reliability rose from 0.63 to 0.80, indicating less incorrect material. The receiver correctly assessed the target's function and, the paper states, correctly identified a beam divergence angle of thirty degrees, while twenty per cent of the function material was incorrect. A third trial in the series, conducted in 1990 against an underground explosion target, was never analyzed because the contract ended; the author reports that the sponsors considered its qualitative correspondence as good as the first two.

The conclusion returns to the paper's stated purpose. Both targets involved large changes of energy in a very short time, and therefore large changes of thermodynamic entropy. The author states that the laboratory's database dating from 1972 holds twelve to fifteen similar examples, with no case of a complete miss on such targets, and that this anecdote, with the post hoc analysis, was a major contributing factor in the design of the Shannon entropy experiments reported in a 1994 paper. He acknowledges that equating thermodynamic and Shannon entropy changes is a significant step, and points to the entropy and information literature for a demonstrated relationship. A substantial discussion is also given to a confound: during feedback for the microwave trial, the receiver was driven past an operating solar power collection facility about three kilometres from the test area, and elements of mirrors and collection devices permeate his response; the paper keeps those elements in the response, at a cost to reliability, and uses the episode to raise questions about target definition and the contribution of feedback. All of these findings, figures, and interpretations are the author's, reported here as the paper states them; this assessment adopts none of them.
