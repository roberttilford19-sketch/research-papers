## Premise

The magazine material was produced to introduce high school readers to the popular subject of psychic phenomena in accessible language. Its cover questions — can a person move objects by concentration, send and receive mental messages, or exercise a sixth sense — frame the issue, and its articles present the people and claims then most associated with the topic: former astronaut Edgar Mitchell's organization for consciousness research, Uri Geller's reported psychokinesis, Cleve Backster's plant-perception experiments, and reported out-of-body experiences. The issue presents these claims as wonders to consider; it is a school magazine feature package, not a scientific report.

## Abstract

This item is a copy of material from *READ*, the Xerox magazine for high school students, devoted to psychic phenomena. The lead article profiles former Apollo 14 astronaut Edgar D. Mitchell, who, the magazine reports, conducted a telepathy experiment during his 1971 Moon flight — concentrating at prearranged times on numbers from a special card deck while four people in different cities attempted to receive them, with results he described as statistically significant — and who afterward founded an organization, rendered in the magazine as EDMA Corporation, to research and test forms of psychic phenomena. The article defines its terms for young readers: psi, with three categories of extrasensory perception, psychokinesis, and spirit communication, and parapsychology as the science that studies them. A second article reports demonstrations attributed to Uri Geller, including a gold ring said to have been cut and twisted into an S-shape by concentration, a watch whose hands were reportedly moved back, and a plant bud said to have withered in twenty seconds, and it states plainly that no one pretends to know how such an ability would work. Further articles present Cleve Backster's polygraph experiments said to show that plants respond to human intention, and accounts of out-of-body experiences by Robert Monroe and Ingo Swann, including a laboratory shelf test. ## Content Account

The surviving contents page lists, besides the cover story, recurring school-magazine features: a piece drawn from *The Waltons* and its basis in a real family's life, a feature titled "The Point of It," a "Will the Real Bad Guys Please Stand Up" item about readers' favorite villains, and a haiku feature. Only the psychic-phenomena articles are substantially legible in the OCR, and this assessment covers those.

The cover article, "Man, He Can Do a Lot of Things: But Can He Move Objects by Concentration?", begins with Edgar Mitchell. It recounts that Mitchell piloted the lunar module on Apollo 14 in 1971 and became the sixth man to walk on the Moon, and that he then turned to investigating consciousness, which the article presents as a world nearer than the Moon and as close as the human mind. The magazine defines consciousness broadly, as believers are said to use it: not only waking functions but the phenomena involving communication and activity outside the five senses — psychic phenomena. It reports that Mitchell formed an organization, EDMA (for Edgar D. Mitchell & Associates) Corporation, to do research and testing in various forms of psychic phenomena, and it introduces the term psi and its three categories: extrasensory perception, psychokinesis, and spirit communication, with parapsychology named as the science that studies psi in all its categories. The article notes the popularity of eerie television programs of the period as evidence of public fascination, and then reports Mitchell's own experiment: while aboard the Apollo 14 spacecraft, at four prearranged times he concentrated on numbers based on cards from a special deck used by parapsychologists, while at the same times four people in different cities on Earth tried to pick up the numbers; the magazine says the receivers picked up the transmissions often enough to produce what Mitchell calls statistically significant results. It attributes to Mitchell the belief that by understanding their psychic selves people can better understand each other and the natural order, and develop a harmony that might help create a better world.

The second article takes up Uri Geller. It opens with drawings of a gold ring progressively split, twisted, and finally S-shaped, and explains that, according to people who say they saw it happen, the ring was sheared through and slowly twisted simply by the power of a man's mind. The article identifies psychokinesis as one kind of psychic phenomenon that EDMA Corporation planned to investigate, and Geller as the man who caused the changes. It describes Geller as a 25-year-old native of Israel well known there for telepathy, whose ability attracted the physician and psychical researcher Dr. A. Puharich, who arranged a trip to America for tests after becoming convinced Geller possessed psychokinetic ability. The article then gives a "report of events" based on an interview with a member of the witnessing group of doctors and scientists, among them Mitchell. In that account, Geller caused the prongs holding a stone in a ring to bend back while the wearer felt nothing; a second ring was cut through on one side and twisted over several hours into the S-shape pictured; a needle was shattered by concentration; the pointed end of a safety pin was bent by rubbing; and outdoors, a green bud held in another person's hand, covered by Geller's hand, was brown and withered within twenty seconds. The article asks directly whether the events were a hoax, answers that Geller was carefully watched by trained scientists and doctors, "an audience not easy to fool," states that nobody pretends to know how the ability works, and describes EDMA's intention to test Geller while monitoring brain waves, heartbeat, and muscular activity — while noting Geller's own statement that his power comes and goes.

The third article presents Cleve Backster's plant experiments. Backster, identified as a lie-detector expert, is said to have attached a polygraph electrode to a houseplant's leaf in 1966 to time how long water took to reach the leaf tip, and to have seen the chart produce the pattern of an emotionally moved person after watering. When he merely thought of burning the leaf, the article reports, the graph's curve shot upward before he acted, as if the plant were afraid. The article summarizes further reported experiments: a selected vegetable fainting on the polygraph before being dropped into boiling water; a surviving plant reacting only when the student who had destroyed its companion plant walked past; plants reacting while tiny shrimp were boiled alive in another room, with reactions diminishing as more shrimp were cooked; and an inventor, Paul Sauvin, said to have started a car in an outdoor parking lot through a plant controlled by his thoughts from twenty-four and a half miles away. The article closes by quoting the suggestion of a "primary form of instant communication between all living things," noting that Soviet scientists were said to be convinced plants possess electrical impulses similar to human nerve impulses, and warning the reader, half in jest, that the wrong thought might make a willow tree weep.

The final legible article covers astral projection. It narrates Robert Monroe's account of floating against his bedroom ceiling and seeing his own body in bed beside his wife, and his conclusion that the experience was real. It explains the belief, attributed to some scientists in psychic research, that in an out-of-body state the spirit or soul leaves the body, can look back at it, and can travel to other locations, even thousands of miles, while the body remains in a coma-like state with breathing and organs functioning. It then presents Ingo Swann, whose experiences are said to have occurred in a laboratory under observation: seated in a dimly lit room, Swann reportedly entered a trance and afterward described three objects — an umbrella, a black leather case, and an apple — placed on a shelf above him out of his view. The article states that scientists cannot say for sure that Swann's spirit actually left his body, that there is no definite proof, and that such an experience is considered possible if a person believes in having a soul separate from the body. It closes with a warning that astral projection can lead to harm without a trained person nearby, including the risk that the comatose-seeming body could be mistaken for dead.
