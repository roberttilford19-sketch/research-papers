## Premise

The document is a paper by G. Feinberg of the Department of Physics, Columbia University, titled "Precognition — A Memory of Things Future?", headed "Appendix 1" in the released copy and marked for presentation at the Conference on Quantum Physics and Parapsychology in Geneva, Switzerland, on August 26–27, 1974. It sets out to ask whether the known equations of physics leave room for precognition, and it proposes a specific, testable model: that precognition, if it occurs at all, works like memory directed toward the future rather than like perception directed toward distant events.

## Abstract

Feinberg argues that the fundamental equations of physics are symmetric in time, and that physicists exclude effects running backward in time not because the equations forbid them but because such effects have not been observed. From the wave equation, he explains two families of solutions: the familiar retarded solution, in which a disturbance radiates outward after its source acts, and the advanced solution, in which effects precede their source. He notes that advanced waves, if they exist, must be far weaker than retarded ones, since otherwise striking astronomical anomalies would long ago have been seen, and he describes a proposed experiment by Professor Riley Newman of the University of California to detect a small advanced component in light from a sharply timed source. He then builds his model on an analogy with short-term memory. If the oscillatory brain pattern set up by a stimulus has an advanced part as well as a retarded part, a person might in principle "remember" a stimulus before it occurs. From this model he derives testable consequences: a person could precognize only what he will later learn through ordinary perception; the effect should decay over a short time range comparable to short-term memory; it should not depend on the distance or shielding of the target event; and it should be impossible to precognize events occurring after one's own death. He closes by proposing experiments, including trials whose results are never revealed to the subject, that could confirm or refute the model.

## Content Account

The paper runs to eighteen numbered pages plus a short reference list, and it proceeds in five parts. An opening section, partially garbled in the scan, frames the central claim: time-symmetric theories do not forbid phenomena akin to precognition but rather suggest them, and the exclusion of reversed cause and effect is an observational habit rather than a theoretical necessity. The first numbered section, "Time Symmetry of the Equations of Physics," develops the wave-equation argument in detail, including the Mars example: if advanced effects were as strong as retarded ones, astronomers would see doubled images of planets displaced by twice the light-travel time, and solar prominences would appear to erupt twice. Since no such phenomena are reported, advanced waves are at most a small fraction of retarded ones, which Feinberg observes is consistent with precognition being a weak and unreliable channel compared with perception and memory.

The second section, "A Model for Precognition," presents the future-memory model and lists five qualitative features that follow from it, including the possibility of "precognitive chains," in which a recorded prediction itself becomes a stimulus that can be precognized earlier still, pushing warning time backward step by step. The third section, "Tests of the Future Memory Model of Precognition," proposes a literature search for accurate predictions made under conditions that excluded later ordinary knowledge, controlled experiments varying whether and when results are revealed to the subject, and tests of whether precognitive ability tracks ordinary memory ability and responds to the same influences, such as drugs, fatigue, age, and training. The concluding section argues that if advanced effects occur in the brain they should also be detectable outside it, addresses the objection that advanced effects would generate causal paradoxes, and suggests investigating how much reported extrasensory perception could be explained as ordinary perception combined with precognition. The references cite J. B. Rhine's *The Reach of the Mind* (1947) and Feynman and Wheeler's 1945 *Review of Modern Physics* paper. Every substantive claim in this account is the paper's own; this assessment adopts none of them.

## Errors and Anomalies

One instance of the release stamp is misread as "2090/08/10" where the remaining stamps read "2000/08/10." The document's internal numbering is also uneven: after the opening section, the sections are numbered with mixed conventions (I, then unnumbered transitions, then "I. A Model for Precognition," "IV. Tests," and "VY. Conclusions"), which appears to be an artifact of the original typescript and the scan rather than missing pages, since the argument runs continuously across the eighteen pages. The byline gives only an initial, "G, Feinberg," with the Columbia University physics affiliation; the fuller identification of the author is not stated anywhere in the document itself.
