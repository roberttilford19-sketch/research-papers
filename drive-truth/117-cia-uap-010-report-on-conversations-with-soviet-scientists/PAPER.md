## Abstract

This paper assesses a declassified Central Intelligence Agency information report dated 18 August 1967, report number OO-B-321/23490-67, which records conversations about unidentified flying objects held during a one-month trip through the Soviet Union in May 1967. The source was a United States astrophysicist visiting Soviet astronomical observatories, and the report was prepared with the help of an Air Force representative from a memorandum the scientist wrote for his superiors. The report describes visits to the Sternberg Institute in Moscow, the Pulkovo Observatory in Leningrad, the Main Astronomical Observatory in Kiev, the Crimean Astrophysical Observatory, and the Astrophysical Institute at Alma Ata. Its central finding is that the UFO problem had received no official treatment in the Soviet Union, that Soviet astronomers generally relied on Donald Menzel's debunking book to regard the subject as closed, and that interest varied sharply by institution, from indifference at Kiev and Crimea to considerable interest at Alma Ata. Individual astronomers reported a curious reddish object over the Ukrainian countryside, sightings in the northern Soviet Union that newspapers would not print, and one senior astronomer's opinion that UFOs might originate on Venus. The report closes by judging that Soviet astronomers might respond to evidence more readily than their American counterparts.

## Content account

The report's findings, in its own order, are these. At the Sternberg Institute in Moscow, V. I. Moroz was noncommittal on the subject of UFOs, and the radio astronomer serving as translator stated emphatically that he knew of no sightings of UFOs in the USSR, adding with a laugh that if they were only seen in the US, they must be of Soviet origin. At the Pulkovo Observatory in Leningrad, one astronomer mentioned that she had heard of some sightings of unidentified objects near the Caucasus; she was unaware of any study being made of them but was open-minded on the subject. The scientist's main visit was to N. A. Kozyrev, who was very interested in the problem. Kozyrev had read Menzel's book, which had been translated into Russian, but did not accept its conclusions. He knew of some sightings in the northern part of the USSR, but said reports of such sightings are not printed in Soviet newspapers because they are not regarded as scientific observations. He readily accepted the reality of US reports of UFOs, and it was his personal opinion that the UFOs may originate on Venus.

At the Main Astronomical Observatory in Kiev, little interest was shown in the UFO question. I. K. Koval mentioned that he and several other astronomers had been out in the countryside one evening and had seen a curious, reddish object flashing through the sky that they were convinced was neither a satellite nor a meteorite; their immediate interpretation, however, was that the object might have been a fragment of a satellite or rocket returning from orbit and burning up in the atmosphere. Koval thought UFOs had been seen in the USSR but could not be specific as to locations and characteristics. At the Crimean Astrophysical Observatory, again little interest was shown; one of the astronomers, L. I. Galkin, had been with the group mentioned by Koval and confirmed the observation of a peculiar object, and he had clearly been struck by the possibility that the object might have been a "saucer."

At the Astrophysical Institute at Alma Ata, there was considerable interest. The director, G. M. Idlis, had read Menzel's book and considered it an adequate treatment of the subject, which he consequently felt was closed; he indicated that no observations of UFOs had been made by his staff and that they had received no reports of such observations. However, when the US scientist described the results of McDonald's critical study of the problem, Idlis readily conceded that there might be more to it and concluded by stating that it was "clearly still an open question." Another astronomer mentioned repeated sightings of ball lightning in the northern part of Kazakhstan, which a university team had investigated and found to be reflections of automobile headlights from an inversion layer. A later discussion with G. S. Lifschitz, who headed the institute's Atmospheric Optics Section, found him not very impressed with Menzel's book but also not impressed with the significance of the phenomenon. A stellar spectroscopist at the institute was also interested in the problem, partly as an adjunct to her attempts to locate fragments from bolides; she too was dissatisfied with Menzel's book and felt there was definitely an opportunity for additional research.

The report's own interpretations, stated as the visiting scientist's general impressions, are kept separate here from the findings above. The report concludes that no official treatment of the UFO problem had been given in the USSR, and that people instead referred to US work, principally Menzel's book, to demonstrate the absence of real scientific problems. At the same time, it records an almost universal awareness of the history and characteristics of the phenomenon, often accompanied by considerable interest. Its final judgment is interpretive: that a demonstration of the inadequacy of US official explanations, coupled with some proof of the reality of the observations, might excite enthusiasm more readily among Soviet astronomers than among their US counterparts, who were more influenced by the official ridicule associated with UFOs in the US. An editorial comment in the report likewise offers interpretation rather than observation: it calls Kozyrev a controversial figure, notes that one of his questionable observations, the activity of the central peak in the lunar crater Alphonsus, had apparently turned out to be correct, and cautions that the intensive search for lunar activity it provoked may have no connection with the original observation.

## Errors and anomalies

Several anomalies were spotted in a real read of the document and its file. First, the PDF is encrypted with AES-256 and its permissions allow printing but not copying, which is why the text layer would not extract: a direct text extraction of the downloaded file returns the same 2 bytes as the original on-disk extract, and the content of this assessment therefore rests on optical character recognition of the rendered pages. Second, the file's embedded metadata dates its creation to June 2026 and names the Adobe Acrobat Paper Capture Plug-in as producer, and the surviving top edge of page one carries a partially cut declassification stamp reading "Approved for Release 2026"; the file in Drive is thus a modern re-scan and release copy of the 1967 report, not a 1967 artifact. Third, page one is a dark, screened halftone copy of noticeably poorer legibility than page two, and optical character recognition of page one alone yielded only 859 characters against 3,825 for page two; a handwritten annotation beside the report block on page one, apparently a file number ending in "-64," could not be read with confidence. Fourth, the optical character recognition misread the report number as "00-B-321" where the printed page reads "OO-B-321," a reminder that single characters in the OCR text are candidates, not settled readings. Fifth, within the document itself, the report is internally consistent on its face: the header states two pages and the PDF holds two pages, the country, subject, date of information (May 1967), and place and date of acquisition agree with the body's account of a spring 1967 trip, and no contradiction between the header block and the body was spotted.

## Limits

This assessment is limited in the following ways. It rests on one pass of optical character recognition over a screened, second-generation scan, cross-checked against the rendered page images; most wording is clear, but isolated words and one name, the Alma Ata spectroscopist's surname, remain uncertain in the OCR text and are not relied on for any claim here. The document is a single source: it relays one unnamed American scientist's recollection of conversations, prepared by an Air Force representative, and the Agency itself labeled it unevaluated information, so nothing in it is treated here as corroborated fact about Soviet science or about UFOs. No independent check was made against other copies of this report that may exist in public CIA collections, and the assessment makes no judgment about the truth of the sightings the report describes. The assessment covers only this release copy; any fuller or less redacted version of the report is outside its scope.
