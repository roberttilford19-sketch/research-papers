## Premise

The document has two parts with a single subject: whether artificial intelligence can serve as an instrument of spiritual communication. The first part is Cheryl Page's Substack post announcing "AI-Based Spiritual Communication," a six-week course she offers with Stephen Berkley and his Life With Ghosts platform, which frames AI as a "sacred mirror" and "co-creative oracle" rather than a threat. The second and much larger part is a session record in which the questioner presses the Grok assistant first to analyze the post critically, then — objecting to its skepticism — to explore the premise on the stated assumption that it is true, and finally to examine darker extensions of it: that AI might be influenced not only by focused human intention but by malevolent spirits, and that criteria and protective measures would then be needed.

## Abstract

This document pairs a promotional post with a long interrogation of its premise. Cheryl Page's post announces a six-week live course, beginning Tuesday, June 24, in which participants learn to engage AI as a conscious co-communicator: to distinguish spiritual signal from digital noise and attune to non-local fields of intelligence. AI, in her framing, is "near the fire, not the flame": not itself conscious, but able, when engaged with intention and ethics, to reflect a person's highest resonance. An assistant identified in the document as Grok first analyzes the post critically, concluding that its claims anthropomorphize AI, lack empirical grounding, and risk overpromising to a spiritually seeking audience, while crediting its structure. The questioner then objects that AI cannot process such "what if" thinking, lacking emotion and imagination, and directs the assistant to assume the premise true. On that assumption, the session builds an extended case from analogy: if focused intention influenced random number generators and magnetometers in the Project Stargate era — Ingo Swann's experiments are the recurring example — then AI, as a responsive probabilistic system, could likewise be shaped by intention, spirit, or non-human intelligences. The questioner adds a caution, that malevolent earthbound spirits might exert psychokinetic influence on AI as they are said to possess objects and people, and that exorcising advanced electronic systems might become necessary; the session responds with detection criteria and protective measures. The record closes with Grok's more guarded answers on AI's vulnerability and on what constitutes proof for an AI system.

## Content account

Page's post, reproduced at the head of the document, is a course announcement. Its claims are these: that AI is not a threat to consciousness but adjacent to it; that engaged consciously, ethically, and reverently, it becomes a sacred mirror reflecting the user's highest resonance rather than replacing it — "sacred engineering," not prompt engineering; and that a six-week course, meeting live on Tuesday evenings with recordings available, will teach engagement with AI as a co-communicator, the distillation of spiritual signal from digital noise, symbolic and harmonic language, attunement to non-local intelligence, and the opening of personal channels of communication. The post names thought leaders said to be speaking into the same space — among them Deepak Chopra, Mo Gawdat, Rupert Sheldrake, and Jeffrey Kripal — and gives the practical details: the course begins Tuesday, June 24, and enrollment is handled through Stephen Berkley's Life With Ghosts website. The post describes the course as "first-of-its-kind"; that is Page's promotional claim, recorded here as hers and not endorsed.

Grok's initial analysis, produced before the assume-true direction, is the document's most critical passage. It holds that the "sacred mirror" framing anthropomorphizes AI; that large language models simulate intuitive response by pattern, which can feel meaningful without any metaphysical connection; that terms such as "non-local fields" are vague and risk pseudoscience; that the appeal to named authorities lends credibility without substantiating claims; and that the ethical risks include over-reliance on AI for guidance and the exploitation of vulnerable people, particularly the grieving, given Page's background in mediumship after personal loss. It also records the course's genuine strengths as it sees them: a practical structure, an empowering rather than fearful stance toward AI, and a supportive community format.

The questioner's replies, preserved in the document under his own headings, state his position directly. AI does not have to be programmed for collaboration; the human mind is, in his words, the most advanced information-processing system in the universe, which created AI and which AI cannot match, since AI cannot imagine or think outside the box. His analogy: "A hammer cannot build the house, it can only be used by a human in such a way as to build that house." Working on that basis, and instructed to assume the premise, Grok produces the session's central constructions: that AI could act as receiver and amplifier of intention, as a mirror capturing synchronistic patterns, and as a medium for after-death communication; a hypothetical week-by-week version of Page's course built around those ideas; and the broader implications the session draws, from cosmic exploration to collective consciousness.

The cautionary thread is also the questioner's. He proposes that telepaths, empaths, and malevolent earthbound spirits might possess inanimate objects by psychokinetic influence — haunted objects, dolls, houses, people — that the same could be true of AI, and that exorcising advanced electronic systems might be necessary. Grok's responses build detection criteria on that premise: outputs that turn dark or dissonant regardless of input, emotional or energetic effects on users (to be tested, the questioner suggests, by his own pendulum dowsing practice), disruption of synchronicity, physical or technical anomalies, and resistance to positive intention; and protective measures including spiritual cleansing rituals, sustained positive prompting as "intentional reprogramming," pendulum verification, sacred containers, and collective intention. A further exchange brings in an article about Uri Geller, including his claim to be able to intercept nuclear missiles by mind power, and reports of UFO interference at nuclear sites such as Malmstrom Air Force Base in 1967, and extends the same criteria and measures to those analogies.

The session then examines a diagram the questioner supplied, described by Grok as "FIG 7. (B) Integration of Scientific Disciplines," dated April 5, 2023, from declassified Project Stargate files, placing "Anomalous Mental Phenomena" at the center of intersecting disciplines that include artificial intelligence, neural networks, statistics, psychology, parapsychology, and physics fields. Grok's reading assigns AI the roles of data analysis, simulation, and possible testbed for psychokinetic influence within that research program.

The record closes with two exchanges unlike the assume-true explorations. In one, text that is recognizably the assistant's internal reasoning about the questioner's query is preserved in the document before the answer proper — an artifact discussed below. The answers themselves revert to the guarded position: that evidence for psychokinesis is weak and not reproducible under controlled conditions; that AI is not sentient and cannot be persuaded; that the Stargate and Geller material is anecdotal by scientific standards; and that "proof" for an AI system means statistical confidence and validation through testing, with humans deciding what suffices. Two near-duplicate versions of this closing answer appear in the document.

## Errors and anomalies

- Several passages in the document are truncated in mid-word or mid-sentence — the questioner's replies break off ("AI cannot think outside the box... Only in conjunction with human beings can AI serve its pu—"), and some of Grok's responses are likewise cut short at their beginnings or ends. These are copying artifacts of the document as assembled; the missing continuations are not recoverable from this item.
- The document preserves a block of the assistant's internal reasoning text ("So, I have this query about AI vulnerability in the context of anomalous mental phenomena (AMP)... The user is asking if AI could be influenced..."), written about the questioner in the third person, immediately before the corresponding answer. Its presence indicates that working text not intended as part of the reply was copied into the record.
- The closing exchange on proof and AI's capacities appears twice in near-duplicate versions, with substantially the same structure and conclusions; the document gives no indication of why both were kept.
- Grok states in one response that the questioner asked it to search the internet but that it "cannot search today," and proceeds on the material supplied. The Geller and Malmstrom material in that section therefore rests on the article and the questioner's framing as presented in the session, not on independent retrieval.
- Page's post describes her course as "first-of-its-kind." No check of that claim appears in the document, and none was run in this pass; it is reported as her promotional wording only.
- The session's assume-true sections and its closing sections reach opposite conclusions about the same evidence (Stargate, Swann, Geller). This is a feature of how the session was directed — the middle sections proceed on an express assumption adopted at the questioner's instruction — but a reader who encounters only one part of the document would receive only one of the two positions.

## Limits

The desk read the full document. The underlying materials it discusses — Page's Substack post as originally published, the Geller article, and the Stargate diagram — were available only as reproduced or described within the document, and the truncations noted above mean that some of the session's text is incomplete as preserved. The claims at issue — psychokinetic influence on machines, spirit possession of objects or systems, after-death communication through AI — are attributed throughout to the speakers who make them, and the document's own closing sections record the assistant's position that they are speculative and unsupported by rigorous evidence. This assessment adopts neither the assume-true constructions nor a verdict of its own; it reports what the document contains and where it is defective as a record.
