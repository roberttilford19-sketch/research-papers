## Abstract

This twenty-nine-page released file is headed by John G. Fuller's magazine account "The Adventures of Two 'Kidnapped' Humans," a condensation of his book *The Interrupted Journey* presenting the case of Betty and Barney Hill. It recounts how the Hills, driving home to Portsmouth, New Hampshire, on the night of 19 September 1961, observed a bright moving light near Lancaster, how Barney Hill, watching through binoculars, reported figures at the windows of a large craft, and how the couple arrived home with a period of time they could not account for, troubled afterward by nightmares and anxiety. It describes their treatment by Boston psychiatrist Benjamin Simon, who over some seven months of psychotherapy, including time regression under hypnosis, recorded their separate accounts on tape. Under hypnosis, both described being taken aboard a craft and examined; Betty Hill's account includes a needle examination, a conversation with a leader, and a book she was promised and then denied. The account also presents Simon's stated position: that hypnosis is a pathway to the truth as the patient believes it, that the Hills were not psychotic and told what they believed to be truth, and that nothing was finally proved to him regarding the alleged abduction. Pages sampled beyond the condensation show unrelated companion material in the same file, including German-language magazine pages on the 1973 UFO wave, an Air Force sighting questionnaire, and an article by J. Allen Hynek. The Hills' narrative is Fuller's reporting of their hypnotic-regression testimony, attributed as such here.

## Content Account

The condensation opens by framing the case through its psychiatrist. Nightmares and crippling anxiety, it states, drove Betty and Barney Hill to the office of Dr. Benjamin Simon, a Boston psychiatrist and neurologist who had been chief of neuropsychiatry at Mason General Hospital during the Second World War and had extensive experience with hypnosis in treating military psychiatric disorders. The Hills were disturbed by the haunting memory of an incident outside Lancaster, New Hampshire, and plagued by the feeling that something more had occurred that they could not recall; fearing ridicule, they had kept the experience relatively secret until the strain began to affect them physically and emotionally. Simon accepted them as patients, and during the months that followed they began, under individual psychotherapy including periods of time regression under hypnosis, to relive the adventure; their words were recorded on tape and are transcribed in the article with Simon's analysis and comment.

A prefatory passage explains how the account came to be published. A Boston newspaper, in a series of articles, had disclosed Simon's participation in the case; the doctor states that he never saw the reporter, refused to be interviewed, and strongly disagreed with conclusions in the articles, and that the Hills, who had also refused interviews, were considerably distressed. Simon decided that the Hills' emotional health would best be served by releasing the tapes to Fuller so an authentic version of their story might be told, on the condition that he would have complete approval of their use and of any medical data, with assurance the records would be used accurately and not detrimentally to his patients. The magazine's own framing, reproduced in the file, warns that some readers will find the Hills' account incredible and others vivid and persuasive, and it presents Simon's assessment: that some aspects of the experience are unanswered and perhaps unanswerable, that nothing is finally settled and nothing absolutely proved to him regarding the alleged abduction, that neither patient is psychotic and that both, consciously and under hypnosis, told what they believed to be absolute truth — with the caution that hypnosis is a pathway to the truth as it is felt and understood by the patient, which may not be consonant with non-personal truth, although most frequently it is.

The conscious narrative follows the drive. On 19 September 1961 the Hills began a night drive from the Canadian border down U.S. Route 3 through the White Mountains toward Portsmouth, New Hampshire. Just after ten, in the upper Connecticut River valley, Betty noticed a particularly bright star; south of Lancaster another, bigger light appeared, which seemed to grow as she watched. She pointed it out to Barney, who at first took it for a satellite gone off course. They stopped to walk their dachshund, Delsey, and to look more closely; Betty took binoculars from the car. In the later portions of the narrative sampled for this assessment, Barney's close observation through the binoculars is the turning point: he described figures at the craft's windows, tried to pull the glasses from his eyes and could not at first, and remembered the eyes of one crew member who stared down at him. He broke away, ran screaming back across the field to Betty and the car, jammed the car into gear, and drove off shouting that he was sure they were going to be captured, ordering Betty to watch for the craft, which was nowhere in sight. They then heard a strange electronic beeping, seemingly from behind the car; the car vibrated, they each felt an odd tingling sensation, and a kind of daze overcame them. Sometime later they heard a second, more regular pattern of beeps, their awareness slowly returned, and they found themselves still in the moving car, silent, numb, and somnambulistic, much farther along the road — the missing interval at the center of the case.

The aftermath material sampled covers the couple's attempts to test what had happened. Betty telephoned her sister, Janet Miller, who raised the possibility that the car had been exposed to radiation if the object had hovered over it, and relayed a physicist neighbor's advice about an ordinary compass. Betty found about a dozen shiny circles, each about the size of a silver dollar, scattered on the car's trunk; a compass placed on the spots reacted sharply, while on the side of the car, where no spots appeared, it reacted normally. Barney, whose skepticism had stiffened, finally relented and fetched the compass. Betty also called the Air Police at the air base at Portsmouth — the file's text identifies it as a Strategic Air Command installation — and gave an officer the facts in bare outline; the officer became more interested when she mentioned fins separating at the sides of the craft with red lights, asked to speak with Barney, and mentioned other, similar reports. Barney avoided mentioning the figures he had observed or the shiny spots, but found the call reassuring. Still struggling to correlate fantasy and fact, the Hills each drew sketches of the object in separate rooms. Investigator Walter Webb also appears in the later narrative: the Hills retraced the route of the journey with him and were convinced they had found the exact spot of the roadblock on a side road two or three miles east of Route 3.

The hypnotic material is the condensation's bulk. Barney's transcripts, as sampled, show him under hypnosis reliving the binocular observation in acute distress: describing a figure telling him, without moving lips, not to be afraid and that he would be safe; breaking into sobbing and screaming for a weapon; describing the leader by his eyes — "I've never seen eyes like that before" — and a second figure who looked friendly and smiled over the leader's shoulder. He describes a group of men standing in a brightly lit road, all in dark jackets, the leader in a black shiny jacket with a scarf, the others moving to levers and a big board. Betty's transcripts, as sampled, describe her examination aboard the craft: a long needle, four to six inches, with a tube attached, inserted briefly, the pain compared to a knife, and her gratitude to the leader for stopping it. She describes trying to explain age, years, days, and hours to the leader, who laughed and asked what a year was; being shown or given a book with dots and straight and curved lines, which she wanted to keep as proof and was at first told she could have; the leader's promise, when she said other people could answer his questions if he came back, that "if we decide to come back, we will be able to find you. We always find those we want"; and her fury when, as Barney was brought in, the others took the book back despite the promise. She explains to Simon that she wanted to keep the experience secret because the leader told her to forget it, and that she wanted to please the leader because he told her to forget. Barney's aboard-craft account, sampled alongside, describes being afraid to open his eyes because he had been told not to, feeling the occupants examining him with their hands and counting his spinal column, and something touching the base of his spine.

The condensation's closing movement samples the therapy's end. In joint playback sessions over several weeks, the Hills listened to the tapes together, inconsistencies and paradoxes were re-examined, and the playback released into their conscious minds further details not expressed under hypnosis. Barney remembered the occupants' odd-shaped heads, large cranium diminishing toward the chin, eyes continuing around the sides of the head, and a mouth like one horizontal line with a short perpendicular line at each end. The final hypnosis session frames three points of view, which the article states plainly: Simon could accept the probability that the Hills had had an experience with an unusual aerial phenomenon — a sighting that stimulated an intense emotional reaction — while holding the abduction itself improbable; he had decided the Hills were not lying and thought it highly improbable that both were suffering markedly similar hallucinations, and he weighed the possibility that Betty had absorbed Barney's material, then, after her second trance, that the reverse might be true. Betty felt the hypnosis had demonstrated marked evidence that her dreams were a reflection and remembrance of reality. Barney vacillated, concluding that he could not distinguish between other known reality and the sequence that came out under hypnosis; once the amnesia was overcome, he could sense no difference between what he remembered consciously and what he recalled under hypnosis. Both the doctor and the Hills regretted that continuing into deeper therapy over the long period necessary would be impractical, and the article closes with the question of illusion or reality expressly left to rest with the Hills.

The file does not end with the condensation. Sampled pages beyond it show companion material whose connection to the Hill account is not explained in the pages read: advertising and automotive pages from the same magazine issue; a German-language magazine article on the 1973 UFO wave, discussing sightings including the Pascagoula case and a weather-balloon explanation offered by the Hamburg observatory, under a Der Spiegel heading dated October 1973 as read in the text; a United States Air Force Technical Information sighting questionnaire (Form FTD 164, superseding the July 1961 edition as read in the text); and an article by J. An illustration caption in the sampled pages describes the disc-shaped Avrocar developed by Avro Aircraft of Canada, first released in 1960 and abandoned in 1965 because the craft could not rise more than a few feet with stability. ## Errors and Anomalies

The optical character recognition text is the most difficult in this batch. The magazine's two-column layout interleaves constantly in the recognition, so sentences from adjacent columns braid together and individual passages must be reconstructed by sense; illustration captions, cartoon text, and advertising copy are mixed into the body; and stretches of the later pages — particularly the German-language article, whose umlauted characters the recognition handles badly, and pages of dense tabular or form material — degrade into near-illegibility. Names and quotations are given in this assessment only where they read cleanly and consistently in the sampled text. The file itself is anomalous as a unit: a released Agency file whose pages move from an American magazine condensation to German magazine pages, an Air Force questionnaire, and a Hynek article, with no covering explanation present in the pages read. Whether that assembly reflects the Agency's original file or the order of scanning cannot be determined from the text layer. The accounts inside the principal document are themselves, by the document's own framing, hypnotic-regression testimony: Simon's caution that hypnosis yields the truth as the patient believes it is printed in the file and is carried in this assessment.
