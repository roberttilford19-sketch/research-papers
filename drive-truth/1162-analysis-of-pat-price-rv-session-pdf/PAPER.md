## Premise

The document was produced to judge, from the recorded evidence alone, whether Pat Price's remote viewing of the target designated URDF-3 in July 1974 had yielded information that was both original to Price and correct about the site. Its author states the test he applied throughout: anything the experimenters had already told Price could not count as evidence, and objects Price reported that did not exist at the site counted against the experiment, whether or not other statements matched.

## Abstract

This document is a United States government analyst's day-by-day review, dated 4 December 1975, of a four-day remote-viewing experiment in which Pat Price attempted to describe a target designated URDF-3. The analyst worked from two cassette tapes, seventy-nine pages of tape transcripts, and thirty sketches, and compared Price's statements against the analyst's own familiarity with the target site. The review finds that Price correctly repeated information the experimenters had already given him, including the target's location southwest of the Irtysh River and its character as a scientific military research area, while his building layouts, dimensions, and landmark descriptions repeatedly failed to match the site. A swimming pool, a tall antenna, telephone poles, a village, and a dominant three-story building are listed among objects reported but not present. One item is treated as the experiment's strongest positive evidence: Price's second-day sketch of a rail-mounted gantry crane closely resembled the actual crane, although his account of how the crane related to the underground building beneath it was wrong. Shown a perspective sketch of the real site on the third day, Price described its underground building as four separate buildings on a concrete apron. The analyst concludes that the experiment was unsuccessful and recommends tighter controls in future work.

## Content Account

The analysis proceeds day by day. On the first day, 9 July 1974, at the Stanford Research Institute, the experimenters Russell Targ and Hal Puthoff gave Price the target coordinates, showed him maps placing the target about sixty miles west-southwest of Semipalatinsk and twenty-five to thirty miles southwest of the Irtysh River, and described it as a scientific military research and test area. The analyst credits Price with an early general impression of low, partly buried buildings, then records a series of mismatches: a road through a gorge, a five-hundred-foot antenna, an outdoor pool, an array of telephone poles, an airstrip, a village, a city sixty miles away, and a dominant three-story building, none of which the analyst finds at the site. Price repeatedly deferred specifics with a promise to return to them and twice avoided drawing the perimeter, whose shape the analyst describes as unique.

On the second day, after a telephone briefing that directed Price to the crane and to the main building, Price produced the sketch the analyst calls the most positive evidence of the experiment, a rail-mounted gantry crane closely resembling the real one. The analyst nonetheless notes that Price described two further cranes that do not exist, placed the crane's relationship to the underground building incorrectly, rotated the complex ninety degrees, and reported a dome-shaped building and a silo-like building that are not at the site. The analyst records two explanations for the crane sketch, remote viewing or information supplied by someone who knew the site, and states that the experiment was not controlled in a way that could exclude the second.

On the third day the experimenters showed Price a perspective sketch of the actual operations area, including the gantry crane, the underground building, and four headframes. Price said only one headframe was present, which the analyst states was untrue, and he described the underground building's four surface sections as four separate above-ground buildings. Asked directly whether they might be the surface elements of an underground building, he answered that there was nothing subterranean there. The analyst calls this the most negative evidence yet. The fourth day, 15 July 1974, produced specific statements about the site's overall operation but, in the analyst's judgment, no new checkable evidence.

The conclusions restate the verdict of failure, weigh the single crane sketch against the volume of negative evidence, and raise a practical objection that runs through the document: a user who could not check Price's statements independently would have no way to separate the accurate items from the invented ones. The recommendations are procedural. Future sessions should be recorded on both sides of any telephone conversation, at least one experimenter should know the target thoroughly, and controls should exclude the subject discussing the material with people outside the experiment. The analyst also recommends that the tapes be considered for testing with a psychological stress evaluator, while stating plainly that he is not competent to judge that instrument's reliability. Appendix I is a reprinted article from Science, volume 190, dated 24 October 1975, about that instrument, and is assessed in the anomalies section below.
