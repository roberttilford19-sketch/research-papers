## Premise

The document is the final report, dated December 1987, of an experiment conducted by the Mind Science Foundation in San Antonio, Texas, under subcontract to SRI International, and issued as SRI Project 1291, Objective E, Task 2. Its authors are G. Scott Hubbard and Jessica M. Utts of SRI International (the title page of one copy in the file gives the affiliation as SAI International) and William W. Braud of the Mind Science Foundation, and it was prepared for Peter J. McNelis, the contracting officer's technical representative. The report was produced to establish, under a protocol refined through a pilot study and an intermediate study, whether subjects attempting to protect human red blood cells in a distant room measurably slowed the cells' breakdown, and whether any such result was better explained as a causal remote action or as intuitive data sorting.

## Abstract

Thirty-two subjects each took part in one session of four fifteen-minute periods, two designated as control periods and two as protect periods, in a distant room from the blood samples being measured. During protect periods they used visualisation and other intention strategies to try to retard the hemolysis of red blood cells placed in a hypotonic saline solution; during control periods they were to think of other matters. The experimenter measuring hemolysis was blind to the condition, and subjects were blind to whether two or eight tubes were processed in a period, a variation built in to distinguish a causal remote action from intuitive data sorting. Fourteen subjects were attempting to protect their own blood and eighteen the blood of another person, with subject and experimenter both blind to the source. The report states that nine of the thirty-two subjects showed a statistically significant difference between protect and control periods, seven in the direction of slowing hemolysis and two in the direction of increasing it, and that blood source made no significant difference. It concludes that the data could not decide between remote action and intuitive data sorting, because individual performance was too heterogeneous for the combined analysis either theory required.

## Content Account

### Background and design

The report explains that the Mind Science Foundation had for several years investigated the relationship between an intention to influence a biological system remotely and actual changes in the system, and that such changes had usually been assumed to be caused by the subject through a remote action. In 1985 a competing account, intuitive data sorting, was proposed: on that account, the observed changes result from a psi-mediated sorting of data into experimental and control conditions rather than from any causal influence. A 1986 study of one person's distant influence on another's electrodermal activity had observed significant effects but, by the report's own account, its protocol allowed intuitive-data-sorting effects to enter the condition meant to isolate remote action, so the source of the effects could not be determined. The hemolysis study was designed to repair that defect, building on a preliminary experiment reported in 1979 that had involved a small number of trials with a single subject.

Three phases were run. A pilot study tested the methodology and whether working on one's own blood mattered. An intermediate phase consisted of a salinity study designed to mimic the anticipated changes and a Monte Carlo study that used its results to fix the parameters of the final design; the salinity data were also used at SRI to simulate the results expected if remote action were operating. The confirmation study incorporated the changes indicated by the first two phases, suggestions from the Scientific Oversight Committee, and a site visit by SRI personnel, and the report states that the resulting protocol appears to have been extremely well conducted.

### The confirmation procedure

Each subject's blood had been collected fourteen to forty-two hours before the session and stored at four degrees Celsius. Blood labelling did not go as planned: because of a scheduling change and resulting confusion, fourteen samples carried the donor's own name and eighteen carried another donor's name, so fourteen subjects were in fact protecting their own blood and eighteen another person's, with both subject and experimenter blind to the source until all thirty-two sessions were complete. Subject and experimenter were isolated in rooms in different parts of the building. For each trial, a fixed amount of whole blood was added to a prepared tube of saline in distilled water, and the rate of hemolysis was measured with a spectrophotometer as the change in light transmittance over sixty seconds. Subjects were signalled at the start of each period, consulted a preassigned order to learn whether it was a protect or a control period, and were given several techniques for protecting the blood, including a slide of intact red blood cells as an aid to visualisation. The assignments of conditions and of tube numbers were prepared in advance by another staff member from a random number table.

### Results as the report states them

The analysis of variance found the largest effect to be the differences among subjects, which the report notes are well known for human red blood cells, and the only other significant effect to be the interaction between condition and subject. Comparisons therefore had to be made subject by subject. Of the thirty-two individual tests, nine reached significance at the level the report specifies, seven in the direction of slowing hemolysis and two in the direction of increasing it. Both subjects who appeared to increase the rate were working on another person's blood, an observation the report itself calls post hoc and too small to support conclusions. The analysis found no significant difference between subjects protecting their own blood and subjects protecting another's.

On the question the study was built to answer, the report is negative. The ratio of variances between the two-tube and eight-tube conditions was 1.75, where all three hypotheses under consideration, chance, remote action, and intuitive data sorting, predicted a ratio close to four. The report concludes that the data contain anomalies none of the theories covers, that the most obvious possibility is that the level of functioning differs from individual to individual, and that future studies of this type should be designed so that each subject's data can be analysed separately, or should use a single subject. The appended report by Braud gives the detailed procedural account of the investigation, including the session routine, the measurement apparatus, the subjects' reported techniques and experiences, and the full analysis tables.
