## Premise

The chapter sets out to show that backward causation, the idea that a later event may exert an influence on an earlier one, is not logically incoherent, and therefore cannot be used as an a priori reason for rejecting precognition. It then argues that the same concept has become a working tool in experimental parapsychology, in the form of tests for retroactive psychokinesis.

## Abstract

This chapter, titled Backward Causation and printed in a book whose running head reads The Relentless Question, defends the coherence of the idea that a later event may influence an earlier one. The author, who delivered an earlier version as a paper at a Parapsychology Foundation conference in Copenhagen in August 1976, takes up three familiar objections: that a cause must by definition precede its effect, that backward causation would allow the past to be altered, and that it would permit a person to intervene so as to prevent his own birth. Following Bob Brier's 1974 monograph, the author distinguishes altering the past, which is agreed to be absurd, from influencing the past, which is not. The chapter then turns to experimental work, describing Helmut Schmidt's random number generator experiments in which a subject attempts to affect a sequence recorded earlier, and a project in the author's Edinburgh department in which the expectation that is said to produce an effect is generated only after the recorded performance is complete. It closes by considering free will, arguing that backward causation does not by itself settle that question, while insisting that a person cannot both precognize his own future actions and decide them freely when the time comes.

## Content Account

### Occasion and acknowledged debts

The author explains that he was invited by the Parapsychology Foundation to take part in a conference on philosophy and parapsychology in Copenhagen in August 1976, and chose backward causation as a suitably controversial topic. He records that Bob Brier had already published a book on the theme in 1974 which he had found persuasive, and that observational theory was beginning to interpret psi phenomena as a retrocausal effect produced at the point of feedback. He acknowledges his debt to Brier, whose monograph Precognition and the Philosophy of Science, subtitled "An Essay on Backward Causation," itself followed the lead of Michael Dummett's 1964 paper "Bringing About the Past" in the Philosophical Review. He also records opposition: Anthony Flew had attacked the notion as logically absurd at an earlier conference and, the author says, refused to recognise the distinction on which the defence rests, while the two philosophers who discussed the Copenhagen paper, Shivesh Thakur and Peter French, seemed amenable to the principle.

### The three objections

The chapter lists the objections it means to answer. The first is verbal: it is part of the meaning of the word cause that a cause should precede its effect, so a cause that followed its effect would be a contradiction in terms. The second is that positing backward causation amounts to asserting that we could alter the past, which has been and cannot be changed. The third is a paradox of intervention: if we could intervene in the past, we could in principle prevent our own birth, and would thereby negate the existence of the person who intervened. Behind all three stands the simpler objection with which the chapter's surviving text opens, that an event which has not yet occurred cannot have effects and cannot be an object of knowledge.

### Realism about time

Against the claim that future events do not exist and so can have no influence on the present, the author argues that the objection begs the question. Many contemporary philosophers of science, he says, take a realist or objectivist view of the temporal order, on which past, present, and future mark a relationship between observer and event rather than a division in the world itself, and on which cosmologies that treat the universe as a single four-dimensional continuum recognise no privileged present instant. He is careful about the weight of this move: he does not claim that the realist view is necessarily correct, only that one cannot simply assume, without argument, that future events are any less real than past or present ones.

### Altering the past and influencing the past

The verbal objection is set aside briefly, on the ground that linguistic usage adapts to new circumstances. The substantial answer is Brier's distinction. The author agrees that backward causation would be nonsense if it meant altering the past, making something to have happened which did not happen, or preventing something which did happen. But it does not follow, he argues, that what happened might not have happened differently but for certain later events. The same symmetry holds for the future: what will be, will be, and in that sense the future cannot be altered either, yet we rightly believe that what will be may depend on what we now do. The paradox of the man who cancels his own birth receives the same treatment. Given that a person was born, nothing later can alter that fact, but it does not follow that his having been born might not have been due to some subsequent action. The author's deliberately homely example is a mother who believes her child would never have been born but for its later baptism; we may mock her credulity, he says, but we cannot fault her logic.

### The pragmatic objection and the cheque in the envelope

Critics also argue that causation is a pragmatic concept, and that any backward causal act must be either superfluous, if the earlier event is already known to have occurred, or futile, if it is known not to have occurred. The author answers with Brier's example of a man who discovers by trial and error that clapping his hands on receiving a letter determines whether the envelope contains a cheque. He then meets the obvious counter, that a transparent envelope would destroy the connection, by arguing that every causal connection holds only under boundary conditions, and that where human action is involved the agent's knowledge or ignorance may be among those conditions, just as knowing that one will be killed tomorrow would make precautions pointless while not knowing makes them rational. His stated conclusion is that there are no logical objections to saying that one event is the cause of another so long as the second would not have happened but for the first, regardless of which is earlier.

### Retroactive psychokinesis

The second half of the chapter moves from hypothetical cases to laboratory work. Because psychokinesis, unlike extrasensory perception, is known only through its effects, the author argues that a forward-directed psychokinetic effect could never be demonstrated unequivocally, whereas a backward one can. The method he describes is precise: a physical effect is automatically recorded at an earlier time and kept secret; later, a subject is instructed to try to produce that outcome on that target system; only then is the result checked against the original record. He identifies this as basically the method used by Helmut Schmidt in experiments on psychokinesis with time displacement, with others, including Janin in 1974, working independently along similar lines. In Schmidt's arrangement a random number generator with a binary output supplies the target, and feedback, in forms such as a recording pen or a fluctuating tone, is not an amenity but, on Schmidt's mathematical model of psi as the author reports it, the part of the process that activates the psi source. To convert such a test into a retroactive one, a single modification is needed: the generator's output is drawn from a solid-state memory replaying a sequence recorded earlier, so that a successful subject must influence the machine not as it now is, but as it was when the sequence was recorded.

### The Edinburgh project and the experimenter effect

The author then describes work by Richard Broughton, identified as one of his postgraduate students in his department at Edinburgh. Broughton's starting point was the experimenter effect, the idea that the experimenter rather than the ostensible subject may be the real psi source behind positive results. His design generates an expectation in subjects that they will score better under one of two conditions, although there is in fact no objective difference between the conditions; only when subjects go to the computer for a printout of their scores are they told which condition was the high-scoring one, the assignment being made arbitrarily by the computer at that point. Any significant difference between the two arbitrarily assigned categories must then, in the design's logic as the author presents it, be due to the subject's retroactive psychokinesis, because the expectation is generated after the recorded performance is complete. The author reports that a pilot experiment along these lines had already produced some significant differences, that Broughton had reported on them to the Parapsychological Association convention at Utrecht in 1976, and that a much larger experiment was planned for the following year with support from the Parapsychology Foundation. Those are the author's reports of work in progress in 1976, and they are recorded here as such.

### Feedback, precognition, and free will

If, following what the author calls the Schmidt axiom, all psi interactions depend critically on feedback, then extrasensory perception also involves backward causation, running from the moment feedback is received to the earlier moment at which the response was given; even precognition would be elicited not by the future event itself but by the later confirmation that it came to pass. The chapter closes with free will. The fear that a knowable future makes a mockery of spontaneous choice is, the author argues, unfounded, because a choice made now could as well have caused a precognition of it the week before as cause it to be remembered the week after. One restriction is, however, imposed: a person cannot both precognize his own future actions and, at the same time, freely decide upon them when the time comes, because deciding implies a state of uncertainty that knowledge would already have terminated. The author expressly declines to claim more than he has argued. He names the intervention paradox as raising quite different questions, and his conclusion is that precognition cannot be dismissed on logical grounds alone.

### The notes

The chapter ends with two long notes, added in a later version. The first qualifies the chapter's opening treatment of C. D. Broad, noting that Broad attempted to rescue literal precognition by considering an additional dimension of time. The second answers later critics, Jule Eisenbud and Stephen Braude, who repudiated backward causation as incoherent. The author concedes that Braude's argument is more subtle than Flew's, since it does not rest on confusing altering the past with affecting it, and states his own reply: he disputes Braude's premise that normal causes are necessarily part of a wider causal network, and offers memory as the obvious model for precognition, an epistemic relationship which, he argues, needs no such network.
