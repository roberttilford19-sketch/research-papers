## Premise

The article was written to report and comment on the continuing official handling of unidentified flying objects, and to argue, through the voices it quotes, that an inquiry remained necessary. It sets the Air Force's public position, that the objects are familiar things mistakenly identified, against the view it attributes to senior intelligence and scientific figures, that a minority of the objects represent something the Air Force has been unable to identify and that the public has not been told the plain truth.

## Abstract

This single-page newspaper article, headlined "Inquiry Into UFOs Still Held Needed," reports that some members of Congress had quietly given support to an investigation of unidentified flying objects, while doubting that a full investigation would follow. It states that the Central Intelligence Agency is the nation's authority on the matter, and it gives prominence to a statement it attributes to the former head of the Agency, Admiral R. H. Hillenkoetter (a reconstruction from the historical record, not a rendering of this copy; the OCR passage gives the name illegibly), to the effect that the Air Force had decided a dozen years earlier that the public might panic if told it did not know what some of the objects were, had said instead that they were misidentifications, and had been stuck with that story since. The article describes the Air Force's scientific apparatus for the subject at length, quoting material addressed to Air Force commanders on space surveillance, the catalogue of artificial satellites, and the Aerospace Technical Intelligence Center, and it identifies Dr. J. Allen Hynek of Northeastern University (as rendered in the OCR text) as the Air Force's chief scientific consultant on the subject. It also treats Hynek critically, suggesting that his role had become one of explaining sightings away and questioning some of his explanations and his use of definitions. The views and quotations summarised here are the article's own reporting and argument, recorded as such and not independently verified by this desk.

## Content Account

The article is a column of reported commentary rather than a straight news report. Its structure moves from the congressional position, to the Agency's central role, to the former director's attributed explanation of how the Air Force's public story arose, and then to an extended description, drawn from material the article says was written for Air Force commanders rather than for the public, of the surveillance and scientific machinery bearing on the subject. That material, as quoted, describes a relationship between the Air Force's interest in space surveillance and its continuing surveillance of the atmosphere near Earth for unidentified objects, the responsibility for aerospace defence, and the scientific group, facilities, and national space surveillance centre involved in watching objects in the sky. The article remarks that the commanders' material treats the subject with more gravity than the Air Force's public explanations of sightings. Its closing treatment of Hynek is sceptical in tone, asserting that he had never failed to explain away a sighting, that some explanations strained credulity, and that his explanations had at points been supplied by the Air Force. Whether those characterisations are fair is a matter the article argues rather than demonstrates, and they are recorded here as the article's argument.
