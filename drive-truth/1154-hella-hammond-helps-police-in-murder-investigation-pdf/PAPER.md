## Premise

The report was written to assess the remote viewing work of Hella Hammid at SRI, both from her earlier recorded results with technical targets and from a three-day visit during which the author observed her, took part in sessions with her, and performed remote viewing experiments himself. It sets out what he saw, how well he judges each performance to have gone, what was wrong with the procedures as he found them, and what he recommends should be done next.

## Abstract

This report, dated November 1974 and headed Experiments With Hella Hammid, records a three-day visit to SRI by an unnamed assessor whose name is redacted in the released copy. The author reviewed Hammid's earlier remote viewing of technical equipment, observed her at work, took part in sessions himself, and attempted to interpret her recorded descriptions of unfamiliar apparatus. The report includes her visit, in the company of a police lieutenant, to the Stanford chapel where a young girl had been murdered, and it states that much of what she said there was corroborated, while cautioning that every verified item was already known to the investigating officer. It includes the author's own three remote viewing attempts, with a full transcript and an item-by-item analysis of the second, and shorter trials with an abacus clock and an ESP teaching machine. Throughout, the author weighs how such sessions should be constrained, first so that apparently paranormal results cannot be manufactured by ordinary means, and then so that the reliability of the data can be improved. He concludes that the phenomena exist, recommends a follow-on programme in two parts, and warns that interrupting support for the SRI work would probably end the opportunity to study it under these conditions.

## Content Account

### The author's summary assessment of Hammid

The report opens with the author's summary assessment, based, he says, on his reading of Hammid's earlier results and on his observation of the work described in the report. He describes her as very alert and outgoing but not insightful, and as very concerned with her performance, aiming, in his judgment, to please the experimenters rather than to prove anything to herself. Her results varied greatly in quality, but included some outstanding successes, one of which he witnessed. He generalises several features: she did very badly on absolute size estimates; she did very well on lighting conditions, including steady or pulsing lights; she did not often describe colours, but was very accurate when she did; and she apparently could not assess her own performance, although she was more pessimistic when she knew the target was technical. She was very willing to try new experiments and to work with someone else. His opening conclusion, stated as his own, is that there can be no question that Hammid could repeatably, although not reliably, produce information not available through normal means, and that she had not shown an ability to assess or increase that reliability.

### The scientific apparatus experiment

The first experiment the author witnessed was an attempt by Hammid to remote view a piece of technical equipment. He selected the target himself, an electric typewriter, at the last possible moment rather than drawing it from the safe. Hal Puthoff accompanied him while he sat at the typewriter, and Russell Targ remained with Hammid as her interrogator. The author rates the overall result a miss, although elements of her drawing and some verbal descriptions were, he says, excellent. Two incidental results struck him as more significant than the miss itself. Targ, who had spent the entire experiment actively questioning Hammid, gave a nearly perfect account of the target when he was himself tasked, which the author says brings into question the whole process of interrogation and its potential for leading the subject. And Targ perceived Puthoff, but not the author, at the typewriter, which the author takes to indicate a strong preferential bond between Puthoff and Targ. He notes that later experiments eliminated the interrogator and sent both men to the site, with quite good results, and he treats Hammid's performance at the chapel as further evidence that the link between the two experimenters was not responsible for the phenomena themselves.

### The Stanford chapel murder investigation

The best-known episode in the report, and the one the Drive filename takes its description from, arose because the author accompanied Puthoff, Targ, and Hammid to the Stanford chapel during his visit. The background section explains that he had been introduced to Hammid only as an interested party, that his attendance was not officially documented, and that he was not specifically identified to the participants. The officer in charge of investigating the murder of a young girl in the chapel some weeks before had exhausted his available leads and, having read recent publicity about the SRI work, had contacted Puthoff and Targ and asked for their assistance. Hammid had agreed to try, although she was sceptical of her ability to help. Tape recordings were made of the entire activity, including the car journeys and the later discussion.

The author reports that Hammid performed extremely well, giving much information that was later corroborated, as well as specific information beyond what the police knew. In his account she gave details of the crime location, described the victim and her assailant, indicated the exact location of the fatal wound, alluded to the ritualistic tone of the crime, and traced the victim's movements before the crime and the relocation of the body afterwards. Of the detail given, the only information he records as known to be incorrect was her statement that the victim was wearing earrings. He states that the excellence of her performance was attested by a letter from the investigating officer to SRI, and by the fact that the police were following up leads she provided. He stresses that this was her only performance to date in which neither Puthoff nor Targ was actively involved, that no special environment or preconditioning was used, that tourists were present, and that every word was recorded.

The same section contains the author's own caution, which this assessment preserves because it qualifies everything above. None of the verified data Hammid generated, he writes, was unknown to the investigating officer at the time, so it is possible that she received all of her information from the officer by some unknown means. Only when the additional facts she provided had been verified, he says, could the conclusion be reached that some technique akin to remote viewing was involved. In his separate personal account of the visit he adds that he received impressions of his own at the chapel, that he briefly worked with Hammid on the description of the victim, and that, when the officer began to question him, he deliberately described aspects of the case that were completely wrong and so drew no further attention.

### Interpreting Hammid's recordings

Two sections of the report describe the author acting as an interpreter of Hammid's earlier recorded sessions with technical targets, work he had begun before the visit. After two runs through a tape he guessed a vertical boring machine for a target that was in fact a drill press, a result he considered significant because it suggested that more information might be present in the data than had been supposed. He identifies two factors that he believes mattered: his own familiarity with both cathode ray tube terminals and vertical milling machines, and his briefing on Hammid's background and personality, including that she was basically untechnical in orientation, capable of detailed description of anything she had seen, not prone to fabricate details, and employed in photography. After several hours with her at SRI, he expected his increased familiarity to help, but his attempt on a second tape, whose target was the ESP teaching machine, failed: his guess of a viewgraph projector rested on arbitrary selections from ambiguous and contradictory statements, and on terminology, such as "burning in," whose meaning for him differed from its possible meaning for her. His conclusion is that calibration on the subject helps, knowing for instance that her sizes are generally overestimated and her drawings often better than her descriptions, but that the interpreter's familiarity with the possible target set is the crucial factor. He compares the task to giving raw intelligence data to an analyst well grounded in the pertinent fields.

### The critique of protocols

The report's longest analytical section is in two parts, and the division between them is the author's own. First, if the wish is to prevent the manufacture of apparently paranormal results through covert use of normal techniques, the constraints must be tight enough that any such attempt would be detected, and on that basis he criticises the practice he found: the outbound experimenter's movements, destination, and perceptions should be documented by tape recording and photographs during the experiment, so that a site cannot be manufactured to fit the subject's description afterwards; no interrogator should be used, and no one should be present with the subject, to avoid collusion; the subject should be free to roam, to avoid subliminal cueing; and the subject should not first appear at SRI, but should go directly to his experiment location and begin at a time given to him in advance in writing or through a third party.

He then states the premise of the second part in his own words: having concluded that the phenomena do exist, which he believes has been justifiably concluded, the constraints should now be shifted to enhance the reliability of the data and make its analysis more meaningful. Under that heading he argues that the preferential link between Puthoff and Targ must be eliminated from experiments, while giving two reasons why its existence is not grounds for dismissing the two men, namely that any pair of people in such experiments appears to establish an increasingly strong link of the same type, and that Puthoff and Targ probably represent the strongest such link yet found and may therefore be of particular value in evaluating the individual-dependent aspects of the phenomena. He recommends that debriefers be unwitting as to the target, that analysis not wait on an interrogator's involvement, and that the drawings be analysed both alone and with the verbal report, with inconsistencies followed up in debriefing. He rejects the word "symbolism" for this data, arguing that it connotes repression and unconscious substitution, whereas the processes he observed were simple extrapolations and analogies, and that what is needed is an understanding of the subject's vocabulary, background, and speech patterns. He recommends simpler targets for calibration, repetition of targets to assess learning and familiarity, recording the subject's satisfaction with his drawing and description before and after feedback, a simple matching analysis in which the subject selects a site or object from a set of ten or fifty, and more real-world problems, both for independent assessment and for the emotional involvement which, he says, seems to enhance performance.

### Recommendations

The author states that the operational utility of the phenomena and of the SRI data had not been addressed in his assessment, since that was not the purpose of his contract, but that any further effort in support of the sponsoring office must address it. He proposes a follow-on in two parts. The first is the identification of methods for increasing the signal-to-noise ratio of remote viewing data and for estimating the attainable ratio, which implies a meaningful definition of that ratio; the effects of calibration, training, repetition, multiple outbound experimenters, groupings of subjects, and the combination or decomposition of targets should be considered, and the phenomena analysed in the way an unknown machine is analysed in order to draw its state diagram. The second is the design and running of tightly constrained experiments to demonstrate the use of redundant coding techniques to transmit coded messages telepathically, with a predetermined degree of reliability, so that the basic utility of paranormal communication can be assessed directly in an operationally useful context. He warns, as his own judgment, that one alternative is not available: if support for the SRI research were not continued without interruption, there would probably be no later chance, because Puthoff and Targ were dedicated enough to publish everything they had generated if forced to seek other funding, and because outside attention was already growing. He describes SRI in this passage as, in his words, the most advanced centre for paranormal research, with an excellent reputation for credibility.

### The introduction and the schedule

An introduction dated November 1974 explains the report's arrangement: a schedule of the author's activities at SRI, a report on each of seven activities, and an extensive analysis of the second remote viewing, each section standing alone. He records that his impressions were favourable with regard to the SRI effort, the existence and hardiness of the phenomena involved, Hammid's credibility and capability, and his own performance, and that he tried to restrain his amazement and excitement in writing, weeks after the events, and not to give himself the benefit of the doubt when relying on memory. The schedule shows three crowded days: arrival, a tour and meetings, his first remote viewing and its debriefing, lunch and a review of critiques of a Nature paper, meeting Hammid and observing her at the Stanford chapel, discussions with Targ and Puthoff, further sessions on the following days, and a flight out on the Friday evening.

### The author's own first remote viewing

In the first experiment using the author as subject, run immediately on his arrival when he was tired and suffering from a bad head cold, Puthoff took a target site from the office safe under the usual protocol while the author remained with Targ and described the images that came to mind. His recorded points included an area some fifty feet from parked cars, a geometrical shape that Puthoff had decided to stay inside, a railing more than waist high, and a sense of looking both out and down with movement past him like a breeze. In debriefing he confessed that he had suppressed his initial impressions of Puthoff standing on a wooden dock with rippling water going past, because the image resembled his own apartment dock and because he had driven around SRI and was certain there was no water nearby. He reports that his suppressed description was very accurate, and that the target was a bridge across a man-made brook on the SRI grounds. His critique of his own session complains that a fifteen-to-twenty-minute session made his mind wander, that the questioning sometimes distracted or misled him, and that the process felt like translating an experience into words, analogous, he says, to smelling something which may recall a fragrance or an entire event. He also sets out the objection he put to himself, that a site might be manufactured after the fact, and the three answers arrived at, ending in the decision to tape the outbound experimenter's complete journey.

### The second remote viewing and its objective analysis

The second experiment, for which a complete checked and verified transcript is included later in the report, took place on Thursday 7 November 1974 at the Baylands Nature Preserve as the target. It fell into three parts. The author first attempted to describe the site before Puthoff had reached it, and reports that Puthoff was at that moment still waiting in an office for a site to be selected, leaning against a counter more than waist high with a light wood top, which matched his impression of a light wood workbench with papers and small items on it. At the agreed time he described shielding, then moving geometrical forms which he compared to looking through a prism or a kaleidoscope, with Puthoff grinning; Puthoff was in fact, the report says, standing inside a tower at the preserve and, remembering that Pat Price had reported a shielded sensation under the same conditions, looked up through the tower and spun around. For the remainder of the session the author described the interior of a building, a museum and art gallery type of tourist place, busy in its architecture, with a main room he estimated at about forty feet square. He reports the details as a little better than fifty per cent correct in later discussion.

The objective analysis scores each aspect of the drawing and each phase of the verbal description as true, not true, or unverified, and then summarises the scores by category. Its stated finding is that only five categories were significant: the body position of the outbound experimenter, the ambience of the location, and relative locations were significantly true, while object names and descriptions relating to the outbound experimenter's environment were significantly untrue. The analysis is candid about its own weaknesses. The author lists six problems, including the role of the interrogator in directing attention, the need to weight recognition of a site's peculiar qualities more heavily than correctness of colour or size, the arbitrariness of his categorisation, the correct statements implied by described details but not counted, the possibility that the channel also involves telepathy, and the fact that a subject who knows the analysis procedure beforehand can improve his score against it. He concludes that the attempt to obtain as much information as possible, without filtering, may have cost the results in validation, and that the current concern should be increasing the signal-to-noise ratio of the subject's output.

### The third remote viewing

For the third experiment the author changed the protocol in line with his own critiques: both Targ and Puthoff went to the site, no one remained with him and there was no interrogator, and the two outbound experimenters recorded all their movements, intentions, and descriptions from the time they left the building, while the author moved about the room and outside it without mentioning his movements on his tape. He described white arches on a circular base, sensed both men, formed a clear impression of Targ sitting very low to the ground with his knees up and his head resting on folded arms, disliked the place, and produced the word "cupola," which he says came to mind although he did not know what it meant. The site was a park playground, and the two men had been sitting on and spinning a merry-go-round formed of a disk with large white loops to hold on to. An adjoining maintenance building had a cupola on its roof, with a black lightning rod and divisions matching his overhead drawing, which neither outbound experimenter had noticed. The author is careful to record his own doubt: his feeling was that he had originally seen only the arches, and that when Targ directed him to go back and draw the cupola during debriefing, he had found one at the site. He also notes that no strong perceptions of the many people, noise, or rapid movement at the playground ever came through.

### The abacus clock and the ESP teaching machine

Two shorter trials complete the report's experiments. In the abacus clock trial, Puthoff attempted to visualise bead positions the author had selected from a random number table and obtained three of five bits, which the author calls essentially chance results. When the author instead formed an impression of the whole apparatus after Puthoff had set it up, he reports that the outline of the bead pattern was the correct shape, that of the digit sequence he generated, 66689, three digits were correct when positioned according to the outline, that the small hand of the clock was correct, and that the position of the date slide was close although the number he assigned to it was wrong. He judges the results not good enough for further consideration at that time.

On the ESP teaching machine, a four-state machine designed by Targ, the author made runs under several conditions, varying hand use and whether his eyes were open or shut, and reports that after two hundred runs of each type he appeared to do best using both hands, with eyes open, proceeding very fast. A run of two thousand such trials produced an average score whose figures are not legible in the recognised text, and a repetition the following day scored precisely at chance, although with possibly a significantly extra-chance variance; he notes that he had not received the calculations. Later that afternoon, alone and, he says, in an angry mood over his earlier results, he cleared the machine and punched out fifteen hits in twenty-five trials, fetched Targ and Puthoff to show them, and then failed to repeat the performance in two further trials. All of these are the author's reports of his own performance, and they are recorded here as such.

## Errors and Anomalies

The Drive filename garbles the subject's surname, which the document itself gives consistently as Hella Hammid, and it takes its description from a single episode. The chapel investigation is one section of a much longer assessment whose own title is Experiments With Hella Hammid, and most of the report concerns laboratory sessions, protocol criticism, and the author's own performance as a subject.

The investigating officer's name is rendered inconsistently in the recognised text, appearing in forms including Konar, Korvar, and Konvar. This assessment therefore does not rest any point on the spelling of his name, and it does not supply a spelling from outside the document.

Several sections of the report appear twice in the scanned text, in substantially the same words, including the account of the author's first remote viewing, the two accounts of his interpretation of Hammid's recordings, and the account of the visit to the chapel. Whether those repetitions are duplicate pages in the scan or repetitions in the report as issued cannot be settled from the recognised text alone. They are recorded here as an anomaly of the item as held, and no point in this assessment counts a repeated passage as two separate pieces of evidence.

Substantial parts of the scan are not readable as text at all. The pages carrying drawings, photographs, and the scoring tables for the second remote viewing produced only character noise under recognition, as did parts of the categorisation tables, so the drawing analysis and the fine detail of the category scores could not be read in full. The numerical result of the author's two-thousand-trial run on the ESP teaching machine is likewise illegible in the recognised text and is left unstated here rather than reconstructed. Names and figures are used in this assessment only where they are legible.

## Limits

This assessment is based on the recognised text of the report, 96,424 characters across 58 scanned pages, read in its entirety, subject to the illegible pages described above. The report is a single observer's account of his own visit, written weeks after the events by his own statement, and it is partly a report of his own performance as a subject. Its transcripts of the author's sessions are included, but the transcripts of his first and third sessions were not completed before he left SRI and are not in the report; his accounts of those sessions rest on his notes and memory, as he says himself. The letter from the investigating officer which the report cites as attesting to Hammid's performance is not included in the item and was not read. Every evaluative claim in the content account above, including the author's conclusion that the phenomena exist, is the author's own claim, attributed to him throughout. Whether remote viewing occurs is not adjudicated here.
