## Premise

The report sets out to survey Soviet and Czechoslovakian research into extrasensory perception, psychokinesis, and related phenomena, as that research stood in April 1975, so that the Defense Intelligence Agency could judge what the work involved, who was conducting it, and what significance it might hold for the United States. Its preface states that its data were drawn from intelligence reports, scientific and technical journals, books, magazines, newspapers, and personal communications.

## Abstract

This September 1975 Defense Intelligence Agency report surveys parapsychology research in the Soviet Union and Czechoslovakia using sources available up to April 1975. The authors, Louis F. Maire III and Major J. D. LaMothe, begin by reconciling vocabulary, explaining that Soviet writers generally preferred the term biocommunications, divided into bioinformation, covering telepathy, precognition, and clairvoyance, and bioenergetics, covering psychokinesis, dowsing, and related effects, while Czechoslovak writers used the term psychotronics. The body of the report is organized into parts on extrasensory perception, psychotronic generator research, psychokinesis, and out-of-the-body phenomena including remote viewing. Within the portion available to this desk, the psychokinesis part gives extended attention to two Soviet subjects, Nina Kulagina and Alla Vinogradova, describing experiments attributed to researchers Sergeyev and Adamenko, including claims that Kulagina influenced the beat of an excised frog heart and imprinted images on shielded photographic film. The same part describes Czechoslovak work centered on Robert Pavlita's psychotronic generators, devices said to collect and concentrate biological energy so that ordinary users could produce psychokinetic effects. The report's own contents list conclusions, trends and forecasts, gaps, and an appendix on personnel and facilities at pages beyond the portion converted for this desk, so those sections are not characterized here. All experimental claims in the report are attributed to the sources the report cites and are not adopted by this desk.

## Content Account

The opening sections establish terminology. The report states that Western parapsychologists use the terms extrasensory perception and psi phenomena for the complex of phenomena under study, while Soviet writers prefer biocommunications and divide it into bioinformation and bioenergetics. A figure compares the American and Soviet vocabularies. The report notes that Czechoslovak researchers had begun using the term psychotronics for the same general field, defined in the report as the study of borderline phenomena with a psychosomatic base that appear to exceed that base, including autosuggestion, hypnosis, telepathy, and psychokinesis.

The first part covers extrasensory perception, with sections on background, telepathy in animals, telepathy in man, classical theories and experiments, current Soviet and Czechoslovak theories and research objectives, and telepathic behavior modification in basic and applied forms. The second part covers psychotronic generator research, centered on the devices of the Czechoslovak researcher Robert Pavlita. The third part covers psychokinesis research.

Within the portion converted for this desk, the psychokinesis part is the most fully represented. It presents a theory attributed to the Soviet researcher Dubrov, who proposed that conformational changes in biomolecules could generate a quasigravitational field, and who suggested that psychic subjects might synchronize such changes to affect target objects. It describes experiments in 1973 and 1974 with Boris Ermolayev, reported to have suspended objects in midair between his hands at Moscow University. It then gives detailed accounts of Nina Kulagina and Alla Vinogradova. According to the report, Kulagina was studied by Sergeyev and Adamenko; she was reported to move objects weighing a pound or more, to control the beat of frog heart preparations, stopping one preparation's beat in five minutes in a 1970 experiment the report describes, and to imprint images on unexposed film sealed in black envelopes. The report also catalogues the physiological stress she was said to experience during testing and the mechanical and electrical characteristics attributed to her effects, including the absence of effect on objects in a vacuum and the absence of influence on an electroscope. Vinogradova is described as producing similar effects with far less physiological stress, with electrostatic energy measured around the objects she influenced. The report closes this part by contrasting the Soviet focus on gifted individuals with the Czechoslovak approach, in which Pavlita's generators were intended to make psychokinetic effects available to nearly anyone, and by describing simple rotation devices used by the Czechoslovak physicist Julius Krmessky as research tools. The fourth part, on out-of-the-body phenomena, covers remote viewing and the apport technique, according to the contents.
