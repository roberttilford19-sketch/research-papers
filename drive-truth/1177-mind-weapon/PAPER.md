## Abstract

This item is an Associated Press article by Barton Reppert, dated 6 November 1983, headlined "Study Cites Soviet Progress on Mind Weapons," preserved in a declassified CIA file. The article reports that a 1981 United States Army study, "Fire Support Mission Area Analysis," quoted at length in Martin Ebon's book "Psychic Warfare: Threat or Illusion?," concluded that the Soviet Union had made significant progress toward developing psychotronic weapons, and recommended that the United States develop defensive tactics and explore mind-control weapons of its own. It sets that study beside a Congressional Research Service report by Christopher H. Dodge, which suggested possible applications of psi phenomena and repeated a speculation that Soviet spending on psi research ran to tens of millions of dollars a year, against an estimated United States total of about five hundred thousand dollars. It records support from Senator Claiborne Pell and describes Robert G. Jahn's Princeton remote-perception and psychokinesis experiments. It also records the contrary position in full: critics charged that much parapsychology research was scientifically unsound or fraudulent, and Paul Kurtz of the Committee for the Scientific Investigation of Claims of the Paranormal attacked the Congressional Research Service report on replication grounds. Every claim in the article is attributed by the article to its sources, and this assessment adopts none of them.

## Content Account

The article opens with its central claim, attributed throughout to its sources. The Soviet Union had achieved "significant progress" toward developing mind-control weapons, according to a United States Army study disclosed in a new book on military uses of psychic phenomena. The book is Martin Ebon's "Psychic Warfare: Threat or Illusion?" Ebon is quoted contending that mind-altering effects, or remote monitoring of brain wave activity, are among the possible reasons behind the Soviet microwave bombardment of the United States Embassy in Moscow. He is quoted further that Soviet scientists view the brain as an apparatus available for probing and manipulation, and that perfected techniques in extrasensory perception and related phenomena would make effective wartime strategies.

The Army study, as the article presents it, was prepared in 1981, was titled "Fire Support Mission Area Analysis," and analyzed the potential impact of psychic warfare tactics and other battlefield factors on the stamina and performance of United States artillery forces. It used the word "psychotronics" for the projection or transmission of mental energy by individual or collective mental discipline and control, or by an energy-emitting device, described as a kind of mind jammer. It cited the significant amount of research completed by Warsaw Pact countries during the previous decade, stated that the Soviet Union in particular appeared to have made significant progress toward developing psychotronic weapons, and recommended that the United States develop special defensive tactics and begin to explore the use of its own mind-control weapons to counter the potential threat.

Alongside the Army study, the article reports a recent report by the Congressional Research Service, prepared by Christopher H. Dodge of its Science Policy Research Division. That report concluded, as quoted, that psi phenomena could be applied in fields such as education, medicine, geological exploration, and business management, and that mind-control techniques might prove useful for military intelligence and police work, along with crime, persuasion, mischief, and disinformation. It said recent experimental results suggested that some mind-control phenomena could be repeated fairly reliably, if less than ideally, under controlled conditions. It also carried the spending comparison the article repeats: Soviet annual spending on psi research had been speculated to amount to tens of millions of dollars, while total funding for parapsychology studies in the United States probably did not greatly exceed five hundred thousand dollars a year, most of it from foundations and other private sources.

The article defines its terms for the reader: psi phenomena include various forms of extrasensory perception, such as telepathy and remote viewing of distant locations, and psychokinesis, the ability to move or bend solid objects with the mind. It records the institutional context as its sources described it: the Central Intelligence Agency had scaled down its involvement with psychic research during the mid-1970s, but an unnamed government official said there seemed to be somewhat renewed interest at the agency in psi phenomena, particularly psychokinetic metal bending, and Pentagon units said to be interested included the Defense Intelligence Agency and the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency.

Congressional support is represented by Senator Claiborne Pell of Rhode Island, who had discussed the field with Soviet researchers during an August visit to the Soviet Union, said he had never personally experienced or seen a psychic event, and said adequate scientific articles indicated that such events do occur. Pell said he was particularly impressed by the work of Robert G. Jahn, dean of Princeton's School of Engineering and Applied Science, whose laboratory had carried out about three hundred remote-perception trials over distances of up to eleven thousand miles and was studying possible psychokinetic effects on batches of nine thousand plastic spheres tumbling through a random mechanical cascade.

The contrary position is given in the article's own text and is recorded here as part of its content. Critics of parapsychology charged that much of the research was either scientifically unsound or fraudulent, and other skeptics argued that even if the phenomena existed, they were too weak and unpredictable to have military value. Paul Kurtz, chairman of the Committee for the Scientific Investigation of Claims of the Paranormal and a philosophy professor at the State University of New York at Buffalo, said the Congressional Research Service report was in no sense an adequate account of the status of research, because the whole parapsychological area was rife with controversy, and that replication was very difficult to find.

The article closes with Ebon's treatment of the Moscow embassy microwaves. It notes that speculation about the beams, believed to have begun as early as 1953, had centered largely on eavesdropping or jamming of United States electronic intelligence equipment. Ebon offered two further hypotheses, stated as his: that the radiation might have been used to effect mind changes in embassy personnel, and that the beams might have been used to read minds by tuning microwaves to the level of brain waves. The article attributes the circumstantial-evidence claim about unorthodox electronic influence on human behavior to Ebon's book.
