## Abstract

This memorandum, dated 14 February 1994 and written by Ms. L. Faith and Dr. E. May of the Cognitive Sciences Laboratory of Science Applications International Corporation, details the operator training technique used by Dr. Dolin in his laboratories for experiments in remote influence. The authors state at the outset that Dolin regarded the training, experience, and techniques of operators as crucial to the success of such experiments, and they set out the procedures in fourteen numbered points. The training requires self-confidence, three-phase breathing, sustained concentration on a visualised image of the receiver, close knowledge of and rapport with the receiver, and the ability to experience emotions on demand, which the memorandum compares to the training of actors. Work proceeds in short impulses of concentration rather than continuously, and a second method, called field influence, is described for use at distances of dozens of metres. The memorandum also states that the effect sought is relatively weak and statistical, that rapport with a new receiver may take two to five days to establish, and that part of the skill can be learned only through one-to-one apprenticeship with experienced operators. The document presents a method as its practitioners described it, and it reports no experimental results.

## Content Account

### The memorandum and its authors

The document is headed as a memorandum of Science Applications International Corporation, Cognitive Sciences Laboratory, dated 14 February 1994, from Ms. L. Faith and Dr. E. May, with the subject "Dolin's Training Technique." A street address in Menlo Park, California, is printed at the foot of each page. The body of the memorandum is a single continuous account, numbered from (1) to (14), of the training procedures "as they are practiced in his laboratories," in the memorandum's own words. No data, trial counts, or results are given anywhere in the two pages.

### Prerequisites and breathing

The first points concern the operator's state and preparation. The main prerequisite, in the memorandum's account, is the aspiring operator's self-confidence, and in particular confidence in his ability to "connect" with the person-receiver or to affect the receiver's mental or emotional state or physiology. Previous experience in yoga, especially yogic breathing, or in martial arts is described as helpful but not mandatory. Correct breathing is treated as important but subordinate to concentration: the recommended three-phase breathing consists of a slow exhalation, a period of breath retention whose duration is found individually within comfortable limits, and an effortless inhalation through the nose.

### Concentration, visualisation, and the receiver

The operator is to learn to concentrate on a particular mental image, visualising clearly the face of the receiver and the receiver's situation, surroundings, and actions. He should know the receiver as well as possible and have a good rapport with the receiver. During a session, the operator attempts mentally to impose on the receiver an image of an event that would be pleasant, in calming trials, or upsetting, in arousing trials. The memorandum stresses a practical qualification: an image that might upset someone in general but is not part of this particular receiver's life or experience usually does not work, in its account, so the operator has to learn what is in fact arousing, upsetting, or pleasant for the individual receiver. The emotion accompanying the image matters as much as the image itself, and the operator is advised to learn to simulate and experience emotions as vividly as possible, a part of the training that the memorandum explicitly compares to actors' training.

### How the work is paced

The operator is not required to maintain intense concentration for the whole trial period of three to five minutes. Instead he works in an "impulse mode," concentrating for approximately two to fifteen seconds, then relaxing, and repeating the process. The best concentration is said to be achieved while exhaling or during the retention phase after exhalation, and the body muscles should be relaxed throughout. The visualised image is not expected to have exceptional, hallucinatory clarity; it may resemble a memory of an event. The memorandum states that what counts more is the operator's clear intent and inner confidence in his ability to connect.

### Imagery of the connection, and feedback

It can be subjectively helpful, the memorandum says, for the operator to visualise the process of remote influence as entering the receiver's brain through the receiver's eyes, and to see the receiver's eyes clearly. Operators knowledgeable in neuroanatomy may find it helpful, though not essential, to attempt to affect particular structures of the receiver's brain; the one example given is the substantia nigra. When the connection is established, an operator often experiences subjective feedback, described as a burning sensation in the middle of the forehead. These are the memorandum's reports of its practitioners' subjective accounts, and they are recorded here as such.

### Trial and error, and the acknowledged weakness of the effect

The training process is described as trial and error. The operator in training repeatedly modifies his approach after learning the outcome of a particular trial, until a certain degree of success is achieved. At the same time, the memorandum states plainly that the operator is aware that the effect is relatively weak and statistical, that changes in the receiver's physiology in the desired direction sometimes do not happen during a given trial, and that he should not be discouraged by that.

### Mental influence and field influence

Dr. Dolin's name for the method described above is "mental influence." In addition, the memorandum says, many of his operators master "field influence" or "field effect," usable at relatively short distances of dozens of metres. In that method the operator visualises himself near the receiver and imagines that his hands emit "energy" directed at the receiver's head or other parts of the body. It is said to require a lesser degree of mental concentration, and the two methods can be combined if the combination does not disrupt concentration. The words "energy" and "connect" are the memorandum's own terms, used in quotation marks in the original at several points; this assessment adopts neither term as an established mechanism.

### Rapport, new systems, and apprenticeship

Even with experienced operators, the memorandum states, establishing rapport with a new receiver takes from two to five days, at times up to seven. The same is often true when an operator begins work with a new biological system, such as plants, where the operator's mental strategy is developed intuitively and then confirmed or modified by trial and error. Finally, the memorandum states that the operator's performance has an important non-verbal component that can be mastered only by observing experienced operators and interacting closely with them, so that this training is a one-to-one apprenticeship requiring patience on the part of the operator in training.
