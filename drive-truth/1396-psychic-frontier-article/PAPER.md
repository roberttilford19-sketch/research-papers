## Premise

The article was produced as a news magazine's survey of the early-1970s surge of public and scientific interest in psychic phenomena — spoon bending, psychic healing, plant perception, dream telepathy, and government-funded laboratory research — at the moment when that interest was attracting money, university courses, and prominent converts. Its purpose is journalistic and evaluative: it assembles the most publicized claims and the people making them, presents the believers' case in their own terms, and then weighs the quality of the evidence, ending in an explicit editorial judgment about what would be required before the claims could be accepted.

## Abstract

TIME's cover story of 4 March 1974 surveys what it calls boom times on the psychic frontier. It opens with a catalogue of the phenomena then drawing attention: Uri Geller apparently bending spoons and keys by the force of his thoughts; a Philippine psychic healer said to have relieved tennis player Tony Roche's elbow by removing blood clots by touch; the bestseller *The Secret Life of Plants* and its thesis that greenery can feel human thoughts; Soviet reports of blindfolded women said to see colors with their hands; dream-telepathy work at Maimonides Medical Center; former astronaut Edgar Mitchell, who conducted telepathy experiments during Apollo 14 and founded the Institute of Noetic Sciences; Arthur Koestler's examination of coincidence; Charles Tart's ESP teaching machine; Kirlian-style photography of a cut leaf; and a Defense Department project investigating seemingly authentic psychic phenomena at the Stanford Research Institute. The article gives the adherents' argument — that a physics which admits black holes, antimatter, and faster-than-light particles should not declare any phenomenon impossible in advance — and then turns critical, noting questionable procedures dressed in laboratory respectability, the connections of many researchers, including Harold Puthoff's promotion of Scientology, and the paucity of objectively verifiable results. It concludes that psychic phenomena cannot be accepted on shoddy or fraudulent evidence, and that only examiners with nothing to lose but their skepticism can settle the question. (227 words)

## Content Account

The article opens under a paired epigraph from Shakespeare's *Henry IV*: Glendower's "I can call spirits from the vasty deep" against Hotspur's reply, "But will they come when you do call for them?" That exchange frames the whole piece. The introduction observes that for all the achievements of science, odd things keep slipping past the sentries — the old friend remembered just as the telephone rings, the vivid dream that becomes the morning's reality, the sense of having done and said exactly this before, the stab of anguish for a distant loved one followed by the telegram — and that hardly a person lives who can deny some such seeming visitation from across the psychic frontier. For most of human history, the article says, those intrusions were mainsprings of action, the life of Greek epic and biblical saga; modern science and psychology have explained much that was once inexplicable, but mysteries remain, and reports of psychic phenomena persist.

The body of the article is a catalogue of the boom. In Israel, Geller bends spoons and keys apparently with the force of his thoughts, and in the Philippines a psychic healer relieves Tony Roche's tennis elbow when an incision is made and three blood clots are apparently removed by touch, by a healer who knows nothing of surgery or modern sanitation. In the United States, *The Secret Life of Plants* becomes a bestseller with its thesis that greenery can feel the thoughts of humans; the number of colleges offering courses in parapsychology rises above one hundred; at Maimonides Medical Center in New York, the image of a painting is transmitted by ESP and seems to enter the dreams of a laboratory subject sleeping in another room. In the Soviet Union, researchers file reports on blindfolded women who can "see" colors with their hands. In England, a *New Scientist* poll finds nearly seventy percent of respondents, mainly scientists and technicians, believing in the possibility of extrasensory perception. In California, Edgar Mitchell — who on the Apollo 14 Moon mission conducted telepathy experiments with friends on Earth — founds the Institute of Noetic Sciences to investigate occurrences that will not yield to rational explanation. In London, Arthur Koestler examines psychic research with the zeal of the believer, spotting in anecdotes of foresight and in the strange behavior of random samplings what he calls the roots of coincidence. At the University of California, psychologist Charles Tart reports that subjects showed a marked increase in ESP scores after working with his new teaching machine. In Los Angeles, a leaf cut in half is photographed by a special process and the picture shows the aura or outline of the whole leaf. In Washington, the Defense Department's Advanced Research Projects Agency assigns a team to investigate seemingly authentic psychic phenomena at the Stanford Research Institute. A caption in the copy, attached to a photograph of a device set up to record an out-of-body trip at the American Society for Psychical Research, reads: "Questionable procedures costumed in the prim gown of laboratory respectability."

The later pages give the adherents their argument before turning against its evidence. The new physics posits black holes and particles that travel faster than light, and has confirmed neutrinos, antimatter, and quasars; why, the article asks in the believers' voice, should any phenomenon be assumed impossible? It quotes Sir James Jeans — "The universe begins to look more and more like a great thought than a great machine" — and answers the psychic adherent's simple reply, that anything is possible, with the article's central criticism: simply saying so, and then supporting the contention with shoddy or downright fraudulent evidence, is not enough. Psychic phenomena cannot be accepted on a priori belief. The article notes that the paranormal establishment has questioned the dogma of scientific method, and reports that Harold Puthoff, one of the two SRI investigators of Geller, is singled out in *The Secret Life of Plants* as a reputable scientist who has experimented with the response of one chicken egg to the breaking of another, and is also a promoter of the controversial movement Scientology, which Ingo Swann, another psychic tested by SRI, also practices. It reports that Mitchell's Institute of Noetic Sciences helped fund SRI's Geller research, conducted largely by Puthoff and Russell Targ — Targ being the son of the Putnam executive who had contracted to publish Mitchell's book *Psychic Exploration* — and that the elder Targ stated at the signing that "the real race now between the Russians and us is in the area of sciences like ESP."

The closing argument widens to medicine and method. James Reston's acupuncture experience after surgery in China in 1974 — reported in the article as 1974, in text that OCR renders imperfectly — is used to show how an unorthodox method can win examination and partial acknowledgment while remaining unexplained. Biofeedback research at the Menninger Foundation is cited as showing that subjects can control normally involuntary bodily functions, making the ancient yogic skills suddenly seem within everyone's grasp. Against this the article sets C. P. Snow's observation that scientists regard it as a virtue to know what not to think about, and a complaint attributed to an SRI spokesman that society does not give permission to have psychic abilities. Its verdict is that parapsychologists could learn the truth seeker's most valuable weapon, doubt; that credulity, in Charles Lamb's phrase, is the child's strength but the adult's weakness; that shoddy or ignorant research is used to lend legitimacy to extravagant tenets; and that the field should not be excluded from serious scrutiny, since first-rate minds — Freud, Einstein, Jung, Edison — have been attracted to it. The paranormal may exist, the article concedes, against logic and beyond present evidence; but there is only one way to tell: thorough examination by those who do not express an a priori belief, for whom probability is a comprehensible code, and who have nothing to lose but their skepticism. Until such examiners are allowed to play the psychic game, the article concludes, the human mind will remain an underdeveloped country. It also notes the practical consequences of weak results: funding is hard to raise, parapsychologists barely survive on a few foundations and gifts, and there is only one academic chair in parapsychology.

## Errors and Anomalies

The OCR of this item is severely degraded across its middle pages. Much of the article's central spread — photograph captions, sidebars, and portions of the main text — reduces to dense character noise from which no continuous reading can be recovered, and figures quoted from those stretches have been avoided in this assessment. In the legible pages, OCR errors affect names and dates (SRI is several times rendered "S.R.L."; one internal date in the acupuncture passage renders as 1974 where the sense of the passage suggests the article's own timeline; the spelling "Puthoff" fluctuates). Quoted phrases in this assessment are taken only from stretches where the OCR is continuous and internally consistent. The ten pages of the copy do not preserve the magazine's full layout, and it is not possible to confirm from this copy that every page of the original article is present.
