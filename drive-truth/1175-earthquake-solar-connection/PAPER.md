## Abstract

This magazine article by Patrick Huyghe profiles Marsha Adams, a biologist at SRI International who proposed that increases in solar activity trigger earthquakes and a range of biological and social effects. Adams's route to the idea began, the article says, in a Stanford Medical School laboratory, where her results with embryo chick hearts reversed without explanation; she later screened eleven thousand measured surgical bleeding cases against geophysical variables and reports that bleeding anomalies followed increased solar activity and preceded large earthquakes. The article describes her forecasting study: twenty-five people who reported flu-like symptoms before earthquakes charted their symptoms twice daily and telephoned a hot line when symptoms peaked, and Adams is reported as saying the group was accurate between seventy and eighty percent of the time, although she did not attempt to forecast all large earthquakes. It reports fire forecasts supplied to a San Francisco fire chief tracking an arsonist, sets out her candidate mechanism, extremely low frequency electromagnetic radiation generated when solar particles disturb the Earth's magnetosphere, acting through piezoelectricity or magnetostriction, and records both official encouragement and sharp criticism from United States Geological Survey scientists, who found her evidence and her physical model unpersuasive. Adams concedes that her work had been publicized before her data were rigorously analyzed.

## Content Account

The article's subject is Marsha Adams, identified as a biologist, thirty-eight years old at the time of writing, at SRI International in California. Her own summary of her position is quoted at the outset: she did not start out, she says, as a sun enthusiast, but came to believe that almost all variations in biological and physical processes may result from fluctuations in solar activity.

The narrative begins about a decade before publication, in a basement laboratory at Stanford Medical School, where Adams was studying cardiac stimulants on embryo chick hearts. Shortly before she was to present her results, the results reversed: instead of stimulation, the hearts showed depression. She dismantled the apparatus, changed the electrodes, solutions, and air supply, and could not restore the original results; later, without apparent reason, the experiment worked again. Having controlled for the variables biologists ordinarily control, she concluded that something in the wider environment was influencing the process. She then accumulated other instances, the largest being eleven thousand cases of measured surgical bleeding, which she screened against cosmic radiation, geomagnetic and solar measures, and standard weather variables. She reports that the bleeding anomalies occurred following periods of increased solar activity and preceding large-magnitude earthquakes, and the article adds testimony from clinical staff who said they could tell when an earthquake was coming from disturbances among their patients, and who, challenged to predict one, did so, with the bleeding data showing the expected anomalies.

The forecasting study built on that claim about human sensitivity. Twenty-five participants, called responsives, most of whom contacted Adams because they had noticed their own symptoms preceding earthquakes, kept twice-daily charts of symptoms including fatigue, vertigo, chills, headaches, nausea, ringing in the ears, and a flushed sensation, together with depression and arguments. When symptoms reached peak intensity they telephoned a hot line with the time, symptom, and strength, and their forecasts were recorded on a computer that time-stamped each entry. Adams used each person's track record to judge their reliability for large or small, local or distant earthquakes and for timing. The article reports her figures: one participant had called only four times and had been, she says, completely accurate for earthquakes over magnitude seven, and the group as a whole was accurate between seventy and eighty percent of the time, with the qualification the article itself adds, that she had made no attempt to forecast all of the more than thirty-six earthquakes of magnitude six and a half or greater in the period. She also supplied fire forecasts to Andrew Casper, chief of the San Francisco fire department, who was tracking an arsonist responsible for more than thirty fires and is quoted endorsing her accuracy.

The proposed mechanism occupies the article's center. Solar flares, described in some detail, disturb the Earth's magnetosphere and, Adams proposes, generate extremely low frequency electromagnetic radiation, between three and thirty hertz, both directly and through weather fronts. The article notes supporting fragments she assembled: a German study of fifty-three thousand subjects suggesting slower responses near such waves, symptom lists from other researchers resembling her responsives' symptoms, and the coincidence between the frequency of alpha brain waves and the Schumann resonance. For the earthquake trigger itself Adams speculates, and the article presents it as speculation, that such radiation could convert to mechanical vibration in the crust through piezoelectricity, as in a quartz watch, or through magnetostriction, and that a vibration resonating with a fault that had accumulated the most strain could provide the final nudge. Because low-frequency waves travel around the world in the cavity between the Earth and the ionosphere, she argues, responsives need not be near an earthquake to forecast it, and she suggests that differing symptoms may carry location information. She expected, funding permitting, to replace the human variable with direct measurements and to issue daily earthquake probabilities within a year or two.

The article closes with the reception. California's governor called her, she testified before a state assembly committee, and the United States Geological Survey's Office of Earthquake Studies gave her an opportunity to present her work, but most of its scientists found the evidence not persuasive enough to fund, and C. Barry Raleigh, then with the Survey, is quoted at length: he never saw the kind of critical evidence required to make such a hypothesis even marginally acceptable, and the weakest link was the absence of a reasonable physical model, although something in the idea had plainly convinced her. Adams regrets the premature publicity, which the article says forced her to present through the media before completing a rigorous analysis of her data. A separate sidebar on sunlight, melatonin, mood, fertility, and the immune system shares the article's pages and is not part of Huyghe's argument.
