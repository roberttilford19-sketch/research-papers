## Premise

The document is an SRI International final report on the “Targeting Requirements Task,” an October 1980 remote viewing study. Its question is practical: remote viewing sessions must somehow be aimed at a target site, and three aiming methods were in use, a person at the site acting as a beacon, the site's geographical coordinates, and simply telling the viewer that a site exists to be described. The study was designed to determine the relative effectiveness of the three procedures, whether any differences depended on the individual viewer, and whether giving the viewer limited feedback in the middle of a session improved accuracy.

## Abstract

SRI International reports a four-month study comparing three ways of targeting remote viewing sessions. In beacon targeting the viewer had personal contact with, or a photograph of, a person present at the target site. In coordinate targeting the viewer was given the site's geographical coordinates. In abstract targeting the viewer was told only that there was a site to describe. Four remote viewers each completed twelve trials on San Francisco Bay Area sites, thirty-six trials divided evenly among the three techniques and twelve further coordinate trials testing the effect of limited mid-session feedback about the general nature of the site. The report states that three of the four viewers performed reliably, that for the viewers as a group no significant differences in efficacy among the three targeting modes emerged, and that all three techniques produced data of comparable accuracy, subject to individual preference: one successful viewer developed an order of preference, and that viewer's results differed significantly in line with it. It also reports no significant difference between personal contact and a photograph in beacon targeting, and that mid-session feedback produced no significant improvement, with some nonsignificant indication of degraded accuracy in the better transcripts. The report adds a caveat that a global precognitive channel could not be excluded under its design, and recommends a separate study with feedback withheld on a random basis.

## Content account

The report's table of contents and executive summary frame the study cleanly. The objective section states the task: to determine the relative effectiveness of various targeting procedures for use in remote viewing, and, if differences were found, whether they depended on the characteristics of individual viewers. The protocols section, listed in the contents, covers viewer selection, the distribution of trials across session conditions, and transcript evaluation on a nought-to-seven point scale of target and transcript correspondence.

The sampled middle of the report consists largely of its data tables: summaries of the remote viewing data for each viewer, identified by code numbers, and a combined table of all data from the forty-eight trials with mean values for each viewer and each session category, together with a table of the coordinate trials with mid-session feedback. In the OCR copy these tables are severely degraded, their entries rendered as scattered digits and symbols from which individual values cannot be recovered reliably; this assessment therefore takes the study's findings from the report's own executive summary, its overall findings section as listed in the contents, and its summary and conclusions, which state the results in prose.

Those conclusions are as given in the abstract. The report states that three of the four viewers exhibited reliable remote viewing functioning; that no significant differences in the efficacy of one targeting technique over another emerged for the group or for the successful subgroup; that one viewer's significant differences aligned with an expressed order of preference, which the report reads as showing that the intrinsic equality of the techniques can be modulated by personal preference or bias; that beacon targeting showed no significant difference between personal contact and the use of a photograph; and that mid-session feedback, given as limited information about the general nature of the site after the viewer had developed a coherent fifteen- or twenty-minute narration, yielded no significant improvement and some statistically nonsignificant evidence of degradation, at least in the better transcripts.

The report also examines a possible artifact. Because viewers eventually receive feedback about the true target, statements in a transcript that anticipate that feedback might reflect precognition rather than the targeting method. The report states that it counted future feedback references in the transcripts, sixteen, eleven, and fourteen for the three targeting techniques, found no evidence of an uneven distribution across session conditions, and found the correlation between the number of such references per transcript and transcript ratings nonsignificant, at r equals 0.08 with p equals 0.70 as printed. It concludes that there was no evidence that such references affected individual ratings or the comparisons between targeting conditions, while noting that the global possibility of a precognitive channel remained open under the design and required a separate study with randomly withheld feedback. The report's reference list cites earlier SRI remote viewing work by Puthoff, Targ, May, and others, and Charles Tart's book on learning to use extrasensory perception.
