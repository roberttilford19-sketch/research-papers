## Premise

The document is a journal article by William G. Braud of the Mind Science Foundation, published in the Journal of the American Society for Psychical Research, volume 84, January 1990. It reports a formal experiment testing whether unselected subjects could exert a distant mental influence on the rate at which human red blood cells broke down under osmotic stress. The study sits inside a wider argument the article makes explicitly: if people can influence their own separated biological material, then a psychic component in self-healing becomes a testable possibility rather than a speculation.

## Abstract

William Braud reports an experiment in which thirty-two unselected subjects each attempted, from a separate room, to protect red blood cells in ten tubes from hemolysis by visualization and intention, while ten further tubes served as noninfluence controls. The cells were placed under osmotic stress in hypotonic saline, hemolysis was measured photometrically over short trial periods, and subjects and experimenter were blind to critical features of the procedure, including whether the cells a subject tried to influence were the subject's own blood or another person's. The article reports that significantly more subjects than chance would predict showed individually significant differences between their protect and control tubes, with a reported probability value of 1.91 times ten to the minus five. Blood source made no significant overall difference, although a nonsignificant trend favored the subjects working with their own blood, both in the full sample and among the nine individually significant subjects. Analyses performed at SRI International to decide whether the data fit a remote action, causal model or an intuitive data sorting, informational model were inconclusive, the article says, because of the heterogeneity of the data, while the authors' own comparison of the two-tube and eight-tube conditions found no difference, which they read as more consistent with remote action. The article closes with cautions about generalizing from in vitro hemolysis to the living body.

## Content account

The article's introduction places the experiment in the Mind Science Foundation's program of biological psychokinesis research, citing earlier reported influences on the spatial orientation of fish, the locomotor activity of rodents, and the physiological activity of another person. Braud argues that a psychic contribution to healing another person can be isolated experimentally by blinding and separation, but that self-healing ordinarily cannot, because the healer knows the timing and nature of the attempt and expectation and self-regulation cannot be excluded. The hemolysis design is offered as a way around that problem: the biological activity is removed from the body, so ordinary self-influence cannot reach it, and any effect must be either psychic or an artifact of the procedure.

The method, as sampled, used thirty-two healthy subjects, seventeen female and fifteen male, aged twenty-three to fifty-three, twenty-one of whom had taken part in a pilot study. Each donated a ten millilitre blood sample. Labels on two of each group of four tubes were switched by a nurse under a randomized schedule kept from the experimenter, so that some subjects unknowingly attempted to influence their own blood and others another person's. In the experimental session the subject was shown the equipment, then seated in a distant room; target tubes were measured in a spectrophotometer while the subject attempted to protect the designated cells during effort periods, with relaxation and guided imagery instructions recorded beforehand. The number of tubes measured in each fifteen-minute period, two or eight, was set by a sealed envelope, a feature designed to test competing models of how psi might operate.

The results section reports the headline outcome given in the abstract. It also reports the blood source analyses: no significant overall effect of whether the cells were the subject's own, a trend toward stronger positive scoring in the own-blood condition among the nine individually significant subjects that was not significant at the reported values, and a post hoc analysis by an anonymous referee suggesting a scoring pattern in the own-blood subgroup, which the article reports as the referee's analysis rather than its own primary result. The SRI International analyses of the remote action versus intuitive data sorting models are reported as inconclusive, with heterogeneity across subjects given as the reason. The authors' own two-tube versus eight-tube comparison found no significant difference in either the full sample or the significant subgroup.

The discussion, as sampled, takes up a statistical objection raised by George Hansen concerning the independence of measurements within a fifteen-minute period, and answers it by pointing to the use of a different tube for each trial, the blocked and randomized order of periods, and the controls identified during pilot work. The closing pages caution that the hemolysis studied occurred in vitro under osmotic stress, whereas breakdown of red cells in the body usually has other causes, and note the peculiar biological status of mature red blood cells, which lack nuclei and cannot reproduce, while suggesting that white blood cells or cultured neural cells might be used in future work. The article ends with its references and the author's Mind Science Foundation address in San Antonio, Texas.
