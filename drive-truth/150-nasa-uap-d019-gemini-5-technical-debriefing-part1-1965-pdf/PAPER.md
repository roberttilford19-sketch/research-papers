## Premise

The document is the preliminary transcript of the Gemini V flight crew's technical debriefing, conducted from August 30 through September 1, 1965, at Crew Quarters, Cape Kennedy, Florida. Its purpose, as its preface states, is to get the crew's account of the flight — countdown, powered flight, orbital operations, retrofire, reentry, and landing — onto the record from the voice tapes while the flight was fresh, ahead of a more fully edited record. Part 1 covers the operational narrative of the flight; the systems, experiments, and training sections were assigned to Part 2, which is a separate document and is not in this file.

## Abstract

The transcript records command pilot Gordon Cooper and pilot Charles "Pete" Conrad Jr. describing the eight-day Gemini V flight to a representative of the Flight Crew Support Division, which prepared the transcript on September 1, 1965 and states plainly that it had not been thoroughly editorially reviewed. The crew describes a launch marred by pogo oscillation, dirty and fogged windows, and a primary horizon scanner that repeatedly drove the spacecraft toward nose-down attitudes. The central systems story of the flight is electrical power: a fuel-cell oxygen heater circuit breaker found off and a burned-out heater forced the crew to power the spacecraft down and abandon the rendezvous evaluation pod exercise, even though the pod was ejected, tracked by radar, and seen nearby on later orbits. The crew also recounts degrading orbit attitude and maneuvering thrusters, two unexplained loud sounds against the hatch, tracking of Minuteman missile launches from Vandenberg, photography of a White Sands rocket-sled run and of tropical storm Doreen, and a reentry in which a late, unscheduled computer load from the ground left the spacecraft landing roughly 83 miles short of the target. The account is detailed, candid, and frequently critical of ground procedures.

## Content

### The document itself

The cover identifies the document as "GEMINI V TECHNICAL DEBRIEFING (U), Part 1," marked CONFIDENTIAL, Group 4, with downgrading at three-year intervals and declassification after twelve years, and carries a notice under the Freedom of Information Act and NASA Policy Directive 1382.2. The preface states that the transcript is preliminary, taken from voice tapes of the debriefing, and not thoroughly editorially reviewed, and that Part 2 — covering systems operations, operational checks, visual sightings, experiments, pre-mission planning, mission control, and training — would follow by September 3, 1965. The printed document runs to 216 pages, with sections 1.0 Countdown through 7.0 Landing and Recovery. Everything in the Content account below is the crew's account as recorded in this transcript; it is reported as their testimony, not as independently established fact.

### Countdown and powered flight

The crew reports pogo oscillation at about 11 cycles per second beginning at 2 minutes 5 seconds into powered flight, estimated at plus or minus one-half to three-quarters of a g, severe enough to hinder reading the displays. At fairing jettison, heavy debris came past the spacecraft and gray flecks settled on the windows. The crew also reports that the windows were fogged and dirty between the panes before launch, and that a small bee and a fly were sealed inside Cooper's window unit.

### Orbital flight: the horizon scanner and the power problem

The crew reports that the primary horizon scanner malfunctioned repeatedly, driving the platform toward nose-down attitudes that reached about 40 degrees in one instance, and that the problem worsened through the flight. The decisive systems failure was in the fuel cells: a circuit breaker for the oxygen and hydrogen heaters was found off, and an oxygen heater had burned out. In the crew's account this was a single-point failure whose consequence was a spacecraft power-down and the abandonment of the planned rendezvous exercise with the rendezvous evaluation pod. The pod had been ejected, 15 seconds late, at 2 hours 7 minutes 15 seconds into the flight, and the spacecraft radar locked onto it. The crew reports that the pod's range rate never decreased as expected and that radar range reached about 0.89 mile, yet the crew continued to see the pod nearby on many subsequent orbits and rejected as impossible a ground-reported separation figure of 375 miles. The cold infrared sensor doors were opened late, at 2 hours 16 minutes 15 seconds with the pod about 2,500 feet away; Conrad accepted the blame for the delay in the transcript.

### Orbital flight: thrusters, sounds, and observed events

The crew reports that the orbit attitude and maneuvering system thrusters failed progressively: thruster No. 7 was lost, No. 8 and others degraded, and cross-coupling appeared by the fifth to seventh days. Conrad reports two loud "dings" against his hatch, which he speculated might have been micrometeorite strikes; the crew left the cause unresolved. The crew tracked Minuteman missiles launched from Vandenberg on the third day (experiment D-4 event 423a) and saw a second missile on the fourth day without tracking it. They photographed a rocket-sled run at White Sands and located tropical storm Doreen by S-7 photography. During high-frequency radio tests they received broadcast music, identified in the transcript as "Never on Sunday," relayed through the Ascension station. The crew also formed the impression of UHF jamming or radar interference over the China and Tibet region; the transcript presents this as the crew's impression, and it is reported here the same way.

### Retrofire, reentry, and landing

The crew's strongest criticism concerns reentry. In their account, about 27 minutes before retrofire, while the spacecraft computer was in reentry mode, the Carnarvon station sent an unscheduled digital command system load with a new retrofire time, and the crew received no confirming DCS light. The load targeted a point about 240 miles short, the down-range guidance needle never moved off its peg, and the crew reverted to the nominal 53-degree bank angle, landing about 83 miles short of the intended point; Cooper also gives a figure of 86 miles in the section 6.1 account. The crew reports noticeable gaps between the retro rockets: No. 3 stopped well before No. 4 started. They state their view that a night retrofire is purely an instrument maneuver. The drogue parachute was deployed at about 70,000 feet instead of adding the second reaction control system ring. The water landing was soft. The crew remained in the spacecraft about 35 to 40 minutes; the high-frequency whip antenna did not deploy, and the first clear two-way communication was through the swimmers' external telephone jack. The carrier was about 75 miles away, and the crew chose helicopter pickup.

## Errors and anomalies

The transcript is internally inconsistent on the landing shortfall figure, giving about 83 miles in one passage and 86 miles in another; both figures are the document's, and the discrepancy is noted rather than resolved. The document's own preface discloses its main limitation: it is a preliminary transcript that had not been thoroughly editorially reviewed, so transcription errors in names, numbers, and technical terms are to be expected. The optical character recognition of the downloaded text adds a further layer of garbling, particularly in tables and in speaker labels. The cover's classification markings are historical: the document states its own declassification schedule, and the file circulates as a declassified record under the Freedom of Information Act notice printed on it.
