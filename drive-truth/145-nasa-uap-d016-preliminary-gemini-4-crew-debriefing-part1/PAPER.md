## Premise

The document was produced to capture, while memory was fresh, the Gemini 4 crew's own account of their mission from an operational viewpoint, for use by mission analysis personnel. According to its preface, the transcript was made from voice tape recordings of the debriefing conducted aboard the recovery ship USS Wasp on June 9, 1965, and was prepared, in preliminary form and before thorough editorial review, by the Spacecraft Operations Branch of the Flight Crew Support Division on June 16, 1965. The speakers throughout are the two crew members, James A. McDivitt and Edward H. White II. The preface states that a second part, covering systems operations, operational checks, visual sightings, experiments, pre-mission planning, mission control, and training, was to follow; that second part is a separate item in the same Drive collection (census number 146).

## Abstract

This preliminary transcript records astronauts James McDivitt and Edward White describing the Gemini 4 mission in their own words, phase by phase, shortly after their recovery at sea. The questioning follows the mission chronology: countdown and crew insertion, powered flight, insertion into orbit, orbital flight, retrofire, reentry, and landing and recovery. The longest and most detailed passages concern the orbital phase, above all the attempted station-keeping with the spent booster stage and White's extravehicular activity, in which he left the spacecraft, maneuvered with a hand-held gas gun, and returned. The crew speak candidly about difficulties as well as successes: confused communications in the final minutes of countdown, a booster that separated with an unexpected relative velocity and tumbled at an estimated forty to fifty degrees per second, windows filmed with a white deposit that badly hampered outward vision, and a maneuvering gun whose six feet per second of velocity change they judged far too little for the work expected of it. One passage describes an unidentified satellite-like object seen near Hawaii. The document's value is as a primary operational record: it preserves what the crew reported, in the order they reported it, before any official edited transcript replaced it.

## Content Account

The countdown section (Part 1 of the transcript's own numbering) covers crew insertion, communications, crew participation, comfort, the environmental control system, sounds, vibrations, visual observations, and crew station controls and displays. McDivitt describes his suit fogging during insertion before the fans were turned on, a problem they had already rehearsed in the wet mock-up. Both men criticize the communications arrangement in the last three or four minutes before launch, when the spacecraft test conductor, the booster test conductor, and the capsule communicator, Al Shepard (spelled "Shephard" in the transcript), were at times audible simultaneously, and callouts meaningful to the test conductors meant nothing to the crew.

The powered flight section follows the ascent in detail: liftoff cues, the roll and pitch programs, aerodynamics, maximum acceleration, windshear, engine operation for both stages, booster engine cutoff, staging, second-stage ignition, guidance initiation, go and no-go calls, and steering through second-stage engine cutoff. The insertion section then covers the crew's activities in the first minutes of orbit.

The orbital flight section is the bulk of the volume. Its first topic, station-keeping, is an extended account of the attempt to remain near the spent second stage. The crew report that the booster separated with a relative velocity much greater than anticipated and not in the expected plane, that it began tumbling and built up rates they estimated at forty to fifty degrees per second, and that repeated attempts to close with it consumed propellant without achieving a rendezvous before the ground instructed them to abandon the attempt. Judging distance to the booster proved extremely difficult; McDivitt says that at their closest he could only guess they might have come within about two hundred feet. It is within this discussion that McDivitt describes the sighting for which the document is most often consulted. Over the Pacific, near Hawaii, he saw an object they were closing on, distinct enough in the thirty to forty seconds it was visible that he believed he could make out its shape. Concerned, he checked the position of the power switch to confirm he had maneuvering capability. The sun then crossed the window and he lost sight of it entirely. He states plainly that he did not know its distance: it might have been five miles away, or fifty. The transcript offers no identification of the object, and the lesson the crew draw from the episode is a practical one about optical rendezvous: when the sun is on a filmed window, nothing beyond the nose of the spacecraft can be seen. White adds that their windows carried a white film of material on the outside that scattered sunlight and made outward vision difficult whenever the sun's rays struck it.

The extravehicular activity section, which runs from about page 87 to about page 250 of the printed transcript, is the longest single topic. It covers the assembly of equipment, the force needed to open the hatch and White's ability to restrain it as it swung, his exit, his translation along the spacecraft, and his evaluation of the hand-held maneuvering unit. The crew's central engineering judgment is quantitative: the gun provided about six feet per second of velocity change, and White states that forty to fifty feet per second would be a minimum for serious work, roughly ten times what he had. Asked directly whether he would detach his tether and maneuver untethered, White answers carefully: not with the unit as flown, but with more capability and more practice he would be willing, at that time, to make short untethered transfers of fifteen or twenty feet between nearby spacecraft, of the kind envisaged as a backup mode for Apollo, because the spacecraft itself could come and recover him. He recommends further practice with the gun in the zero-gravity aircraft, much of it in plain flying suits rather than pressurized suits. The section also covers his return into the spacecraft, during which McDivitt steered his legs down and settled him into his seat, and the crew's reflections on the experience; White calls it the most interesting and dramatic moment of the flight. The remaining orbital discussion works through the long middle of the mission revolution by revolution: experiment and photography tasks, ground passes, a flight computer that stuck in the on condition at about seventy-five and a half hours, stowage and trash management, and preparations for retrofire.

The retrofire section walks through the timed sequence of events from thirty-six minutes before retrofire to the firing itself and the jettison of the retropack, including the crew's discussion of their qualified trust in automatic retrofire. The reentry section covers the parameter updates, the acceleration profile, spacecraft control, descent through one hundred thousand and fifty thousand feet, main parachute deployment, and the post-deployment checks. The final section covers impact, the crew's decision to remain in the spacecraft with hatches closed in moderate seas, their exit into the life raft, and their pickup by helicopter. Neither man used the survival gear. White, who leapt into the raft before inflating his flotation device, insisted on being the last man hoisted; McDivitt records being dragged against the heat shield during his own hoist and being, nonetheless, thoroughly happy to be in the water.

## Errors and Anomalies

The extracted text carries heavy optical character recognition noise throughout, including garbled renderings of the classification banner on nearly every page and frequent misspellings of the speakers' names; quotations in this assessment have been confined to passages whose wording is legible beyond reasonable doubt. The cover carries declassification markings, including an authority line and a classification change dated 1973, and the body is stamped as originally confidential; the document was therefore once restricted and is present in the collection in declassified form. The table of contents as extracted contains numbering irregularities, including the landing and recovery part rendered with the wrong part number, and the headings for two orbital subsections are partially lost in extraction. The transcript is explicitly preliminary: its preface warns that the urgent need of mission analysis personnel precluded thorough editorial review and that errors would be corrected in a later official transcript, so wording in this version should not be treated as the final official record. Finally, the volume is Part I only; readers looking for the crew's formal treatment of visual sightings and experiments should note that the preface assigns those topics to Part II.

## Limits

This assessment rests on a sampled read, executed as follows. The front matter, preface, and the complete table of contents were read in full, as were the opening sections on crew insertion and communications, the station-keeping passage containing the Hawaii sighting (printed pages 74 to 77), the maneuvering-unit discussion (printed pages 179 to 185), and the closing pages on crew pickup (printed pages 328 to 329). In addition, the opening lines of every tenth page segment were read across the whole volume, thirty-five sampling points spanning printed pages 3 to 323, and the topics found at those points agree with the table of contents at every point. In total roughly 43,000 of the text's 503,806 characters were read closely. The remaining passages are repetitive two-voice operational exchanges whose content is indexed by the table of contents; they were sampled rather than read line by line, and fine detail within them, including exact figures quoted in exchanges not sampled, has not been verified. All factual statements in this assessment are drawn from the document itself or from its ledger card; the document's own figures, such as tumble rates and distances, are reported as the crew's estimates, not endorsed as measurements. No external source was consulted, and no claim is made about whether this preliminary transcript is the only or the earliest version of the debriefing record.
