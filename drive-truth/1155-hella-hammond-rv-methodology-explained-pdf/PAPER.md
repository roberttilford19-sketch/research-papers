## Premise

The memorandum records what the four members of the AMSAA GRILL FLAME Core Group saw, heard, and discussed during a two-day visit to SRI on 21 and 22 August 1979. It was produced so that the group's home organisation would have a written record of the visit, including the visitors' own observations and reservations. Much of it is given to the group's discussions with Hella Hammid, a participant in the SRI remote viewing programme, and to her account of how she worked.

## Abstract

The document is a memorandum for record, dated 19 September 1979, reporting a two-day visit by the four members of the AMSAA GRILL FLAME Core Group to SRI on 21 and 22 August 1979. Most of the memorandum records the group's discussions with Hella Hammid, who had taken part in the SRI remote viewing programme for five to six years. It describes her working method in detail, including her short period of quiet preparation, her practice of drawing each visual impression as it occurs before turning to her other senses, her near-exclusive work with Russell Targ as interviewer, and her reliance on his guidance when assessing her own performance. It also records discussions with Targ and Hal Puthoff about the Army Science Board's criticism of SRI's evaluation procedures, about object selection for the box technique, about a proposed twenty-site target pool, about earlier tunnel-location work, and about a disputed evaluation in which four of six beacon sessions were matched correctly. The visitors report that SRI personnel rejected null target sessions as a form of trickery that would destroy trust between viewer and experimenters. The memorandum is a contemporary visit report, and its accounts are the participants' own statements as recorded by the visiting group.

## Content Account

### The visit and its subject

The memorandum states that the four members of the AMSAA GRILL FLAME Core Group visited SRI on 21 and 22 August 1979 for discussions with the SRI staff and with Hella Hammid. Its paragraphs are marked with the classification abbreviation used throughout the original, and a classification line at the foot of the first page records that the document was classified under a Headquarters, Department of the Army authority dated 7 July 1978, with review set for 7 July 1998. The visitors explain their particular interest at the outset: they had heard a great deal about Hammid and the remote viewing work she had done, and, because the nature of their own participation had given them very little contact with other remote viewers, they were looking forward to discussing their ideas and feelings about remote viewing with another viewer.

### Hammid's background, as she described it

Hammid had participated in the SRI remote viewing programme for the past five to six years, according to the memorandum. She described three weeks spent in Egypt with Steve Schwartz, during which the procedure she used was basically coordinate remote viewing carried out in time rather than space: given that she was at a site, she was provided with a date to view, such as 200 BC. She told the visitors she knew little more about the results of that work than they did, because she had not yet received any after-the-fact feedback from Schwartz. She had originally joined the SRI programme as a control subject, as a personal favour to Russell Targ, whom she had known for several years. The memorandum records that she approached psychic phenomena with a positive and willing attitude, but was in no way an absolute believer, and that out-of-body experience frightened her.

### Her working method

The longest passages of the memorandum describe how Hammid worked. She tried to avoid focusing on the fact that a session was coming, and then used only about five minutes of quiet time to relax while staying alert. She viewed lying down with her eyes closed. As each visual impression came to her, she opened her eyes and drew that image; only after the visual and drawing portion of the session did she let her other senses come into play, often perceiving, in the memorandum's account, the sounds, smells, and temperature conditions at the target site. She always assumed a very comfortable body position so that sensations from the room would not intrude, and she was, the visitors note, always very aware of her bodily state. She explained that drawing each impression immediately after it occurred let her let it go and move forward with a clear screen, and avoided embroidering the basic impression later through analytic or memory overlay. She also told the group that she frequently drew things ninety degrees off in the horizontal or vertical plane.

### Her reliance on Targ

Hammid worked practically exclusively with Russell Targ as her interviewer. The memorandum records that they had been good friends for a long time, that she relied heavily on him to suggest approaches, perspectives, and directions, particularly during sessions, and that she also derived help and confidence from talking with Ingo, whose surname is not given in that passage. The visitors observed that when Targ was out of the room she was hesitant about making her own assessment of how well she had done in one sensory mode or another, and they remark, in their own words, that it was as if Targ constituted her self-assurance. They also note that she often did sessions alone at home when called to do so. This dependence is the visitors' observation, and it is reported here as their observation rather than as a settled finding about Hammid.

### The visitors' own sessions, and Hammid's daily use of her impressions

When the visitors described their triple sessions, in which three viewers worked sequentially on the same target with feedback and group discussion delayed until after the third session, Hammid became enthusiastic, called the arrangement a marvellous way to handle limited feedback, and said she would like to do that type of session herself. Asked whether she used psi in her daily life, she said she had always had feelings about people before she began remote viewing, but that she now trusted those feelings to be fairly accurate rather than coincidental; she described setting up situations with friends and family to try them out, using the same instincts in her photography work, and receiving unwanted impressions about fellow passengers in a subway. The visitors close this part of the record by describing her as warm, engaging, and alive, and their discussions with her as both pleasant and profitable.

### Methodology and evaluation disputes

The memorandum records several professional disagreements. On the morning of 21 August, Manny Gale met Russell Targ and Hal Puthoff, whose discussions centred on an earlier Army Science Board visit to SRI and on Targ and Puthoff's frustration with the Board's criticism of their evaluation procedure and statistical analyses, which they said they had not presented to the Board. They asked Gale to set up a meeting with Drs. Tang and Snyder, the Board's experts in that area. Targ was also, the visitors report, still very upset about the group's Phase I Interim Note, fearing that readers would become unsupportive of the SRI effort; after two days of argument he accepted that the note was written as it should be. The group discussed Wayne's methodology for the LaBerge task, about which Targ was at best unenthusiastic, and a new evaluation procedure prepared by Wayne's group, which Ed May and Beverly Humphrey received with interest.

In a separate passage, the memorandum describes work at Princeton by a dean whose name the recognised text renders as Robert John, together with Brenda Dunne, in which forty factors, queried in the text as "essences," were extracted from target material and their frequency and probability of occurrence analysed. The visitors found the approach interesting, and their parenthetical question marks show that parts of it were not clear to them either.

### The box technique, the target pool, and tunnel work

The group discussed the box technique for remote viewing, which Hammid had performed in all of SRI's box sessions. Targ said he had no compelling rationale for the four-by-four, sixteen-bin arrangement beyond the fact that it nicely represents a geographical coordinate grid and that he had planned to use the structure later as a bookcase of that size. The criteria for choosing objects were discussed in detail: objects should not be personally distasteful to the viewer, should be instantly recognisable, and should be easily distinguishable from one another in shape and texture, a requirement the group found surprisingly difficult, since objects that sound very different, such as a compass and a pair of eyeglasses, can share the description round, glass, and smooth.

The visitors' own planned experiment, a target pool of twenty sites at Aberdeen Proving Ground with the location of a reactor as the objective, was discussed and thought a good experiment, with coordinate precision identified as the main risk. Targ and Hammid suggested that other location methods, including grid search, map dowsing, and pendulum techniques, might prove interesting. On tunnel location, Targ expressed frustration that earlier SRI work had produced known tunnels, which drew no interest, and new locations on which the group received virtually no feedback or follow-up, and he was adamant that SRI would do no further tunnel work unless a response and follow-up were assured. He also stated, as his own account, that Marines had dowsed for tunnels and caches with some success and that experiments at Quantico had also been successful, citing a book, The Divining Hand by Christopher Bird.

### Null targets, videotaping, and a disputed matching

The visitors' null target sessions were discussed with Targ, Puthoff, and Hammid, who were, in the memorandum's words, frankly appalled at the idea. They considered it a form of outright trickery against the viewer, detrimental to the process and liable to destroy the trust between viewer and experimenters, and Hammid indicated that she would not participate in a series of sessions even if she had been told that a null target was a possibility. Targ and May were interested in the visitors' use of videotaping and thought it a good idea.

The memorandum closes with the training programme for the INSCOM viewers, on which Targ relayed no details beyond saying that things were going well, and with a disputed evaluation. Targ was, the visitors write, totally exercised about the evaluation of six beacon sessions by one INSCOM viewer, in which Beverly Humphrey, using May's concept evaluation procedure, correctly matched four of the six and switched the remaining two, a large stone building with columns and a cemetery. When the visitors were shown the original drawing on which Targ relied, they found it was one of seven or eight small drawings on a page, with further pages of similar amounts of drawing, and they observed that the drawing could also represent a mausoleum at a cemetery. Targ was not receptive to their comments; in later discussion, May and Humphrey indicated that they stood firm by their results.

## Errors and Anomalies

The Drive filename garbles the subject's surname and misdescribes the document. The memorandum itself names her as Hella Hammid throughout, and it is a visit report, not a methodology explainer under the filename's title. The ledger card makes the same two points, and this assessment follows the document.

The recognised text renders one Princeton researcher's name as "Dean Robert John." That reading is reproduced in the content account above exactly as it stands in the recognised text, flagged here rather than silently corrected, because the recognised text alone does not establish whether the rendering is a recognition error or the memorandum's own wording.

The distribution list at the end of the memorandum is largely illegible in the recognised text; one name, rendered as John Kramar, is partly legible, and no distribution claim in this assessment rests on that passage. Release stamps and running heads are garbled in places throughout the scan, as is common in recognition of stamped pages, and the memorandum's spelling of the Egyptian collaborator's name appears in the text as Steve Schwartz.

Several judgments in the memorandum are the visitors' impressions of the people they met, most plainly their remarks about Hammid's dependence on Targ and about Targ's state of mind over the interim note. This assessment attributes those judgments to the visitors who wrote them.

## Limits

This assessment is based on the full recognised text of the memorandum, 17,405 characters across six pages, read in its entirety. The memorandum is a record of conversations and of the visitors' impressions during a single two-day visit. It contains no session transcripts, no target material, no scoring data, and no independent test of any claim made in conversation. Statements attributed above to Hammid, to Targ, to Puthoff, or to the visitors are reported as those speakers' own statements as recorded in the memorandum. Whether remote viewing occurs is not adjudicated here; the memorandum itself does not attempt to settle that question, and it records at least one evaluation dispute, over the six beacon sessions, in which the participants did not agree.
