## Premise

The paper was written to serve as the basic technical reference for a research program at Duke University's Experiential Learning Laboratory that aimed to study physiological correlates of psi — fluctuations in bodily state, above all in the electroencephalogram (EEG), measured alongside performance in parapsychological tasks. Its premise is methodological: the authors state that it has not yet been clearly established that psi events bear orderly relations to physiological events, and that exploring the question is technically demanding, so the paper documents the computer-based facility — hardware, real-time software, data management, calibration, and statistical analysis — built to make such research possible, as the foundation for later experimental reports.

## Abstract

This paper describes a computer-based laboratory facility built in the Department of Electrical Engineering at Duke University for the psychophysiological study of psi. The authors' program seeks relationships between performance in parapsychological tasks and measurable fluctuations in bodily state, with the EEG as the signal of greatest interest because of its close relationship to information processing in the brain. The facility is organized in three physically separate areas: a computer room housing a PDP-11, an instrumentation room where the experimenter monitors a session, and an electromagnetically shielded controlled-environment room for the subject, some hundred feet and two floors away. A downstairs microcomputer based on an 8085 processor digitizes physiological signals and transfers them to the PDP-11 through a digital multiplexer. The software system is written almost entirely in FORTRAN so that complete experimental protocols can be constructed quickly; it includes a clock-driven, priority-ordered real-time system, circular data buffers that preserve a continuously updated record of recent physiology, and an interactive data-management and statistical-analysis system built around self-describing standard-format data files. Special procedures for daily calibration and system measurement protect the integrity of the physiological data. The authors conclude that the facility is the most elaborate resource parapsychologists have yet had for studying momentary relationships between brain areas, while acknowledging essentially complete ignorance of the physiological basis of psi processes. ## Content Account

The paper opens with a candid statement of its field's position: although it has not yet been clearly established that psi events bear orderly relations to physiological events, theoretical and empirical considerations converge to suggest that they should, and that the relevant events may be measurable. The authors list what discovering consistent relationships could in principle yield: improved prediction and control, resolution of experimental ambiguities concerning the time and source of psi effects, and possibly fundamental insight into the mechanisms underlying psi. Their central strategy is to study fluctuations in psi task performance in relation to fluctuations of bodily state as reflected in electrical and mechanical signals measurable at the body surface, with the EEG the signal of greatest interest because of its intimate relationship to many information-processing activities of the brain. Because the research is technically demanding, the group devoted a large proportion of its initial effort to constructing a suitable technical base, and the paper presents that base as the reference for subsequent experimental reports. A footnote records support from the American Society for Psychical Research, the John E. Fetzer Foundation, the Foundation for Research on the Nature of Man, the James McDonnell Foundation, a National Institute of Mental Health small grant, and the Psychical Research Foundation, with further acknowledgment to the Parapsychology Foundation and the Arthur Vining Davis Foundations.

The facility itself occupies three physically separate regions. The computer, a PDP-11 owned by the Electrical Engineering Department, sits on the first floor of the new engineering annex. The instrumentation room, where the experimenter monitors a session in progress, and the adjacent controlled-environment room, where the subject sits during a session, are in the sub-basement of the old engineering building, some hundred feet, three walls, and two floors distant; the subject room is electromagnetically shielded. Data travel between the areas over multichannel paths, with analog tape storage and a polygraph alongside the digital chain.

The hardware description centers on a two-level computing arrangement. Downstairs, an 8085-based microcomputer works partly independently and in parallel with the PDP-11: when its clock ticks, it converts the physiology to digital form and transfers the results to the PDP-11 through the digital multiplexer. It can also execute "remote events," including driving feedback devices and triggering a random number generator. Before an experiment begins, the PDP-11 loads the 8085 through an auxiliary serial port with the program data for the remote events that protocol requires; the subroutines themselves are written and compiled on the PDP-11 with a cross-assembler, so all program development, storage, and execution are handled on the larger machine even though the microcomputer can also run stand-alone.

The software organization is the paper's main subject. The real-time system is clock-driven and priority-ordered: whenever the clock ticks, whatever processing is under way is interrupted and put on hold, the system inspects an internal list of scheduled events, executes the associated subroutines in priority order, and then resumes the interrupted program. The authors contrast this with systems in which a user requests a service and waits idle until it completes. Auxiliary subroutines give FORTRAN programs access to the laboratory peripheral system, including analog and digital input and output, an LED display, and relays. A further group of routines forms queues — circular data buffers in which data enter at one end and are removed at the other — maintaining a continuously updated record of physiological data from the immediate past, which matters when the event of interest is a self-initiated response in an ESP task whose timing is not known in advance. The authors report that the facilities can be used effectively by persons with moderate FORTRAN skill, and that initial programming time for a new experiment typically ranges from a few hours to a few days even for intricate experiments, with modifications usually easy once a protocol's basic structure exists.

The second half of the system handles data after acquisition. It is written almost entirely in FORTRAN, making it relatively machine-independent and exportable, and it is designed to demand roughly the sophistication required for commercial statistical packages such as SAS or BMD. Its key concept is the standard file format: real-time programs write output directly in standard form, and all later processing reads and writes standard files. Such files are self-describing — header records describe the structure and content of the data that follow, and only the first record is fixed in structure, so a wide variety of experiments can be accommodated in one uniform system of data management, reduction, display, and multivariate statistical analysis. The paper also describes computer-assisted daily-calibration and system-measurement procedures developed to protect the integrity of the physiological data, with the most detailed material reserved to appendices available from the authors on request.

The discussion returns to the scientific problem. The authors acknowledge, in their words, essentially complete ignorance of the physiological basis of psi processes: they do not know what they are looking for, and so must look as widely and searchingly as possible. Their single strongest intuition is that the key to psychophysiological understanding, not only of psi functions but of all human mental functions, lies in the analysis of momentary relationships between brain areas, citing Luria (1973). They describe the system as the most elaborate resource parapsychologists have yet had at their disposal for this kind of research, and close on a conditional: if physiological signals derived from the cortex do harbor traces of psi processes, they believe they now have research tools powerful enough to detect and study them. The reference list spans EEG analysis, random data, random number generators for psi tests, and prior psychophysiological studies, and gives the authors' address as the Department of Electrical Engineering, Duke University, Durham, North Carolina.
