## Premise

The article was written by the CIA project officer who had run much of the agency's early-1970s parapsychology work, to record his personal account of that work for future officers who might have to evaluate the subject. It sets out to summarize selected highlights rather than a complete history, to state plainly that the agency's investigation was never completed, and to argue, from the author's experience, that the data contained genuinely unexplained results of intelligence significance alongside mixed and unusable ones, and that a proper assessment had still not been made.

## Abstract

This item is a personal review article by Dr. Kenneth A. Kress, the CIA project officer for much of the agency's early parapsychology work, published in Studies in Intelligence in Winter 1977. Kress traces the agency's involvement from a 1965 review prepared under Project ULTRA, through Russell Targ and Harold Puthoff's 1972 demonstrations at SRI International, the small Office of Technical Services contracts that followed, and the decision at Director level in 1973 to table the work as too sensitive. He describes the 1973 remote-viewing experiment in which Pat Price described a sensitive government installation near a vacation-property target, the jointly funded 1974 program and its disputes over rigor, the four-state teaching machine results and their reanalysis, and the first operational collection attempt, against the Semipalatinsk facility, which he judges inconclusive overall despite an accurate description of its crane. He recounts the embassy coderoom test, the meetings that ended agency research funding, and the final Libya tasking, cut short by Price's death. His conclusion is that the phenomena were demonstrated in part but never explained or made reproducible, that applications-oriented agencies press for results too early, and that only long-term basic research funding would let the subject rise or fall on its merits.

## Content Account

Kress begins with his framing. The agency sponsored serious parapsychological research, but circumstances, biases, and fear of ridicule prevented it from completing a scientific investigation. He names the "Proxmire Effect," the fear that research contracts would be held up for scorn, and the polarized reactions the subject provokes, as obstacles to careful evaluation. His central claim, stated as his own, is that tantalizing but incomplete data were generated, that on occasion unexplained results of genuine intelligence significance occur, and that this is not to say parapsychology is a proven intelligence tool, only that the evaluation is not yet complete. Attention is confined to psychokinetics and remote viewing, which he defines.

The early history he recounts runs from anecdotal reports reaching national security agencies since the Second World War, through a 1952 Pentagon lecture on extrasensory perception in psychological warfare, to one of the earliest government investigations in 1961, when the chief of CIA's Office of Technical Service became interested and Stephen I. Abrams of Oxford's Parapsychological Laboratory prepared a review under Project ULTRA claiming that extrasensory perception was demonstrated but not understood or controllable. The report produced no further action for a decade.

The revival came through two laser physicists, Russell Targ and Harold E. Puthoff. In April 1972 Targ met CIA personnel, Soviet psychokinesis films were shown to analysts, and a laboratory demonstration followed in which a subject, taken on a surprise visit to a superconducting shielded magnetometer used in Stanford quark experiments, appeared to disturb its output when he placed his attention on its interior, with correlations never seen before or after the visit. The Office of Technical Services then funded a demonstration at SRI for $874 in August 1972, in which the subject described hidden objects with what Kress calls startling accuracy; a further $2,500 work order followed, and by October 1972 Kress was project officer over a $50,000 expanded effort.

His account of that effort is candid about its character. The paranormal abilities seemed individualistic: one subject apparently raised a thermistor's temperature and the second could not duplicate it; the second reproduced information sealed in envelopes and the first could not; and repeating the same experiment with the same subject did not yield consistent results. Kress writes that he began to have serious feelings of being involved with a fraud. In April and May 1973 the request to expand went to William Colby and then toward the Director, James Schlesinger; Kress was told not to increase the scope and not to anticipate follow-on work, because the project was too sensitive and potentially embarrassing, and it should be tabled. He notes the timing: the Office of Technical Services was then being investigated for involvement in Watergate.

The turning point, in his account, was an informal experiment in the summer of 1973. Two subjects, one of them Pat Price, were given only the coordinates of a vacation property and described, apparently as misses, a military-like facility. The correlation of the two independent descriptions led an Office of Scientific Intelligence officer to visit the area and discover a sensitive government installation a few miles away. Price was then asked about that site's interior workings and, in Kress's account, provided a list of project titles including one of extreme sensitivity and the site's codename, together with accurate information about its physical layout, while other information, such as the names of people at the site, proved incorrect. In February 1974 a jointly funded program began, run on the premise that the phenomena existed: the Office of Research and Development funded basic studies and the Office of Technical Services funded evaluation of operational utility.

Kress describes the program's internal disputes in detail. The basic research tasking proved more extensive than time and funds allowed, and the rigor of the research became a serious issue between the Office of Research and Development officers and SRI, with Kress generally between them. His extended example is the four-state electronic random number generator, the "ESP teaching machine": SRI reported a subject averaging a statistically very significant 29 percent correct over more than 2,500 trials, against a chance expectation of 25 percent. Skeptical reviewers argued the machine might not be random and the subject might have learned its patterns; Kress had the data reanalyzed by the Office of Joint Computer Services, which found no evidence of nonrandomness and no solid reason for the subject's success, and the subject averaged more than 28 percent in a further 2,500 trials on retest. The reviewers, he writes, maintained there must be another flaw but that it was not worth finding.

The first intelligence collection operation used Price against the Semipalatinsk Unidentified Research and Development Facility-3 in the USSR. Kress designed a characterization protocol: two known features, a tall crane and four structures resembling oil well derricks, would show the channel was working. Price, given only coordinates, a marked atlas map, and the information that it was a Soviet research and development test site, produced a drawing of the crane overnight, remarking on its size from the scale of a person beside its wheels. The derricks were never described; when later confronted, Price answered that they were not there any more. Later reconnaissance showed two derricks partially disassembled but all four basically visible. Kress's verdict on the operation is explicit: in general most of Price's data were wrong or could not be evaluated; he produced some amazing descriptions, including buildings under construction, spherical tank sections, and the crane, which two analysts agreed was accurate; but, with so much bad information mixed with the good, the overall result was not considered useful, and as proof of remote viewing the data are at best inconclusive.

A final operational challenge produced the coderoom test: Price, given exterior photographs and coordinates of two foreign embassies whose interiors were known to audio operations teams, correctly located the coderooms in both cases and produced copious accurate detail alongside the usual vague and incorrect material; the operations officer involved concluded, as quoted, that the technique offered definite operational possibilities. The review meetings that followed nonetheless ended the research: the Office of Research and Development officers judged the research not productive or competent and dropped support, the Director of the Office of Technical Services judged its charter would not support research, all agency funding in paranormal research stopped, operational utility was considered questionable but deserving further testing, and testing moved to a personal services contract with Price under an operations psychologist, Kress having been judged a positively biased advocate. The last tasking concerned Libyan sites: Price described a guerrilla training site and a related underwater sabotage training facility, some of which was verified by reconnaissance and matched a collateral agent report, and a second requirements list was passed to him. Price died of a heart attack a few days later, and the program stopped. Kress records that there had been no further CIA-sponsored intelligence collection tests.

His postscript surveys the other agencies as he knew them: DIA interested on a low priority basis; the Army Materiel Command's interest, arising from Vietnam point men, fading after a few meetings; a hostile DARPA briefing after which contacts stopped for years; Navy replication funding stopped over concern the research was mind-warfare related; and current work shifted to the Air Force Foreign Technology Division, with results more consistent than the CIA's but still mixed. His conclusion returns to his premise: there is no fundamental understanding of the mechanisms and reproducibility remains poor; applications-oriented agencies demand quick results before reproducibility or understanding exists; and unless agencies commit long-term basic research funds and confine attention to abilities reproducible enough to augment other collection techniques, the situation is unlikely to change.

## Errors and Anomalies

OCR noise is heavy in the page furniture and moderate in the body; the text is nonetheless legible in substance throughout, and names and figures are used here only where legible. Two date renderings in the OCR text are internally impossible and are flagged rather than corrected: the narrative places the management-committee approval in May of the year rendered as 1978, in a sequence that runs from October 1972 to the summer of 1973, and a facility designation is rendered both URDF-3 and, in places, URDF-8. This assessment does not silently substitute corrected dates or designations; where the sequence matters it follows the article's own chronology of events. Kress himself flags the limits of his account: it is a personal review, expressly not historically complete, written by an officer whom his own management judged a positively biased advocate, a judgment he reports himself.
