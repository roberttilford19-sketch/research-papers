## Premise

The file was produced to circulate, inside the intelligence community, a piece of foreign press reporting about that community's own psychic-spying programs. The underlying article, Jim Schnabel's "Tinker, Tailor, Soldier, Psi," published in London's Independent on Sunday on 27 August 1995, sets out to reconstruct the history of United States government remote viewing — its Cold War origins, its institutional life, and its decline — from the testimony of retired servicemen and intelligence officials.

## Abstract

This file is a memorandum dated 28 August 1995, marked "FYI. Interesting stuff," routing the Foreign Broadcast Information Service's five takes, dated 27 August 1995, of Jim Schnabel's "Tinker, Tailor, Soldier, Psi" from the Independent on Sunday. The article argues, on the stated belief of retired servicemen and intelligence officials, that United States intelligence seriously used psychic phenomena for two decades, tracing the origin to Cold War "psi gap" anxiety: a 1972 Defense Intelligence Agency report warned Soviet research might let Moscow read top-secret documents, mould leaders' thoughts, kill officials at a distance, and disable equipment. It describes CIA funding, from late 1972, of Harold Puthoff and Ingo Swann at Stanford Research Institute, where "remote viewing" was adopted; Pat Price's June 1973 description of an NSA facility; pooling under the DIA program Grill Flame; and the Army's 1978 unit at Fort Meade. It distinguishes extended remote viewing from Swann's structured coordinate remote viewing, and recounts former viewers' claims: a compromised agent, a KGB colonel, hostages in Tehran and the Middle East, Manuel Noriega in 1989, and al-Qadhdhafi before the 1986 Libya raid. Its second half traces decline: Albert Stubblebine's championship and career-ending 1983 incident; the unit's 1986 expulsion from the Army and adoption by the Defense Intelligence Agency as Center Lane and Sun Streak; Ed Dames's extraterrestrial and commercial work; and a 1988 Inspector General review preceded, Dames and Mel Riley claim, by shredding. It closes with the unit as a ghost of its former self.

## Content Account

The article is history built from interviews, and its evidentiary layers should be kept distinct. Its documentary anchor is the quoted 1972 DIA report, whose list of feared Soviet capabilities it reproduces and itself characterizes as now sounding "absurdly alarmist." Around that anchor it arranges named-source testimony: Puthoff on the program's SRI beginnings and his initial grant; Major General Edmund Thompson, who encouraged the Fort Meade unit's creation, on becoming convinced remote viewing was real; Mel Riley, a founder member of the unit, on its methods, cases, and atmosphere; and Ed Dames on Stubblebine, the Monroe Institute episode, and the 1988 review. Other claims rest on unnamed former viewers and officials, and the article flags that basis as it goes ("if a number of retired servicemen and intelligence officials are to be believed"; "according to several former remote-viewers, as well as officials familiar with the programme").

The operational catalogue — Tehran hostages, Noriega, Qadhdhafi, Silkworm missiles, Scud locations during Desert Storm — is presented in the article as the claims of those sources, not as documented fact, and this assessment preserves that attribution. The same discipline applies to the article's most colourful material: Stubblebine's spoon-bending sessions and the "yellow salamander" briefing anecdote, the channelled entities "Maurice" and "George," Dames's Martian civilization and his August 1992 prediction, and the claim that files were shredded ahead of the Inspector General team. These are reported speech and recollection within a newspaper feature, vivid and specific, but unverified by any document reproduced in the file.

What the file itself, as a government artifact, establishes is administrative: that the article was transcribed by FBIS on the day of publication, circulated under the subject line "US Use of 'Psychic Spies' Reported" on 28 August 1995 with an "FYI" note, and later released through CIA CREST. The file contains no official confirmation or denial of the article's contents.

## Errors and Anomalies

The text available to this desk is optical character recognition of the released file, with moderate noise: the FBIS header blocks repeat before each take, marginalia and redaction codes (SG1I, SG1J) intrude into the text, and small passages are garbled, though the article's narrative is recoverable throughout. The transcription repeatedly renders the Libyan leader's name in the article's own spelling variants. No substantive wording has been silently repaired. One framing point in the article deserves flagging rather than correction: its opening scene, in which viewers track a fleeing dictator around the clock before an attack, is a dramatized reconstruction in the article's own voice, explicitly reframed by the author as reported history only "if" his sources are to be believed.
