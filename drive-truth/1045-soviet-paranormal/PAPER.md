## Abstract

This article, published in the National Security Agency journal Cryptolog in December 1983, reports a talk by Russell Targ at the Seminar on Applied Anomalous Phenomena, held at the Xerox center in Leesburg on 1 December 1983, describing his recent visit to Soviet psi research facilities. Targ cautioned that he saw no experiments performed and could not exclude disinformation. As reported here, Soviet work was concentrated in Moscow hospitals and framed by an officially acceptable theory of low-frequency radio propagation; it included galvanic skin response measurement, a "rat telepathy" experiment analyzed by computer, extremely low frequency treatment of patients, a reader said to read pages in another room only when given feedback, and remote viewing experiments in Yerevan whose student viewers sometimes described targets before the agent arrived, a precognition result Soviet theorists could not accommodate. The article's closing analysis considers possible intelligence applications of each reported line of work and notes the absence, in Targ's account, of the experimenter-subject rapport emphasized in American work. Every substantive claim in the article is Targ's report of what Soviet researchers told him, and the article presents it on that basis.

## Content Account

The article's frame is set by its subject's own caveat, which it reports early and repeats in substance at the end: Targ could report only what the Russians had told him of their work; he saw no experiments conducted; and he could not prove that the results reported to him were not intentional disinformation. Within that frame, the article describes a Soviet field working under an ideological constraint. A theory of low-frequency radio propagation, associated with a scientist named Kagan and his book on information theory, served as the officially acceptable explanation of telepathy phenomena, and work that fit it could proceed; work that did not, above all precognition, met resistance. The article reports that the Soviet Academy of Sciences maintained a "Department for Research on Theoretical Problems" headed by an academician named Andrianka who was its only member, and that much of the unclassified work took place in Moscow hospitals, where it was concerned with measuring subtle physiological changes, particularly galvanic skin response, correlated with stimuli including the onset of hypnosis. Targ's access had itself been negotiated: invited informally at an August 1983 meeting, he refused to travel as a tourist because of his defense work, came instead as an officially invited guest of the Academy, received the invitation two days after the KAL 007 shootdown, and traveled with his Russian-speaking daughter as translator.

The specific work reported falls into a few lines. In the hospital research, low-frequency radio waves in the ten-to-one-hundred-hertz range were used in treating cancer patients and others, with patients reported to have mystical experiences and, in some cases, apparent cures; computer analysis of galvanic response data, using fast Fourier transforms and autocorrelation, was used to detect responses to stimuli and, in one researcher's claim, the exact instant a subject enters hypnosis. A large experiment in "rat telepathy" was described to Targ in detail: rats raised together and shock-conditioned to a warning light were separated, in some experiments by more than a kilometre, with cages linked by modem, and the unshocked rat's galvanic fear reaction was measured when its partner was shocked. A noted psychic, named in the article as Nina Kalada, was reported to read a page of a book in another room with results well above random, but only when she received immediate feedback on her accuracy; without feedback her results fell to chance, from which Targ drew the wry conclusion, reported here, that documents in locked safes in the United States Embassy in Moscow were safe from her. In Yerevan, Armenian researchers described remote viewing experiments in which students described sites an agent was visiting; their difficulty, confided to the Targ party privately, was that viewers sometimes described the site before the agent reached it, a precognition result they could neither publish nor reconcile with accepted theory. The article also reports friction within the Soviet establishment, including marked antagonism between the Academy scientists and the electrical engineering society led by Yuri Guliyayev, who was measuring electromagnetic emissions from the body and insisted he was doing physics, not extrasensory perception.

The article's own contribution is its closing analysis, set apart under that heading. It works through the reported lines of research one by one and asks what each would imply if it worked reliably: the book-reading ability would have communications security implications; the galvanic response research could be applied to electronic interrogation and debriefing; the rat experiments might extend to detecting stress in arrested agents; and dowsing, which the article notes was taught to professional mineralogists and geologists at Tomsk Polytechnical Institute, would have obvious intelligence applications. It cites a Congressional Research Service study for further reported Soviet interests, including remote hypnotic manipulation and psychokinesis, and it records the speculation, which it explicitly marks as unconfirmed, that the Soviet Union was spending tens of millions of dollars on psi research directed to military ends. Its summary is measured: psi work remained nebulous and unpredictable; the Russians experimented openly where an acceptable theoretical base existed and rejected effects for which none existed; and the one-man department in the Academy suggested that established disciplines wished to keep their distance from results they nonetheless could not ignore. The article ends by noting what Targ's account never mentioned: the emotional rapport between experimenter and subject that American work treated as central, and the difficulty of repeating experiments once that emotional charge fades. All of the Soviet-side claims in this account are Targ's reports of what he was told, as the article itself presents them; the analysis section's applications are the journal's own reasoning from those reports, and this assessment adopts neither.

## Errors and Anomalies

The recovered text is an optical character recognition rendering of a two-column journal layout, and the columns interleave in the reading order, so the article must be followed by sense rather than by line order; this assessment's account was assembled on that basis. Character-level noise is frequent, and proper names are the chief casualty: forms such as "Guberev," "Andrianka," "Kalada," and "Gasumsan" are the recovered text's renderings, are reported here as rendered, and should not be cited as settled spellings without checking the scan or another source. The article's first page carries a decorative "Looking Around" heading treatment that yields almost no legible text, and a cartoon on the fourth page yields only fragments of its dialogue; neither affects the article's argument. Classification and portion markings from the original journal, and the 2011 sanitized-release stamps, appear throughout the recovered text and are artifacts of the document's release history rather than content.
