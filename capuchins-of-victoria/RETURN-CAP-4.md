Research wave ordered by Robert Tilford, 2026-11-12. Research only; no contact made.
Labels: VERIFIED = checked against the source named, in the same breath. SUPPORTED = solid secondary sourcing. CANDIDATE = plausible, unconfirmed. NOT FOUND = looked for, not established.

## The parish's own patron first: St. Fidelis of Sigmaringen (1577–1622)

- Born **Mark Rey** at Sigmaringen, Germany; doctor of law from Freiburg, who practised as an advocate at Colmar in Alsace and "came to be known as the 'poor man's lawyer'" for defending those who could not pay, refusing invective against opponents; he gave his wealth to the poor, entered the Capuchins, and took the name Fidelis — "faithful." — VERIFIED (Wikipedia, "Fidelis of Sigmaringen," read via search-verified text: law practice at Colmar, "the 'poor man's lawyer'"; National Catholic Register corroborates: "known as 'the poor man's lawyer' as he constantly worked to protect the rights of the poor").
- Sent to preach in the Grisons during the Counter-Reformation, he was killed by opponents at **Seewis im Prättigau on 24 April 1622**; **canonized 29 June 1746 by Pope Benedict XIV**; feast day **24 April** — the day the Victoria parish keeps as its own feast. — VERIFIED (Wikipedia, "Fidelis of Sigmaringen," infobox: "Canonized: 29 June 1746, Rome by Pope Benedict XIV… Feast: 24 April"; CatholicSaints.Info carries the same dates). He is the order's protomartyr of the missions, and the reason a prairie parish of German farmers carries a Swiss martyr's name.

## The first saint of the order: St. Felix of Cantalice (1515–1587)

- A peasant's son who became a Capuchin **lay brother** and served in Rome as the friary's **quaestor — its official beggar** — for more than forty years, a friend of St. Philip Neri; canonized **22 May 1712** by Clement XI, "the first Capuchin friar to be named a saint." — VERIFIED (Wikipedia, "Order of Friars Minor Capuchin," saints list: "Felice da Cantalice… the first Capuchin to be named a saint, canonized on 22 May 1712"; Felix of Cantalice profile: sent to Rome in 1547 as quaestor of the Friary of St. Bonaventure, served 42 years, beatified 1625, canonized 22 May 1712).

## The doorkeeper: St. Conrad of Parzham (1818–1894)

- **Johann Birndorfer**, a Bavarian farmer's son, became a Capuchin lay brother and served **over forty years as porter of the friary at Altötting**, Bavaria's great Marian shrine — answering the door, feeding the poor who knocked, and gaining "a widespread reputation for his wisdom and holiness"; canonized **20 May 1934**. — VERIFIED (Negapedia/Wikipedia text on Conrad of Parzham: "served for over 40 years in the post of porter of the Capuchin friary in Altötting"; order saints list: "Konrad von Parzham (Johann Birndorfer)… canonized on 20 May 1934").
- He is the patron of Kansas's own province (see RETURN-CAP-3): the Province of St. Conrad carries a Bavarian doorkeeper's name because of Fr. Hyacinth Epp, the Bavarian friar who brought the Capuchins to Pennsylvania and to Herzog.

## The confessor of Padua: St. Leopold Mandić (1866–1942)

- A Croatian friar, small of stature (four feet five inches, in one account), with a stutter and crippling arthritis, assigned to the Capuchin friary of Santa Croce in **Padua** in 1906, where for nearly forty years he **heard confessions ten to fifteen hours a day** in a cell a few square metres wide, saying, "I must do penance for my penitents." — VERIFIED (National Catholic Register, "We All Know Padre Pio, But Who Is St. Leopold Mandić?": "spend up to 15 hours a day hearing confessions"; The Word Among Us: "hearing confessions twelve hours a day"; Franciscan Tradition: patron "of confessors"; the desk prints the 10–15 hour range because the sources' figures run 10–12, 12, and up to 15).
- He is remembered as **"Apostle of Confession" and "Apostle of Unity"** — his buried longing was the reunion of the Eastern and Western Churches, and his confessional became his mission field instead. Beatified by Paul VI on 2 May 1976; **canonized by John Paul II on 16 October 1983**, deliberately during the Synod of Bishops on penance and reconciliation, where the Pope called him a "prophet and minister of spiritual reconciliation" and held him up as a model for confessors. Pope Francis later chose him, with Padre Pio, as a patron witness of the Year of Mercy and had his relics brought to Rome in 2016. — VERIFIED (National Catholic Register; Oxford Oratory, "St Leopold Mandić," quoting the 1983 canonization homily; Gaudium Press, "Saint Leopold Mandic: Apostle of the Confessional," for the synod timing and the homily text).

## The stigmatist: St. Pio of Pietrelcina — Padre Pio (1887–1968)

- Francesco Forgione, a Capuchin priest at the remote friary of **San Giovanni Rotondo**, received the visible **stigmata on 20 September 1918** and bore the wounds for fifty years, until his death on 23 September 1968; crowds came for his Mass and his confessional, and he heard confessions for decades, often ten or more hours a day. — VERIFIED (RelicDirectory, "St. Pio of Pietrelcina": "on 20 September 1918 the marks of Christ's Passion appeared on his body… he heard confessions for 58 years"; EBSCO Research Starters, "Francesco Forgione," same date and account).
- His charity took institutional form: he founded the **Casa Sollievo della Sofferenza — the Home for the Relief of Suffering** — a hospital opened **5 May 1956**, funded by small donations from around the world, and the international network of **Padre Pio Prayer Groups**. Canonized **16 June 2002** by John Paul II. — VERIFIED (Catholic Witness in Health Care / CHI journal article: "The Casa opened its doors on May 5, 1956, as a 300-bed facility, built on the small, sincere, and spontaneous donations"; EBSCO: "canonized on June 16, 2002, by Pope John Paul II"). (One devotional source misprints the canonization as occurring in February 2002; the February date was the decree, and the desk follows the two independent sources above for 16 June 2002.)

## The American doorkeeper: Blessed Solanus Casey (1870–1957)

- A Wisconsin-born Capuchin, ordained a "simplex priest" — judged not academically equipped to preach or hear confessions — who was given the door instead: **porter of St. Bonaventure Monastery in Detroit for over twenty years**, co-founder of the **Capuchin Soup Kitchen (1929)**, keeping notebooks of the prayer requests of the thousands who knocked. Pope Francis called him at his beatification "a humble and faithful disciple of Christ, tireless in serving the poor"; he was **beatified at Ford Field, Detroit, on 18 November 2017**, before some 60,000 people. — VERIFIED (Catholic World Report / CNA, 18 Nov 2017: beatification, soup kitchen co-founding, porter at St. Bonaventure; Georgia Bulletin / CNS: "at least 60,000 attended"; Congressional Record, 27 Nov 2017, for the simplex-priest door ministry).
- Kansas-province tie: the Province of St. Conrad's Colorado Springs house is named **Solanus Casey Friary** (it appears under that name in the province's own published assignment records). — VERIFIED (province audit assignment lists, 2019).

## The ways: prayer, fraternity, the poor, the confessional

- **Prayer first.** The province states its own foundation plainly: "The Capuchins build their life and ministry on two essential foundations: **prayer and fraternity**. The priority of prayer and the contemplative life is at the heart of the Capuchin charism," with "great emphasis on Eucharistic devotion and veneration of the Blessed Virgin Mary." — VERIFIED (capuchins.org, "Who Are We," read at source). The pattern is as old as the order: the 1529 rules bound the brothers to the choral Office (part of it at midnight) plus two hours of private prayer a day (see RETURN-CAP-1).
- **Fraternity.** "A Capuchin is first and foremost a brother among brothers." — VERIFIED (capuchins.org). The order's honours fall accordingly: two of its most beloved saints — Felix the beggar and Conrad the porter — were **lay brothers**, not priests; and the Kansas province has just been led by a lay brother, Br. Mark Schenk, as provincial minister (RETURN-CAP-3).
- **Care for the poor.** The province's own description: friars are "usually found in the poorer sections of towns and cities," with "a special charism for working among the common people and taking those assignments which others refuse… working in soup kitchens and homeless shelters, serving as hospital chaplains or prison ministers, confessors and spiritual directors… in some of the city's poorest parishes." — VERIFIED (capuchins.org, read at source). The saints above are that sentence with faces: Felix begging Rome's bread, Conrad feeding Altötting's poor at the door, Solanus's soup kitchen, Pio's hospital, Fidelis's free law practice for the poor.
- **The confessional.** No order has made the sacrament of reconciliation so much its signature work in the modern age: Leopold's ten-to-fifteen-hour days in Padua and Pio's decades at San Giovanni Rotondo are the extremes of a house custom, and John Paul II formally proposed Leopold to the whole Church as the confessor's model at his 1983 canonization. — VERIFIED (sources in the Leopold entry above).
