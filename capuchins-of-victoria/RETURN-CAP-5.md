# RETURN-CAP-5 — THE NAME IN THE PLACE: Victoria, Herzog, and the Volga German road

Research wave ordered by Robert Tilford, 2026-11-12. Research only; no contact made.
Labels: VERIFIED = checked against the source named, in the same breath. SUPPORTED = solid secondary sourcing. CANDIDATE = plausible, unconfirmed. NOT FOUND = looked for, not established.

## The invitation (1762–1763)

- Catherine II of Russia — Catherine the Great, herself German-born — issued two manifestos inviting foreigners to settle Russia's empty steppe along the Volga. The first, in **1762** (the year she took the throne), drew little response; the second, in **July 1763**, liberalized the offer and sent recruiting agents into Germany. — VERIFIED (S. J. Sackett, *History of the Volga Germans in Ellis and Rush Counties, Kansas*, Emporia State: "In 1763 Catherine the Great of Russia issued the second of two manifestos… The first manifesto, promulgated the previous year, had had no effect"; Hoffman thesis, Emporia Kansas State College, 1975: "In July of 1763, Catherine issued a Manifest"). A Fort Hays State collections guide dates the manifesto **25 July 1763**; Sackett and Hoffman give the month without the day, and the desk prints the day as carried by that single guide — SUPPORTED for the day, VERIFIED for July 1763. (The manifesto is conventionally dated 22 July O.S., which is the likely root of the 25 July variant; the desk notes the variance rather than resolving it from these sources alone.)
- What the 1763 manifesto promised: **transport help and loans, land, internal self-government, the free exercise of religion, exemption from taxation for thirty years, and freedom from military service** — the promise the settlers prized most. — VERIFIED (Sackett for the tax, loan, and military terms; Hoffman for "Freedom from military service for one hundred years; freedom from taxation for thirty years… internal self-government; and the free exercise of religion"; Fort Hays State University, "Kansas Heritage: Ellis County," for the summary: "travel costs, religious freedom, 30 years of tax exemption, and most of all, freedom from military service forever"). Desk note: the sources differ on the military exemption's term — "one hundred years" (Hoffman) versus "forever" (FHSU guide). Both wordings ride in the settlers' own memory of the promise; the returns preserve both.
- The response: between 1763 and 1767 **about 8,000 families — roughly 25,000 Germans**, mostly from Hesse and the Rhineland, worn down by war and want — migrated to the Volga, and **by 1768 they had founded 104 colonies**. They lived there a century and more, German-speaking, self-governing, and largely apart. — VERIFIED (Sackett: "about eight thousand families, about twenty-five thousand Germans… By 1768 these people had founded 104 colonies"; Hoffman: "from 1763 to 1767 about 8000 families from the German states of Hesse and the Rhineland migrated to the Volga region").

## Herzog, Russia

- Among the earliest of those colonies was **Herzog**, founded in **1764** on the Karaman River near its junction with the Volga — "The original Herzog antedated even Katharinenstadt." Its Russian name was **Susly**. — VERIFIED (Sackett: "Herzog was established [in Kansas] half a mile north of the English settlement at Victoria… The original Herzog antedated even Katharinenstadt, having been founded in 1764"; Laing, *German-Russian Settlements in Ellis County*: "Herzog (Susly, founded 1764)").
- The Ellis County settlers came from **nineteen Russian villages**, "and gave their new communities the names of the localities from which they came." That is the whole naming law of the county: Catherine from Katharinenstadt, Herzog from Herzog, Munjor from Obermunjour, and so on. — VERIFIED (Sackett).

## The promises withdrawn (1871–1874)

- In **1871** Czar Alexander II's edict ended the colonists' special status — in Hoffman's words, "The year 1871 terminated the one-hundred year Manifest granted by Catherine the Great" — and the **military law of January 1874** made the colonists liable to the conscription Catherine had promised they would never face. — VERIFIED (Hoffman thesis for 1871; Laing for "the military law of January [1874] intro[ducing colonists] to military service"; FHSU Ellis County guide: "In 1874, Russia passed the military law, which required participation from all the colonists").
- In **1874 the colonists met at Herzog (Russia)** to weigh emigration and chose delegates to scout America — five delegates, by the Fort Hays guide's account. After men from Herzog and Katharinenstadt were drafted in late November and December 1874, "the emigration to the United States began." — VERIFIED (FHSU guide: "The colonists met in Herzog in the spring of 1874 for the purpose of electing 5 delegates to visit America… after four men from Herzog and twenty-one men from Katharinenstadt were drafted in late November and early December 1874, the emigration to the United States began"; Hoffman corroborates the 1874 Herzog meeting: "In 1874, 3000 colonists met at Herzog (Russia) to discuss the question of immigration").

## The crossing and the founding (1875–1876)

- The founders of Herzog, Kansas, named in full by the 1926 Golden Jubilee book — the Dreilings, Kreutzers, Romes, Billinger, Hammerschmidts, and their kin, twenty-three heads of families — "left Saratov, Russia, in company with numerous other emigrants, on **October 24, 1875**, and arrived in Baltimore, Md., on **November 23**." — VERIFIED (*The Golden Jubilee of German-Russian Settlements*, Herzog chapter).
- They wintered in Topeka, as did the Katharinenstadt party (which reached Hays on 1 March 1876), and in the spring of 1876 planted their villages: Liebenthal first, then Catherine, then **Herzog, founded within a month after Catherine, a half-mile north of the English town of Victoria**. — VERIFIED (Sackett: "the third community… was founded within a month after Catherine; this was Herzog… established half a mile north of the English settlement at Victoria"; FHSU guide for the Topeka wintering and the Hays arrival date; Hoffman for the five original 1876–77 settlements: "Catherine, Munjor, Herzog (Victoria), Pfeifer, and Schoenchen").

## Victoria — the other name

- The ground Herzog stood beside was already a town with a different story: **Victoria, founded in 1873**, the colony of the Scotsman **George Grant**, who brought English and Scottish settlers to farm and ranch the prairie, naming the place for **Queen Victoria**. — VERIFIED (SAH Archipedia: "The town of Victoria was founded in 1873 by [a] group of English settlers"; Fort Hays State University Center for Ethnic Studies guide: "the British colonists associated with the settlement efforts of the Scotsman George Grant"; the naming for the Queen is the town's own constant tradition, carried by both). 
- The two towns' fates crossed: "As time passed the English failed and left, while the German-Russians prospered and grew; finally the two towns grew together, and **the name Victoria, established by the railroad, is given to the entire community**, which is now wholly Volga German." — VERIFIED (Sackett, quoted closely). Herzog did not vanish; it kept its name in the parish's mouth — the 1926 jubilee book heads its chapter "HERZOG (Victoria)," and the church the friars serve stands on Herzog ground at the north edge of town.

## What they carried

- They carried two things across two oceans and a century: their **Catholic faith** and their **steppe agriculture**. Before any church stood, Mass was said in Alois Dreiling's house (April 1876; see RETURN-CAP-2); and "it was the Volga Germans who **established winter wheat rather than corn** as the primary grain crop on the harsh Kansas prairie in Ellis County." — VERIFIED (National Catholic Register for the house Mass; FHSU Center for Ethnic Studies, *A Guide to the Collections*, introduction, for the wheat).
- Placed in one line: the Basilica of St. Fidelis is what a promise broken in St. Petersburg built in Kansas — a people who crossed the world twice to keep their faith and their freedom from the czar's army, and who, two generations on, raised the largest church west of the Mississippi out of the limestone under their own fields and put a Capuchin martyr's name on it, served by friars who had themselves been expelled from Germany for their order's existence. The two refugee stories — the farmers' and the friars' — met at Herzog in May 1878 and have been one story since.
