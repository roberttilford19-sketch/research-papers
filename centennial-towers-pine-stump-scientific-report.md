# The Centennial Towers Pine: A Scientific Report on Its Stump

**Robert Tilford, Hays, Kansas — October 26, 2026**

## Summary

A pine that stood beside Centennial Towers in Hays, Kansas, was felled in October 2026; this report is the record of its stump. The author photographed the stump on 25–26 October 2026 in three sessions and thirty frames, the last two carrying a twelve-inch ruler on the face. Measured by a deliberately restricted same-axis photographic method, the face is approximately 27 inches through and 33.5 inches across the tips of its widest flutes — photographic measurements, not tape measurements on the wood. The trunk grew fluted rather than round, with broad cream sapwood around reddish-brown heartwood, and rings that run tight near the bark against broader banding deeper in the face. No complete ring count exists in the photographs, so no age and no planting year is offered, and partial ring readings are labelled candidates throughout. The tree was a hard pine, in all probability an Austrian pine (*Pinus nigra*); the needle-bundle observation that would settle the species remains outstanding. What the stump records is the outline of a life — broad growth in the middle years, slow growth at the end — and it closes with the protocol by which a saved cross-section could still supply the rest.

## Briefing, in plain words

This is the record of one pine tree that grew beside the author's building in Hays, Kansas, and of what could honestly be learned from its stump after the tree was cut down.

The author photographed the stump over two days, in daylight and again at night, and measured its face from the photographs: about 27 inches through in one direction, and about 33½ inches across the widest of its ribs. The tree was most likely an Austrian pine, the kind planted all over Kansas towns for shade and shelter.

Its rings show that it grew faster in its middle years and slowly near the end. The photographs cannot say how old it was, and this paper does not pretend otherwise. It separates what was measured from what was only estimated, it shows its working, and it ends by saying what could still be learned if a slice of the wood is saved before the stump is ground away.

## 1. Introduction and site context

The subject of this report is one stump. It stands beside Centennial Towers, a senior residence in Hays, Kansas, in the ground where a row of landscape pines was planted along the building.

Imagery of the site from July 2024 (Google Street View, consulted at this desk) shows the tree in life as a straight, dense, dark-green pine standing in that planted row. By the morning of October 25, 2026, the tree had been felled and the trunk removed, leaving the stump cut low, ringed by the blue utility flags that mark underground services, and awaiting the grinder in the ordinary course of grounds work.

The author, who lives in the building and walks past the site daily, photographed the stump first on that morning. He returned to it after dark the same night, because rain had wet the face and wet wood shows its rings, and closed the record in the small hours of October 26 with two frames carrying a ruler, taken for the single purpose of giving the face a scale.

The stump belongs, in the author's larger work, to his book of trees, and this report is written so that the tree's chapter in that book can rest on measured ground rather than on impression.

The site matters to the science, and it is stated here rather than assumed. Hays sits on the High Plains of west-central Kansas, in a climate of hot summers, cold winters, strong prevailing southwesterly winds, and rainfall that arrives unevenly and sometimes fails for years at a time.

A landscape tree at an apartment complex lives a particular life inside that climate: its roots work a restricted and often compacted volume of soil among pavement and foundations, and it may or may not have been watered in dry years at the discretion of groundskeepers whose names and habits are not recorded. Both facts — the hard root zone and the unknown watering history — will return in the discussion, because both bear on what the rings of such a tree can and cannot be made to say.

## 2. Materials and methods

The evidence base is photographic throughout, and its limits are the limits of photography; no instrument touched the wood at any point in this study, and no cutting of the stump was done or attempted.

The campaign ran in three sessions. On the morning of October 25, in daylight, the author took nine frames of the stump and its setting, among them a whole-face shot with his wooden twelve-inch ruler and his boot in frame for scale, and two ruler close-ups of the face's sectors.

On the night of October 25, beginning at about 23:53, he returned with the face wet from rain and took nineteen further frames in two batches: two whole-top record shots taken with the telephone held directly overhead, quadrant close-ups at varied rotations, and wider takes of the stump in its setting.

At about 00:20 on October 26 he took the two closing frames, each showing the whole fluted outline with the twelve-inch ruler laid on the face and a coin beside it.

Thirty frames were taken in all. The twenty-one frames of the night operation are filed together with an index at this desk, and the morning frames and their working crops are filed with the desk's earlier comparative report on the same stump.

The camera was the author's Samsung Galaxy A17, used on its main 50-megapixel lens with optical image stabilisation. The record frames were made in the telephone's Night mode, which stacks a burst of exposures into one frame; the flash was off throughout, because a flash mounted beside the lens lights a surface flat and erases the fine relief on which ring-reading depends.

In their place the face was lit by raking light: the author's Coast G19 penlight, laid low and to the side so that its beam crossed the face at a shallow angle and threw every latewood ridge into small relief. For the two whole-top record shots the light was set down, aimed across the top, and held still for the duration of each stacked exposure; for the walk out and the quadrant work it rode clipped to the brim of the author's cap.

Raking light is an old reader's trick for worn inscriptions and faint grain, and it is the reason the night frames, and not the daylight ones, carry the sharpest ring evidence in this study — with the offsetting cost, recorded here, that a raking beam also blooms and glares on a wet face, and several frames of the night batches are atmosphere rather than evidence.

Measurements were taken from the two closing ruler frames by a deliberately restricted method, and the restriction is part of the result. Within each frame, points were located on the ruler and on the face, and distances on the face were converted to inches using the ruler in that same frame as the scale — but only along the axis on which the ruler itself lay.

A camera held even slightly off true overhead foreshortens one direction of its subject more than the other, so a length measured across the ruler's axis cannot lawfully be converted using the ruler's scale. This is the ordinary projective geometry of single-image measurement, in which scale is valid only in the plane and along the direction for which it was established [1]. The method therefore refuses every cross-axis computation, and one such figure, produced during the desk work and rejected for exactly this reason, is disclosed in the measurements section below rather than buried.

All figures so derived are stated with the approximation sign they have earned. They are photographic measurements, repeatable by anyone from the filed frames, and they are not a substitute for a tape laid on the wood.

The desk's ring work was likewise restricted and is reported as what it is. On the sharpest of the night close-ups, brightness transects — lines drawn down three columns of the image, counting the dark latewood bands the line crosses — were run across a partial sector of the heartwood, and the morning close-ups were read by eye for ring lines in the clearest sector near the bark and across the sapwood band on one radius.

Neither procedure is a ring count. A count requires a located pith, a prepared surface, and an unbroken radius followed from centre to bark [2, 3]; the photographs offer none of the three, and so the transect figures enter this report as candidates — indications of how much banding the wood contains, offered for comparison against the author's field estimate and against each other, and for nothing more.

## 3. Morphology

The face is not a circle, and almost everything distinctive about this stump follows from that.

The trunk grew fluted: its outline rises and falls in a series of rounded lobes and shallow bays, like the buttressed base of a tree that has spent its life gripping and bracing, and the two closing frames show the full outline plainly — an irregular, many-lobed figure, longer across its widest flutes than across the waist between them.

Fluting of this kind is a growth habit, not a defect; a stem lays down more wood where the mechanical and conducting demands on it are greatest, and over decades the round young trunk becomes a column of vertical ribs. It also means, as the measurements section states, that this stump has no single diameter, and any report that gives it one has already simplified the tree out of existence.

Within the outline, the face divides into the two great zones of a pine's wood. Around the outside runs a broad band of cream-coloured sapwood, the living conducting wood of the tree's last years, following the fluted outline in lobes of uneven thickness. Inside it lies a large heartwood of reddish brown, the dead, extractive-darkened core, occupying most of the face's area.

One caution about that boundary was entered in this desk's earlier work and is carried here because it governs how the face may be read: the line between sapwood and heartwood is a chemical transition, not a growth event, and in a fluted or stressed trunk it can wander irregularly across the rings [4].

The two zones are therefore described in this report as anatomy, and never used as evidence about the rate at which the tree grew; growth is read only where one year's latewood meets the next year's earlywood, in the rings themselves [4].

The rings, where the night frames show them, run in wavy bands through the heartwood, broadening and narrowing and nowhere forming the clean concentric ovals of a plantation stem. The waviness is the fluting expressed in time: a ring laid down on a ribbed stem is a ribbed ring, and its width varies around the face with the rib it crosses. Deep in the face the banding is visibly broader than at the margin.

Near the centre, the night frames show the pith region with a set of radial checks — the narrow cracks that open as a cut face dries and shrinks — running outward from near it, and the morning frames show the same centre torn and swirled, with no pith standing out cleanly enough to anchor a count.

Compression wood, the dense, darker reaction wood a conifer lays down on the loaded side of its stem against the prevailing wind [5], cannot be excluded anywhere on this face from photographs alone; that exclusion is work for a prepared section, and its absence here is one more reason the broad interior banding is described in this report as broad, and not interpreted further.

## 4. Measurements

The locked measurements of this study — locked in the plain sense that these are the figures the record supports and the figures any future work must start from — are two in number, and both come from the closing frames of October 26.

In frame 34701, with the wooden ruler lying vertically on the face, the face measures approximately 27 inches along the ruler's own axis. In frame 34700, with the ruler lying roughly horizontally, the face measures approximately 33.5 inches across the outermost tips of its widest flutes.

Read together, the two figures describe the fluted figure the morphology section sets out: a face some twenty-seven inches through in one direction, reaching thirty-three and a half inches tip to tip where the flutes stand widest, and narrower across the bays between them.

They also refine the estimate the morning frames had supported — twenty-four to thirty inches across the flutes — confirming its lower range, extending its upper, and replacing a range with two anchored axes.

All three statements are photographic measurements under the method of the previous section, repeatable from the filed frames, and each carries the method's limit with it: they measure the face as the camera saw it, along the axes the ruler legitimised, and along no others.

The rejected figure belongs in this section because the method that produced the good figures produced it too, and honesty about the one is the warranty of the other.

During the desk work, a computation carried the ruler's scale across the frame's axis — converting a length in the direction the camera's slight tilt had foreshortened, using a scale earned in the direction it had not [1] — and returned a face width of 42.9 inches. That figure is rejected, on the record, as an artefact of the tilt: it measures the geometry of the photograph, not the geometry of the stump, and it appears in this report only as this disclosure. No other use has been or will be made of it.

One further figure is recorded at candidate grade, because it is useful and because its grade is its honesty. In frame 34701 the ruler lies along a radius running below the pith, and the banded heartwood along that radius extends roughly eight inches below the centre before the sapwood begins.

That depth is candidate geometry — it rests on the position of the pith as estimated in a frame where the centre is checked and swirled — and it is recorded for one purpose: to tell any future worker where, on a saved cross-section, the long readable radius of this tree is most likely to be found.

## 5. Ring evidence

The ring evidence of this study is real, and it is small, and this section exists to keep those two facts in their proper relation.

From the morning close-ups, read by eye on the day they were taken, about six ring lines can be followed across roughly an inch and a quarter of wood in the clearest sector near the bark, and about seven ring lines cross the pale sapwood band on one radius.

Those two readings agree with each other, and together they put the tree's late growth at on the order of a fifth of an inch per year or less in its final years — tight growth, set against the visibly broader banding deeper in the face.

From the night frames, the desk's brightness transects down three columns of the sharpest close-up crossed twenty-three, eighteen, and twenty-one candidate latewood lines respectively over a partial sector of the heartwood.

These are candidates, not a count: a transect across a wavy-grained sector crosses lines that merge and split as the grain wanders, it begins and ends wherever the sharp sector begins and ends rather than at pith and bark, and three different columns of the same image returning three different numbers is itself the demonstration that the wood will not be counted this way.

The transects say that the heartwood sector they crossed is banded at roughly the density the rest of the evidence suggests, and they say nothing tighter.

The author estimated the tree's age in the field, standing at the stump, at forty to fifty years. The candidate evidence is consistent with that range — a face of this size, growing at the late rate the outer sectors show and faster in its middle years, is plausibly the product of four to five decades — and consistency is all that is claimed, because the photographs cannot carry the question further, for three reasons that no improvement in photography would remove.

First, there is no complete count: the pith is not cleanly located, no unbroken pith-to-bark radius is readable in any frame, and without a count there is no arithmetic that ends at an age. Dendrochronology's own standards make the same demand from the other side: dating rests on counted, crossdated rings, with false and missing rings watched for, not on lines tallied from an image [2, 3, 6].

Second, the count, if it existed, would still be a minimum: a stump is cut above the ground, and the rings at stump height begin only after the young tree had already spent its first years growing up to that height, so a stump count dates the wood at that height and understates the tree [2].

Third, the anchor at the near end is conditional rather than fixed: the outermost ring is the 2026 season's growth only if the tree's cambium was still active when it was felled in October 2026, and a tree felled because it had died standing — from drought, root failure, or the pine wilt that the Kansas Department of Agriculture lists Austrian pine among the hosts of [7, 8] — could carry a last ring of 2025, 2024, or earlier.

For the same reasons, no planting year is offered anywhere in this report.

An earlier illustrated overlay on one of the morning photographs, produced by an external image model, attached a planting year and dated wet and dry bands to this face; those claims were examined in the desk's comparative report of October 25, graded not supported — the drawn lines demonstrably followed the fluted outline rather than boundaries in the wood, and no count existed to date them by — and they are not re-entered here as evidence of anything except the difference between an illustration and a measurement.

## 6. Identification

The identification is presented as a ladder, because that is what the evidence builds: each rung is stated with the evidence that holds it and the gap that keeps it from being the rung above.

At the top of what is established stands the genus. The tree is a pine, *Pinus* — established not from the stump alone, which by itself could not exclude every other urban conifer of Hays, but from the convergence of the stump's wood and bark characters with the needle-bearing branch that hangs into the author's photograph of the building wall, the tree's habit in the 2024 imagery, and the planting record of the site.

Within the genus, the hard-pine group is strongly supported: the branch in the wall photograph carries needles estimated at roughly four to six inches long, measured against the brick courses behind them, which places the tree among the two- and three-needled hard pines of Kansas plantings and excludes the soft white pines, whose needles and habit do not match. Eastern redcedar, the one local conifer that might have been confused at stump level, is excluded on the stump's own wood and bark, which are not a juniper's.

The species rung is probable, and is stated at that grade in the same breath as the name: in all probability this was an Austrian pine, *Pinus nigra* [9, 10].

The name is carried there by the needle length — four to six inches, in fascicles of two, in the published character of the species [9] — by the dark, dense crown and straight habit of the tree in life, and by the planting record, in which Austrian pine was the workhorse landscape pine of Kansas institutional plantings of this tree's era [11], a standing documented in the Kansas State University Research and Extension literature (publication MF3423) [12] and reflected in the tree's presence on the Kansas Department of Agriculture's pine-wilt host listing [7].

The alternatives are recorded, not hidden: Scotch pine remains a live but weakened alternative — its needles run shorter, one and a half to three inches, twisted and blue-green, where this tree's ran long [13] — and ponderosa pine a remoter one.

The reason the name stops at probable is single and specific: the decisive character separating the hard pines is the needle bundle, the fascicle, and its count was not legible in the dusk light of the wall photograph, so no fascicle count is claimed anywhere in this report.

What would settle the species is any one of three observations: a daylight close-up of a single needle bundle from a surviving tree of the same row, with a coin in frame to give true needle length beside the count; a fallen cone from beneath the row, whose characters separate Austrian from Scotch on their own; or a cross-section of this tree's own wood, dried and sanded, read under a lens by a practiced eye.

The identification characters relied on throughout this section follow the Virginia Tech Dendrology fact sheets [9, 13], the Utah State University TreeBrowser, and the Morton Arboretum's published tree characters [14], each claim in the desk's underlying workup carrying its source.

## 7. Discussion

What this stump records, kept strictly inside the evidence, is the shape of one tree's life in one hard place.

A pine grew beside the building for what the candidate evidence allows to be read as several decades — plausibly the forty to fifty years of the author's field estimate, stated here as consistency and not as a finding — and it did not grow round or easy.

It fluted, laying its wood in ribs; it banded broadly in the deep face of its middle years and tightly at its margin at the end; and it finished its life as a face some twenty-seven inches through and thirty-three and a half across the flutes, which is a substantial tree for a strip of ground between a residence and its pavement.

The slowing at the end is the one dynamic fact the photographs actively support, and its causes can be listed but not apportioned: the tree aged, as trees do; its root zone was the restricted, compacted volume an urban planting lives in; and its final fifteen years, if it lived them on the schedule the felling date suggests, included the severe Kansas droughts of 2011–2012 and of the early 2020s, years the National Centers for Environmental Information and the U.S. Drought Monitor record among the worst in the state's measured history [15], with Ellis County under a declared drought emergency in the autumn of 2022 (Kansas Executive Order 22-08) [16].

Drought is therefore a reasonable suspect in the slowing. It is not a convicted one — the photographs cannot divide the credit among drought, age, and the root zone, and the suspect carries a further complication the record cannot resolve: a landscape tree that was watered through the dry years would not carry those years in its rings the way an unwatered tree does, and this tree's watering history is unknown.

The regional calendar is context for the life, and it is kept in this report exactly where it belongs, as context; no ring on this face has been matched to any year of it, and none will be until a counted ring-width table exists to match.

Two further readings are available in the wood and are recorded here at the grade they have earned.

The fluting itself is a biography of load: a stem ribs itself where it is worked hardest, and on the High Plains the standing load is wind, the southwesterlies that blow across Hays in every season; whether the deepest flutes and the broadest interior banding mark the windward bracing — compression wood rather than generous growth [5] — is a question the photographs can raise and cannot settle, and it is left open deliberately.

And the face itself, cut in October 2026, is the last page of the record either way: whether the tree came down because it had died, or because it had declined past keeping, or in the ordinary rotation of groundskeeping, the documents available to this desk do not say, and the conditional anchor on the final ring, set out in the ring section, is the report's way of keeping that silence honest.

## 8. Limitations

The limitations of this study are stated together here, though each has also been carried at the point of the claim it limits, as this house's practice requires.

The whole study is photographic: no measurement in it was taken on the wood, no surface was prepared, and photographic geometry — tilt, perspective, and the Night mode's stacking of exposures — bounds every figure in it.

The identification stops at probable for want of one legible needle bundle. The ring evidence consists of two small sector readings and three candidate transects over a partial sector; there is no ring count, no ring-width table, and therefore no age, no planting year, and no dated ring.

The terminal ring's year is conditional on the tree having been alive at felling, which the record does not establish. Compression wood has not been excluded anywhere on the face, so broad interior banding cannot be read as fast growth.

The tree's watering history and the reason for its felling are both unknown, and both bear on interpretations the report has accordingly declined to make.

The desk's transect work was a first pass, performed on the filed images without image-analysis software, and is reported at candidate grade for that reason also.

None of these limitations is a defect of effort; most of them are the ordinary distance between a photograph and a piece of wood, and the final section is about closing it.

## 9. Future work

The stump still stands, and the grinder has not yet come, so the first item of future work is perishable in the plainest sense: everything that depends on the face itself ends when the grinding does.

The road forward was set out in the desk's comparative report and is restated here as this report's conclusion of method. If the cutting or grinding crew produces a cross-section of the trunk or stump — a cookie — it should be asked for and kept, labelled in pencil on its edge with the date, the place, and which way was north.

Dried slowly out of direct sun so it does not crack across its rings, and sanded along a chosen radius to four hundred–six hundred grit, such a section would give the tree what no photograph can: a located pith, an unbroken radius, and ring widths measurable under an optical loupe on an etched reticle, written down from the bark inward as a ring-width table [2, 3] — the actual scientific product, of which every dated drought and every growth story is only commentary.

The count would come first and the weather second, in that order and never reversed, with false rings and wedging rings watched for [4, 6] and compression wood excluded along the measured radii [5] before any wide band is read as a wet year.

The Kansas State University Research and Extension office in Hays, in the Cottonwood Extension District serving Ellis County, is the natural place to carry such a cookie for an independent reading — a check by practiced eyes, not an oracle.

In parallel, and at no cost but a walk in daylight, the species question can be settled from the surviving row: one needle bundle photographed close with a coin in frame, or one fallen cone gathered from beneath the trees, either of which would let the probable name in this report be confirmed or corrected by dated addendum, in the way every correction in this house arrives.

Until one of those steps is taken, this report stands as the stump's record: its form described, its measures locked, its rings honestly unread, and its name carried as far as the evidence carries it and no further.
