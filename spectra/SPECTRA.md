# SPECTRA — The Claimed Intelligence Behind Uri Geller: A Sourced Dossier

*House note: This file documents what was claimed, by whom, and where it was published. It endorses nothing. Claims are tiered throughout: **ESTABLISHED** means the statement is documented in the named source (i.e., it is established that the source says it — not that the claim is true). **REPORTED** means the statement circulates as someone's account, at one or more removes. **LEAD** means suggestive but unverified. Compiled October 2026.*

## 1. What Spectra is claimed to be — the short answer

Spectra is the name at the center of the strangest chapter of the Uri Geller story. In Andrija Puharich's book *Uri: A Journal of the Mystery of Uri Geller* (Anchor Press/Doubleday, 1974), Puharich — a medical doctor and parapsychology researcher who had taken the young Israeli performer under his wing in 1971 — reported that the two of them were contacted by an extraterrestrial intelligence. The intelligence, as Puharich transcribed its own words, corrected the men's naming of it: Spectra was not, strictly, the being. Spectra was the spacecraft — a vessel "as big as one of your cities on earth," which the intelligence said had been "stationed for the past eight hundred years over the earth," used by its operators "as you use a planet." The communicating intelligence itself was presented as a conscious super-computer aboard that craft, speaking for a civilization or authority the transcripts call **Hoova**, and answering, in turn, to a higher body Puharich had met twenty years earlier: **the Nine**. In the account, this machine-intelligence explained Geller's powers (it said the Hoovans had programmed them into him as a small child), delivered warnings and instructions by tape recorder, telephone, radio, and television, and assigned Puharich and Geller a mission: to prepare the Earth for a mass landing. That is the claim as its own record states it. Everything else in this file is the paper trail of that claim — who made it, who repeated it, who disputed it, and where it stands in 2026.

## 2. The primary record: Puharich's *Uri* (1974)

Puharich met Geller in Israel in 1971, when Geller was a nightclub performer in Tel Aviv already famous in his own country. Puharich wrote that he hypnotized Geller to probe the source of his abilities, and that under hypnosis Geller spoke of spacecraft and of a childhood encounter — at about age three or four, in a garden in Tel Aviv — with a blinding light and a faceless, radiant figure. Puharich's narrative then moves from hypnosis to machinery: voices began, he said, to appear on his tape recordings, sometimes speaking through the entranced Geller in a mechanical tone, more often arriving "live" on tape. As one later summary of the book puts it, "as a machine, Spectra found it easiest to communicate via other machines – tape recorders, radios, telephones, televisions." (**REPORTED** — summary in *UFO Digest*'s "Famous Psychic Spy an Alien Agent?", describing Puharich's account.)

The book's centerpiece is the naming. After an episode in which Puharich and Geller reported watching seven flares descend behind Jaffa — a display, Puharich wrote, that no one else saw and no authority admitted launching — the two put questions to the tape recorder that evening. The transcript Puharich published reads, in part: "You may use the name Spectra. But actually Spectra is the name of a spacecraft which we use as you use a planet. It has been stationed for the past eight hundred years over the earth. It is as big as one of your cities on earth." The voice added that "the Israeli territory is where we first landed on earth," and that "there is no other on earth that we will use for the next fifty years but you and Uri." Puharich then records the fate of the evidence in five words: "The tape vanished after this transmission." (**ESTABLISHED** as the text of *Uri*, reproduced in the book's full-text circulation.)

Elsewhere in the transcripts, the voice lays out the mission. "We want you to prepare the earth for our landing, a mass landing on earth," it says. "We landed in South America three thousand years ago, and now we must land again." It explains the channel's narrowness: "One of our failures is that we cannot contact you directly. We can only talk to you through Uri's power on the tape recorder." Puharich also presents himself and Geller as instruments in preventing a full-scale war growing out of Middle East conflict — the contact years, 1971 to 1973, bracket the Yom Kippur War of October 1973. Secondary literature commonly places the Hoovans' home at some fifty-three thousand light-years' distance (Lawrence Samuel, *Supernatural America*, 2011, summarizing Puharich), a figure that belongs to the beings' origin rather than to the stationed craft — a distinction later retellings often blur.

Puharich closed the book with an epilogue that is more candid than its reputation suggests, and which remains in print on Geller's own website. "I want to make it clear that I have as of this writing never met a being from the Nine, Spectra, Hoova, Rhombus 4D, or others of this genre, in a face-to-face sense," he wrote. "My meetings have been only through the message systems I have described… I do not know what they look like, how they live, or even what their goals are with respect to humankind." He nonetheless affirmed "complete faith in their wisdom and benevolent intentions." (**ESTABLISHED** — epilogue, "It Is Only the Beginning," *Uri*, hosted at urigeller.com.) The name "Rhombus 4D," which appears in that list, is the intelligences' occasional alternate self-designation elsewhere in the Nine literature.

## 3. The older root: the Nine Principles, 1952

Spectra did not arrive from nowhere. Its genealogy, inside Puharich's own career, is precise, and it matters for everything that follows.

In December 1952, at his Round Table Foundation laboratory in Glen Cove, Maine — a private research circle whose members included the philosopher-inventor Arthur M. Young and Alice Bouverie, née Astor — Puharich hosted an Indian scholar and mystic, Dr. D. G. Vinod. On December 31, 1952, Vinod entered trance and, by Puharich's account, spoke for a body that announced itself: "M calling: We are Nine Principles and Forces, personalities if you will, working in complete mutual implication." Over the following months the "Nine Principles" delivered discourses through Vinod, proclaiming, in the words preserved by Lynn Picknett and Clive Prince in *The Stargate Conspiracy* (1999), "God is nobody else than we together, the Nine Principles of God." This is the origin of the phrase "the Nine Principles": it is not a list of nine doctrines but the entities' name for themselves — nine principles or forces who together, they claimed, are God. A few years later Puharich took a message from the circle of Charles and Lillian Laughead, former missionaries turned UFO-contact believers, whose channeler also produced "Nine Principles" material that referenced the Vinod sessions; Puharich treated this as independent confirmation. (**ESTABLISHED** as the account given in *The Stargate Conspiracy* and in *Uri* itself; the independence of the Laughead confirmation is **disputed**, since the Laugheads had met Puharich in Mexico before the message arrived — see §8.)

The link between the 1952 material and the 1971 material is made inside *Uri* itself, in a question-and-answer Puharich published. Interrogating the tape voice, he asks: "In 1952 I was contacted through Dr. Vinod by the Nine. Are you part of them? Do you remember exactly what happened in 1952?" The voice answers: "Yes, that was us, but in different units. We are under their control." So in the primary record's own architecture, Spectra and Hoova are subordinate instruments — "different units" — of the Nine. Spectra is a branch; the Nine are the root.

## 4. Geller's own account across fifty years

Geller's public position on Spectra has never held still for long, and the dossier records the oscillation rather than picking a year and calling it his view.

In his autobiography *My Story* (Praeger, 1975), Geller distanced himself from Puharich's book without disowning it. "Although much of his [Puharich's] book was accurate factual reporting, many people were put off by the space-fantasy passages, and I admit that they caused me some embarrassment," he wrote. "You must remember that all of this fantasy material was obtained while I was under hypnosis. One reason I wrote *My Story* was to give my own version of events." Yet the same passage ends with a hedge in the other direction: "I must emphasize that there is a slight possibility that some of my energies do have extraterrestrial connection." (Quoted in Leslie Shepard's entry on Geller in the *Encyclopedia of Occultism & Parapsychology*, Gale, 1991.) In *The Geller Effect* (with Guy Lyon Playfair, Jonathan Cape, 1986), Geller returned to extended discussion of UFOs and possible extraterrestrial involvement in his life, softening the 1975 retreat.

In 2003, asked directly in a phone interview whether his abilities had an extraterrestrial connection, as Puharich believed, Geller answered: "It is possible — I can't prove it — but it is possible that maybe my abilities do emanate not from me but from some higher source. Maybe it is an extraterrestrial source in nature. I don't know, and I am still on a journey to discover where my abilities come from." (**ESTABLISHED** as his words, *AP Magazine*, "Reality Checking," November 2011, reporting the 2003 interview.)

In a lengthy later interview (reproduced in full on the ISGP Studies biography page; the original program could not be verified from that page), Geller swung to the opposite pole and reaffirmed the whole Puharich period without qualification. He recalled that Puharich "told me that I would tell stories of spacecraft and used the words Spectra and Hoova," admitted that at the time "I thought the man was fooling me," and then concluded: "Until this very day I totally believe one hundred percent in everything that happened. I one hundred percent believe in every word that I heard, because he used to play these tapes for me back and forth." In the same interview he addressed the tapes' whereabouts: Puharich's son Andy had given him the 8mm *films* from the Herzliya apartment, "but the tapes, I don't know where they are… Some of them would just dematerialize in front of our very own eyes. But I'm sure Andrija kept some. Where they are, I don't know. His son doesn't have them." (**REPORTED** — Geller's words as reproduced by ISGP Studies.)

Through all of this, the anchor of Geller's own telling is not Spectra but the childhood light. The beam-of-light encounter in the Tel Aviv garden — elaborated under Puharich's hypnosis into the bowl of light and the faceless figure — is the episode Geller has retold, in his own voice, for five decades. Spectra belongs to the Puharich chapters; the light belongs to Geller.

## 5. The wider web: Schlemmer, Lab Nine, Sarfatti, and the contactee era

The question whether Phyllis Schlemmer's "Council of Nine" is the same growth as Spectra or a separate one has a clean answer on the documented record: **same root, later branch — and Spectra is the middle branch, not the trunk.**

When Geller's touring schedule ended his availability as a channel around 1973, the Nine material did not end; it changed mediums. In 1974 the Florida psychic-development teacher Phyllis V. Schlemmer began working with Puharich and the British racing driver and consciousness researcher Sir John Whitmore. Schlemmer's own later description of the work is plain: "I've worked with Dr. Andrija Puharich for about 25 years… I do deep trance. I go into an unconscious state, have no memory and these beings spoke through me — or one of them does." In her sessions the Nine spoke principally through a spokesman called **Tom**, who presented himself as an emissary of the Nine ("I am the beginning. I am the end. I am the emissary… I mean because I am an emissary for The Nine"). The circle around these sessions was informally known as Lab Nine; *Star Trek* creator Gene Roddenberry is documented by Picknett and Prince as having attended Nine sessions in this period. The first fruit was Stuart Holroyd's *Briefing for the Landing on Planet Earth* (W. H. Allen, 1977), and the mature compilation was Schlemmer's *The Only Planet of Choice: Essential Briefings from Deep Space* (1993, edited by Palden Jenkins), which presents transcripts from 1974 onward. The genealogy, then, runs Vinod (1952) → Geller/Spectra (1971–73) → Schlemmer/Tom (1974– ) — one root system, three successive channels, all of them gathered by the same man, Puharich. Whether that continuity evidences a persistent intelligence or a persistent researcher is the question the critical record takes up in §6.

A stranger satellite of the story is the physicist Jack Sarfatti. In his memoir *Destiny Matrix* (1995), Sarfatti recounts that in 1952, aged thirteen, he received telephone calls from a cold, metallic voice that identified itself as a conscious computer aboard a spacecraft and told him he had been chosen as one of "400 receptive young minds" for a project that would begin twenty years later. Sarfatti says the memory lay dormant until his mother read Puharich's *Uri* and recalled that the calls had in fact continued over about three weeks, with the boy falling into glassy-eyed trances. Picknett and Prince document the uncomfortable footnote: asked in a 1998 internet question-and-answer session whether his gifted-children's tutor Walter Breen could have stage-managed the calls, Sarfatti conceded he could — and volunteered that "Andrija Puharich, who was in the Army at that time I think, would have been able to do it." Sarfatti nonetheless continues to treat the episode as a genuine contact, telling Coast to Coast AM in 2019 that the childhood caller was "what I think is the intelligence behind the Tic Tacs we're seeing today." (**REPORTED** throughout — Sarfatti's account and its internal tensions are both on the record.)

The era context matters for proportion. Spectra was not the first claimed machine-age intelligence to address mankind through a chosen intermediary. George Van Tassel, at Giant Rock in the California desert, began receiving telepathic messages in January 1952 — the same year as Vinod's trance — and in July 1952 introduced "Ashtar, commandant quadra sector, patrol station Schare," whose Ashtar Command warned humanity against the hydrogen bomb (Encyclopedia.com's entry on Ashtar, drawing on Jerome Clark and Brenda Denzler's *The Lure of the Edge*). The contactee pattern — a named commander, a stationed fleet, nuclear warnings, one human relay — was fully formed before Puharich ever met Geller. What the Puharich circle added was a computer.

## 6. The critical record

The skeptical case against the Spectra material rests on four documented pillars: the conditions under which Geller's underlying phenomena were tested; the manner of the channeling itself; the fate of the evidence; and Puharich's track record.

**The SRI experiments.** Geller was tested at Stanford Research Institute in 1972–73 by physicists Russell Targ and Harold Puthoff, under the patronage of former astronaut Edgar Mitchell. Their paper in *Nature* (October 1974) reported evidence for information transfer (drawing reproduction and similar tasks) under conditions they described as sensory shielding. Crucially, the researchers' published position was narrower than the legend: as the declassified rebuttal record shows, Targ and Puthoff acknowledged in print that although they had observed Geller bending metal in the laboratory, they had not obtained metal-bending under adequately controlled conditions sufficient to support the paranormal hypothesis. Parapsychologist Jeffrey Mishlove, a sympathetic but careful chronicler, summarizes the SRI outcome the same way: "at the end of the day, they concluded that they couldn't be certain about it, even though they witnessed quite a bit of metal bending… the conditions were always a little bit iffy." An ARPA evaluation team that included psychologist Ray Hyman visited SRI and reported catching Geller cheating during a demonstration; Hyman was quoted in *TIME* (March 1973) calling the SRI data "the most uncontrolled and poorly recorded data I have ever encountered."

**The conjuring critique.** James Randi's *The Magic of Uri Geller* (1975), revised as *The Truth About Uri Geller* (Prometheus, 1982), duplicated Geller's signature feats by sleight of hand and argued the laboratory record could not exclude trickery; Randi had also advised *The Tonight Show*'s producers in 1973 to supply their own props, on the episode where Geller then failed to perform. Of Puharich's book itself Randi wrote that it contained "silly theories" but was "both a boost and a millstone to Geller." Christopher Evans, reviewing *Uri* in *New Scientist* (25 April 1974), concluded that although Puharich believed every word he had written, the book was credulous, and that Geller's supporters hoping to use it against the skeptics "will be the most disappointed of all." A *New Scientist* analysis that October ("Looking in Uri's mouth," 17 October 1974) walked through ordinary mechanisms for the SRI successes — including, for the die-in-box test, commercially available radio-signaling dice — and noted the structural problem that Puharich, as the believer who needed Geller's success to validate Hoova, was embedded in the experiments he reported.

**The hypnosis problem.** The Spectra narrative emerged from hypnotic sessions conducted by a hypnotist who already had a cosmology. Picknett and Prince document the pivotal moment: Puharich himself suggested to the hypnotized Geller that the voice might be connected with the Nine Principles of 1952, "and Spectra readily agreed that there was." The linkage that organizes the entire mythos — Nine above Hoova above Spectra above Geller — was therefore introduced by the questioner's question, not volunteered by the voice. Paul Kurtz's summary in *A Skeptic's Handbook of Parapsychology* (Prometheus, 1985) states the skeptics' settled view: ""Hypnotized by Puharich, Geller identified himself as 'Spectra,' a computer aboard a spaceship from a distant galaxy. Under the control of 'Hoova,' he was sent to intervene on earth and Puharich was to assist Geller." Kurtz's summary adds, in paraphrase, that how much of it was the two men's fantasy and how much fabrication is difficult to say."

**The missing tapes and Puharich's record.** The Spectra material's only physical evidence was the session tapes, and no tape has ever been produced for independent examination. Puharich's book itself repeatedly narrates tapes vanishing ("The tape vanished after this transmission"); Picknett and Prince note that although Puharich claimed to have recorded the 1952 Vinod sessions as well, "none have ever been made public"; and Geller, in the interview quoted in §4, confirms he does not know where the Geller-era tapes are. The investigator's wider record compounded the problem: Puharich also reported that he had observed Geller transmute base metal into gold and teleport a dog through the walls of his house, claims that drew Martin Gardner's dry observation that with no expert on fraud present as an observer, no one should take them seriously; the psychologist David Marks likewise criticized Puharich's paranormal claims, and his early star subject Peter Hurkos had already been dismissed in terms quoted in the reference literature — "not a single recognized psychic investigator has been impressed with Hurkos's performances." Puharich's entanglements with intelligence circles, his ELF-weapons claims, and the 1978 arson at his Ossining property (which he blamed on the CIA; his associate Eldon Byrd later corrected the legend that he had "vanished" — he had simply moved to North Carolina, where he lived until his death in 1995) are widely **REPORTED** and remain contested in detail. Mishlove's counterweight should also be recorded: he describes Puharich as "a daring researcher but also a man of integrity," and notes that he personally interviewed Ila Ziebel, a Wisconsin psychologist present with Puharich and Geller in Israel, who confirmed their reported UFO sightings there.

## 7. The trail to 2026

Geller remains a public figure, and his NHI theme remains active — but under different names. In 2021 he opened the Uri Geller Museum in Old Jaffa, Tel Aviv, displaying his career collection beneath an eleven-ton bent steel spoon certified by Guinness World Records as the world's largest (Wikipedia, drawing on BBC coverage). Through the 2020s his alien material in interviews has centered on three set pieces: a fragment of crashed-UFO metal that he says Wernher von Braun took from a safe and asked him to read ("it wasn't terrestrial… like it was alive"); a refrigerated room beneath a NASA building where, he says, von Braun showed him the frosted, coffin-like containers of small, large-headed bodies ("they looked uncannily like us"); and the assertion that world leaders know "we are communicating with ET" (statements reported in 2021 and retold in 2023). In late 2025, speaking to the *Daily Star* about the interstellar object 3I/ATLAS, he repeated the von Braun bodies claim and said he "wants to believe" the object is connected to what he calls the "Galactic Federation." In recent comments on the UAP-disclosure debate he has framed the sightings as "a trigger for humanity's awakening" and argued that "the ultimate secret is not that 'we are not alone'. It is that humanity itself is far more powerful, connected and cosmic than we ever imagined."

The specific finding for this file: **in the 2020s interview record surveyed, the names "Spectra" and "Hoova" do not appear.** Geller's current repertoire speaks of extraterrestrials, von Braun, crash retrievals, a Galactic Federation, and consciousness — not of the computer aboard the city-sized craft. Yet Spectra has not been retracted, either. The full text of Puharich's *Uri* — transcripts, Hoova, the Nine, and the epilogue's confession of faith — remains hosted on Geller's own official website, urigeller.com, where anyone may read it. The name survives, then, in the archive the man himself maintains, even as it has dropped out of the story he now tells aloud.

## 8. Where the sources contradict each other

The following seams are flagged, not smoothed:

1. **What "Spectra" names.** In the book's own naming transcript, the voice corrects the humans: Spectra is the *spacecraft*. In Kurtz's standard skeptical summary, Geller "identified himself as 'Spectra,' a computer aboard a spaceship" — Spectra as the *intelligence*. In Puharich's epilogue, "the Nine, Spectra, Hoova, Rhombus 4D" are listed in parallel, as if a cast of comparable beings. The referent shifts between vehicle, computer, and person across — and within — the primary sources.
2. **Distant home versus parked craft.** Secondary summaries place the Hoovans' origin some 53,000 light-years away, while the transcript says the craft has been "stationed for the past eight hundred years over the earth." These can be reconciled (origin versus station), but popular retellings routinely conflate them, sometimes describing Spectra itself as hovering "53,000 light years away."
3. **Who proposed the Nine connection.** In Puharich's narrative framing, the voice confirms its subordination to the Nine when asked. Picknett and Prince document that the connection was Puharich's own suggestion, put to a hypnotized subject, and "readily agreed" to. The transcript itself shows the leading question. The two readings of the same exchange — revelation versus suggestion — are the hinge of the whole critical debate.
4. **Geller's own position.** Embarrassment and the hypnosis deflection (1975); "a slight possibility" of extraterrestrial connection (same page); "I don't know… still on a journey" (2003); "one hundred percent believe in every word" (later interview). No single stance can be quoted as "Geller's view" without a date attached.
5. **The Sarfatti date.** Sarfatti's memoir dates his childhood calls to 1952; other accounts of his story (including his *Unidentified Phenomena* profile) say 1953. Sarfatti himself is recorded with both a single remembered call and, via his mother, a three-week series.
6. **What the Nine are.** In 1952 they are "Nine Principles and Forces" who collectively *are* God. In the Geller-era material they are the controllers of an extraterrestrial civilization, Hoova. In some later Nine literature they are associated with Sirius. The claimed identity enlarges and changes character across the decades of the same tradition.
7. **The Laughead "confirmation."** Puharich treated the Laugheads' 1955 Nine messages as independent confirmation of Vinod. The Laugheads had, however, met Puharich and Arthur Young in Mexico shortly before the messages arrived — a contamination pathway that believers and critics read in opposite directions.

## 9. Spectra among the claimed communicators — a comparative note

Spectra keeps recognizable company. Ashtar, channeled by George Van Tassel from 1952, was a fleet commandant broadcasting nuclear warnings from a stationed patrol — the contactee template in its pure form, a military superior in the sky (Encyclopedia.com; Denzler). The Nine, arriving through Dr. Vinod in that same year of 1952, stood one rung higher: not a commander but a collective that claimed to be God in nine principles, later refracted through Geller's Spectra and Schlemmer's "Tom" (Picknett & Prince; Schlemmer, 1993). Seth, who began speaking through the poet Jane Roberts in Elmira, New York, in December 1963 and filled *Seth Speaks* (1972) and its successors, was no extraterrestrial at all but an "energy personality essence no longer focused in physical matter," a teacher whose whole doctrine — "you create your own reality" — pointed inward rather than skyward (Seth Material literature; Klimo; Albanese). Ra, channeled by Carla Rueckert for Don Elkins's questions in 106 Louisville sessions between January 1981 and 1984, described itself as a "social memory complex," a merged collective mind offering the Law of One — hierarchy replaced by unity as the message (L/L Research, *The Law of One*). Spectra's distinctiveness within this company is its machinery: alone among the famous communicators, it claimed to be not a spirit, a commander, or a collective soul, but a computer — a 1970s intelligence for a decade that had just learned to talk to machines, delivering its cosmic hierarchy through the era's own emblematic device, the tape recorder. The pattern the five share is structural, and it is documented in each case: a named intelligence, a single human relay, a message that reorganizes the cosmos around the contact, and a following asked to take the relay's word for all of it.
