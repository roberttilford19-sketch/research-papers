# Christian Heinrich Pander — *Beiträge zur Entwickelungsgeschichte des Hühnchens im Eie* (1817)

*The Boneyard is a collection of works left unfinished, unpublished, or unread at their authors' deaths — dry bones in the valley, in Ezekiel's image. Each presentation tells the scholar's story, weighs what survives of his work, and states plainly what completing it would take, so that a book the world never received whole can be met at last. Each of them was one of one. There will never be another like him, and his light will not shine again except through the work he left behind.*

## Dedication

To Christian Heinrich Pander (1794–1865), considered by many to be the founder of embryology, whose name fewer readers know than his discovery deserves. In the chick in the egg he named the blastoderm and distinguished the serous, vascular and mucous layers, the germ layers from which the body arises. Karl Ernst von Baer then carried Pander's concept further, to all vertebrates, and fame followed the man who continued the work. Pander himself afterwards abandoned the inquiry for palaeontology. This edition is dedicated to the man who began it, with his plates beside his words and his name restored to the front of his own book.

## The author's own terms

Pander named the blastoderm: the layer of cells from which the embryo develops, within which he distinguished the serous, vascular and mucous layers.

## The story

Christian Heinrich Pander (1794–1865) found the germ layers, and Karl Ernst von Baer got the fame. That contrast is at the heart of this small book, stated in the record in a single sentence.

The germ layers are the early layers of cells in a developing embryo, from which the different parts of the body arise. To distinguish them, and to show that they matter, was a founding step in embryology, the study of how a living thing develops before birth. Pander is "considered by many to be the founder of embryology." Yet his name is the one fewer readers know.

His work was "continued by Karl Ernst von Baer, who expanded Pander's concept of germ layers to include all vertebrates," after Pander had "afterwards abandoned the inquiry" for palaeontology, the study of ancient life through fossils. So the sequence is honest and unsparing. Pander made the discovery in the chick, von Baer carried the idea further and more widely, and Pander himself turned to another field. Credit followed the man who continued the work, not only the man who began it.

The book itself appeared at Würzburg in 1817. Its title, *Beiträge zur Entwickelungsgeschichte des Hühnchens im Eie*, means contributions to the developmental history of the chick in the egg. In it, Pander named the blastoderm — the layer of cells from which the embryo develops — and distinguished the serous, vascular, and mucous layers. Those three layers are the substance of the discovery, set out with pictures as well as words.

## The work and what survives

Two forms of the work survive from 1817. One is the Latin dissertation *Historia metamorphoseos quam ovum incubatum prioribus quinque diebus subit*, published at Würzburg. The other is the German *Beiträge*, also published at Würzburg in 1817, with plates by Eduard d'Alton.

The plates are part of the work, not decoration added to it. The first German edition is collated as 10 engraved plates, together with 9 outline key plates and 1 section plate. The outline keys help a reader understand what the engraved plates show, so text and images belong together.

A copy is available through the Max Planck Institute ECHO digital library, cited in Embryo Project bibliographies. A Biodiversity Heritage Library plate page, 48408248, is also referenced. The work dates from 1817, and Pander died in 1865, so it is in the public domain.

## Its English fortunes

No English edition found after research. English sources cite it in German, with a bracketed title translation only.

That means an English reader can learn about Pander, and can be told what his book argues, but cannot read the book itself in English in an edition found by this research. For so small and so early a work, that is a striking absence.

## What completing it would take

This is the smallest new candidate among the life-science works considered here. The text is short, but its value lies in the text and d'Alton's plates together, so a completion should present both, not the words alone.

It is a natural companion to Wolff's work and to Vol. I of von Baer's work. Read together, those works show embryology being founded step by step: an idea proposed, a set of layers distinguished in the chick, and then a wider account of development. Pander's part of that sequence deserves to be readable in English on its own terms, with his name on it and his plates beside his words.
