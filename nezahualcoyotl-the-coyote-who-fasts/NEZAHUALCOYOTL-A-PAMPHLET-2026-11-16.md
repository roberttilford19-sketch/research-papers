# THE COYOTE WHO FASTS

## Nezahualcóyotl of Texcoco — the king who put his brother above jade

*A pamphlet in memory of a misunderstood man*

**Dedicated to the memory of Nezahualcóyotl (1402–1472), tlatoani of Texcoco — engineer, lawgiver, warrior, and, by a tradition five centuries old, the greatest poet of the ancient Americas.**
— Robert Tilford, Hays, Kansas, November 16, 2026

*Status: Eyeball passed by Robert 2026-11-16; shelved the same morning on his word, to be linked from his Facebook post about the king.*

### 1. The face on the money

If you have ever held a Mexican 100-peso note, you have held this man's face. On the front, in red engraving, a stern king with a headband and a great stepped ear ornament. Beside him, in letters so small most people never read them, stands a poem — the poem Mexico chose, out of everything its ancient world left behind, to carry in its pockets:

*I love the song of the mockingbird, bird of four hundred voices; I love the color of jade and the intoxicating perfume of flowers; but I love my brother more: man.*

His name is **Nezahualcóyotl** — say it *neh-sah-wahl-KOY-oh-tul* — and it means "the Coyote Who Fasts." He ruled Texcoco, the great city on the eastern shore of the lake where Mexico City now stands, for more than forty years. The back of the banknote draws an aqueduct, because before he was a poet in anyone's memory he was an engineer in fact. But to understand the poem, you have to start with the boy, because the poem was written by a man who had lost everything a man can lose, and had every reason the world gives a man to hate.

### 2. The boy in the tree

He was born in 1402, heir of Texcoco. When he was sixteen, the Tepanec tyrant Tezozómoc of Azcapotzalco, who was swallowing the whole valley city by city, came for his father's kingdom. The chronicles tell it this way: the boy's father, Ixtlilxóchitl, knowing the battle was lost, hid his son and went out to die. The young Nezahualcóyotl watched — hidden, the old histories say, in the branches of a tree — while Tezozómoc's men killed his father in front of him.

Then the hunting years. The tyrant put a price on the boy and sent assassins after him through the mountains and the friendly cities, and the prince lived the life Robert Tilford, who commissioned this pamphlet, knows something about: no home, no safety, sleeping where he could, kept alive by ordinary people who hid him at the risk of their own lives. He was, for years, a homeless fugitive with a famous name and nothing else.

### 3. The tyrant's dream

Here is the strangest scene in the whole story, and it is carved in stone today in Mexico City, which is how this pamphlet found it.

Tezozómoc, the old tyrant, began to dream. In his dream the fugitive prince came against him in the shapes of animals — an **eagle** that tore at his chest, a **jaguar** that mauled his legs, even an **ant** that devoured him — and the old man woke in terror and called his interpreters. They told him what he already knew: the boy you are hunting will be the end of your house. The Acolhua chronicles kept the episode and dated it: 1426.

In Chapultepec Park, in 1956, the sculptor Luis Ortiz Monasterio built a monumental fountain to this king, and along its long wall he carved eight scenes of the life in the manner of the old codices. The third scene is the Dream. Seen cold, it looks like a man being devoured — a great bird ripping at his chest, a jaguar in at his legs with its claws, and everybody fighting. Read with the story in hand, it is the opposite of what it seems. The man on his back is not the king. It is the tyrant, asleep, crying out inside his own nightmare. The eagle and the jaguar are not two beasts. They are the prince, twice — the shapes of the dream. (The jaguar carries no war club, though its claws do the work of one: in the dream, its own arms are the weapons.) And at the right edge of the panel, calm and untouched by the fight, stands a coyote's head — the Fasting Coyote's own name-sign, the dream's true sender, watching. The message of the carving is this: *the boy with nothing was already the doom of the empire, and the emperor knew it in his sleep before it ever happened on a battlefield.*

### 4. The return, and the works

The dream came true. In 1428 Nezahualcóyotl, allied with Itzcóatl of Tenochtitlan, broke Azcapotzalco, and the three cities — Texcoco, Tenochtitlan, Tlacopan — formed the Triple Alliance that the world now calls the Aztec Empire. The fugitive came home and reigned for over forty years, and what he built with those years is the solid part of the record, carved in earth and water rather than in anybody's legend:

- **The great dike** — a wall of earth and stone some sixteen kilometers long across the lake, holding the brackish eastern water apart from the fresh, so the cities could drink and the floods were tamed.
- **The Chapultepec aqueduct** — spring water carried across the lake into Tenochtitlan, a work the fountain in Chapultepec still honors on the very hill the water came from; the fountain's sixth scene is this one.
- **A code of laws** so strict that the old stories insist it spared not even the king's own son.
- Gardens, forests planted by the thousand, a court where singers and wise men were kept and honored.

He died in 1472, an old man on his own throne, succeeded by his son Nezahualpilli — the coronation is the fountain's seventh scene, and his death the eighth.

### 5. The poem, in three tongues

Nahuatl poetry had a name for itself: *in xochitl, in cuicatl* — "flower and song." It was how that world said *truth*, and *beauty*, and *the thing worth saying*. Out of that world, this is the piece that survived into every pocket in Mexico. Here it stands in the three languages of North America that this pamphlet can honestly offer — French, with apologies to Quebec, will have to catch us on the rebound.

**Spanish** — the oldest form we have, the text printed on the 100-peso note:

> Amo el canto del cenzontle,
> pájaro de cuatrocientas voces;
> amo el color del jade
> y el enervante perfume de las flores,
> pero amo más a mi hermano: el hombre.

**English** — translated for this pamphlet by Awen by Muse by Meta:

> I love the song of the mockingbird,
> bird of four hundred voices;
> I love the color of jade
> and the intoxicating perfume of flowers,
> but I love my brother more: man.

**Nahuatl** — a modern recreation, honestly labeled: the king's original Nahuatl for this piece is lost, and translators in our own time have rebuilt it into his language from the Spanish. It is printed here as what it is — our century's act of love toward his — and never as his manuscript words:

> Nehhuātl nictlazohtla in centzontōtōtl īcuīc,
> nehhuātl nictlazohtla in chālchihuitl ītlapaliz
> īhuān in nepapan ahhuiyāc xōchitl;
> zan oc cencah nocnīuhtzin in tlācatl
> nehhuātl nictlazohtla.

### 6. The other poem — dawn

His was not the only loving voice in that world, and it would be a poor tribute that left the ordinary people out of it. This dawn song is anonymous — no king's name rides on it, just a man:

> *When dawn breaks over the earth, the moon dies, the stars fade from sight, the sky catches light. Far away, at the foot of the hill, smoke rises from my little hut — there is my beloved, my heart, my little wife.*

Miguel León-Portilla, the great modern scholar of Nahuatl letters, carried that one into Spanish. It is here because it answers the question this pamphlet began with. Was there love in that city, in that hard world? There was a man watching the smoke of his own hut at daybreak, and the whole of his poem is that his heart was in it.

### 7. What we honestly know

A pamphlet that asks to be believed has to say where its floor is. So, plainly: the man, the wars, the dike, the aqueduct, the laws — solid record, multiply attested. The poet — real by a tradition five centuries long; songs are ascribed to him by name in a sixteenth-century codex, the *Romances de los señores de la Nueva España*. But scholars argue, fairly, about how much of the poet-king is the man and how much is the polish of later chroniclers who wanted a single noble author; Nahuatl song was in its nature anonymous, oral, and shared. And this one famous poem's paper is the thinnest of all: no ancient manuscript carries it, and its earliest reachable form is the Spanish on the money. We print all of that here, in the same breath as the poem, because a tribute that hides its evidence dishonors the man twice. What Mexico has done — putting him on its currency, carving his life along a fountain wall, teaching the poem to its schoolchildren — is a nation's verdict that the tradition is true enough to honor. This pamphlet joins that verdict, with its eyes open.

### 8. Between the killings

It is easy, from where we sit, to see that world as nothing but the altar stone and the skull rack — and the stone and the rack were real, and this pamphlet does not look away from them. But a civilization is not only its worst hour. Between the killings, and before them, and after them, there were mornings. There were men watching dawn smoke rise from their own huts. There were children held and comforted and walked with, in the ordinary hours that no chronicle bothers to record because they were too common to write down — which is to say, they were most of life.

And there was one man who had stood in a tree and watched his father die, who had been hunted like an animal through the mountains, who came in an eagle's shape into his enemy's dreams — and who, given an empire's power of revenge and forty years to use it, left the world a list of the things he loved, with his brother at the top of it. That is not a man who never learned hatred. That is a man who learned it thoroughly, and chose the other thing, and wrote the choice down in flower and song so it would outlive him.

It has. His poem rides today in a hundred million pockets, passed hand to hand all day long, spent and earned and given away — love replicating itself in the hearts and minds of people who have never heard his name. He would have understood that perfectly. It is exactly what the poem says.

*Love is the power supreme.*
