# The Achilles Lament: Questions, Answers, and Counters on Wes Cecil's Reading of Iliad Book 24

Prepared at the desk of Awen by Muse by Meta for Robert Tilford, 25 November 2026.

## Introduction

This paper works through one lecture: Wes Cecil's "Achilles Lament: A Story from the Iliad," from his Philosophical Fireside Chat series, in partnership with Radio Gwendalyn. The lecture is on YouTube at https://youtu.be/RHZAkbG96Qc.

The passage at its center is Book 24 of Homer's Iliad. King Priam of Troy crosses the battlefield at night, enters the tent of Achilles — the man who killed his son Hector and has been dragging the body behind his chariot day after day — kneels, kisses the killer's hands, and begs for the corpse. Achilles weeps with him, and gives the body back.

Robert Tilford heard the lecture in the small hours of 25 November 2026 and relayed it to this desk, with a commission in his own words: extract all the questions Cecil might have accidentally posed, answer those questions, counter those arguments, and come to a final agreement on the stance — probably, Robert said, winding up with the same conclusion as Wes Cecil.

That is what follows. The questions first. Then answers, each resting on the text of Iliad Book 24 as Cecil quotes it and on the lecture's own evidence. Then the counters, pressing Cecil's reading where it can honestly be pressed. Then the desk's settled conclusion, with its qualifications carried in the open.

One note on method. Claims about what the passage says are checked against the passage as quoted in the lecture. Claims about what the passage means are interpretive, and they are tiered as such — a reading, not a fact. The dissent is not buried at the back. It stands in its own section, in full, before the conclusion is allowed to settle.

## The Questions

Cecil asks some of his questions out loud. Others he poses without noticing — in the shape of an assertion, a comparison, or a wonder. Extracted in full, they are these.

**Question 1.** Why is this passage — Priam in Achilles' tent — one of the great passages of world literature? Cecil asks it directly: "So why is this such a great passage?"

**Question 2.** What happens to enmity when two enemies recognize their shared humanity? Cecil's claim is that "the division between the warriors just vanishes" — but is that what the text shows, and does it last?

**Question 3.** What moves Achilles? Is it Priam's humility — a king kissing the hands of his son's killer — or something else in the scene?

**Question 4.** What is the Greek relation to the gods in this passage? Cecil describes gods who are "free from care" while mortals "live in sorrow," gods more like gravity or weather than like the all-knowing, loving God of Christianity. Is that the passage's own theology?

**Question 5.** Does fate excuse, or only explain? Achilles lays the whole sorrow at the door of the gods' weaving — the two urns on Zeus's floor, one of blessings, one of ills. If the gods spun the thread, what is left of blame, and what is left of responsibility?

**Question 6.** Can compassion live inside wrath that has not gone out? Cecil himself flags the moment Achilles threatens to kill Priam in the middle of the reconciliation. What kind of compassion is it that shares a tent with a death threat?

**Question 7.** Why does a passage composed orally some 2,800 years ago still resonate — in Cecil's word, still feel "shocking"? What in it has not dated?

**Question 8.** What does the passage say about war itself — not this war only, but the condition of people trapped in a hopeless war that "drags on and on and nobody seems to know how to get it reconciled"?

## Answers

### To Question 1 — why the passage is great

Cecil gives two reasons, and the text supports both.

First, the recognition. Priam does not plead as a king to a warrior. He pleads as a father to a son: "think of your own father who is of my generation and so is likewise on the sad threshold of old age." The appeal is engineered — that word is fair — to collapse the distance between the two men, and it works inside the poem: Achilles weeps "for his father Peleus and for Patroclus," and Priam weeps for Hector, "and the sound of the lament filled the hut."

Second, the directness. Cecil notes the Greek manner of stating fortune's reversal without decoration: you were wealthy and happy; now all "has turned to battle and slaughter"; my father was showered with gifts, "yet some god brought evil even to him." The passage earns its greatness partly by refusing consolation. Achilles' comfort to Priam is, in the lecture's own quote, "cold comfort in lament" — and then: "Endure."

The desk adds a third reason, from the structure of the poem itself. The Iliad opens with Achilles' wrath as its first word and its engine. Book 24 is where that wrath is finally spent — not in victory, but in a shared grief with the enemy. A poem about rage ends with rage laying down, briefly, beside sorrow. That arc is the passage's greatness at the level of the whole work. This is an interpretive claim, and it is a standard one in the scholarship of the poem; it is stated here as a reading, not a measurement.

### To Question 2 — what happens to enmity

In the tent, for the length of the scene, enmity is suspended by recognition. Cecil's evidence is the passage's own: the two men discover the same shape in each other's lives. Priam is a father who has lost sons. Achilles is a son who will not come home to his father. "We're fathers and sons," Cecil says. "I'm like your father; my son is like you."

The suspension is real. Achilles raises the old king up by the arm, seats him, feeds him, returns the body, and grants time for the funeral.

But the answer must carry its limit in the same breath. The enmity is suspended, not abolished. Achilles remains, in Cecil's words, "still just terrifying." The war is not ended by the scene; it is paused by it. The full limit is pressed in the Counters below.

### To Question 3 — what moves Achilles

Priam's humility is the visible instrument. A king kneels. A father kisses "the hand of the man who killed my sons" — Priam himself names it as something "no other man on earth would do."

But the text, as quoted, shows the mechanism precisely: "his words moved Achilles to tears at the thought of his own father." The lever is not Priam's abasement in itself. The lever is Peleus. Priam deliberately presents himself as the mirror of Achilles' own father — same generation, same threshold of old age, same helplessness — and Achilles grieves for Peleus and Patroclus through the old man in front of him.

So the desk's answer: humility opens the door, but recognition walks through it. Achilles is not shamed into mercy. He is reminded into it — reminded that the man at his feet is what his own father will be, a bereaved old man with no protector, when the news of Achilles' own death arrives. That reading is interpretive, but it follows the sequence of the quoted text line by line.

### To Question 4 — the gods of this passage

Cecil's description is accurate to the passage he quotes. Achilles himself states the theology: "the gods have spun the thread of fate for wretched mortals; we live in sorrow while they are free from care." The two urns stand on Zeus's floor. The gods allot; men endure.

Cecil's comparison — the gods as gravity or wind, "sometimes it blows a fresh breeze and sometimes it blows a gale and a branch hits you in the head" — is a fair gloss on the passage's moral weather. These gods are not presented as loving, not presented as just, and not presented as sharing the mortal condition. Achilles believes in them completely, Cecil notes, and has no reverence of the kneeling kind: they are greater, they are immortal, and "because we have the troubles of men they can just go away."

One qualification, from the same book of the poem: the gods of Book 24 are not only weather. It is the gods who protect Hector's body from decay, the gods who guide Priam safely through the Achaean camp, and Zeus who commands the body's return. The carefree powers also intervene, repeatedly and deliberately, to make this scene happen. That fact cuts two ways, and the second cut is pressed in the Counters.

### To Question 5 — fate: excuse or explanation

In Achilles' mouth, fate functions as explanation and as consolation, not as acquittal. Notice what he does not say. He does not say: therefore I am blameless for dragging your son's body. He does not say: therefore you should not grieve. He says: this is the structure of mortal life — mixture for most, the jar of ills alone for the unluckiest — "Endure. Let your heart not grieve forever. Sorrowing for your son will achieve nothing."

The doctrine of the urns is offered to Priam as a reason to eat, to sleep, to bury his son and go on living. It is a philosophy for the sufferer, not a pardon for the agent.

And Achilles holds himself inside it. Peleus received every gift "and though but a mortal man a goddess for a wife," yet his only son is "doomed to an untimely end" and "receives no care from me since I sit here in the land of Troy." Achilles knows his own early death is already woven. Fate, in this speech, is the one thing the two men fully share — which is why it can be spoken aloud between them without either man using it as a weapon. Interpretive claim, stated as such.

### To Question 6 — compassion inside unextinguished wrath

Yes — and the passage is more honest for it. Cecil does not hide the moment: when Priam presses to leave at once with the body, Achilles flares — in Cecil's paraphrase, "don't anger me or I will kill you… don't make me go against the will of Zeus because I'll do it."

So the compassion of Book 24 is not a conversion. Achilles has not become gentle. The wrath is banked, not extinguished, and Priam — who understands exactly where he is — adjusts at once.

The desk's answer: the scene shows fellow-feeling and lethality occupying the same man in the same hour, and the poem refuses to tidy that away. That refusal is part of why the passage is believed. A reconciliation in which the dangerous man had stopped being dangerous would be a fable. This one is a truce between a grieving father and a killer whose grief has, for one night, made room beside his anger.

### To Question 7 — why it still resonates

Cecil's answer is the mortal lot: "good things and bad things seem to be mixed for everybody in all times," and the mixture has not changed in 2,800 years. Fathers still bury sons. Wars still trap the people inside them. Fortune still reverses without asking permission.

The desk adds the sharper point. The passage does not resonate because its world is like ours in costume. It resonates because its situation recurs exactly: an old man with no power left except humility, walking unarmed into the tent of the man who destroyed his family, because the alternative — the body unburied, the grief unhoused — is worse than the risk. Every age produces that walk. Every age recognizes it.

### To Question 8 — what it says about war

Cecil's reading: the Iliad, "an epic of war as usually presented," is also a sustained questioning of the war it depicts. The killing "wears on all the participants." Troy feels doomed; its best are "being killed for no reason"; even the question "why are we doing this" is alive inside the poem.

Book 24 is that questioning come to its head. The war's greatest warrior and its most bereaved father agree, in the poem's own words, that the heavenly gods "brought this wretched war upon you" and that all has turned "to battle and slaughter." Neither man can stop it. What they can do — all they can do — is refuse, for one night and one funeral, to make it worse for each other. Cecil: "let's not make it even worse than it needs to be."

That is the passage's whole politics, and it is a small one. It is also, the desk notes, the only politics the passage claims. The war resumes. The poem knows it. The men in the tent know it.

## Counters

Cecil's conclusion is that Book 24 is "a truly spectacular moment of fellow human feeling," in which a king's utter humility "brings Achilles finally from his wrath and into shared compassion at least for a little while." The desk accepts the core of that. But the reading can be pressed at five points, and honesty requires pressing them in the open.

**Counter 1 — The mercy is commanded before it is felt.** In Book 24 as a whole, Zeus has already decided the matter before Priam sets out. The gods protect Hector's body; Zeus sends word to Achilles through his mother Thetis that the body is to be given back, and sends Priam on his way under divine escort. Achilles says as much inside the scene, in the threat Cecil quotes: do not make me "go against the will of Zeus." So how free is the mercy? A counter-reading runs: Achilles obeys an order, and the weeping is real but the outcome was never in doubt. The lecture's framing — humility moving wrath — understates how thoroughly the gods have stage-managed the meeting. This counter is grounded in the poem's full Book 24, beyond the excerpt Cecil reads aloud; it qualifies the lecture, it does not overturn it, because an order obeyed through tears is still a different thing from an order obeyed through gritted teeth, and the poem dwells on the tears.

**Counter 2 — The ransom is in the room.** Priam comes "bringing a princely ransom," and Achilles accepts it. In the honour economy of the poem, the return of a body for ransom is not charity; it is the recognized transaction, the same economy in which warriors strip armour and kings weigh out gold. A hard reading says the scene is not mercy escaping the war's economics but the war's economics completing one of its proper forms — the corpse redeemed at price, honour satisfied on both sides. The desk's judgment: this counter is real but partial. The ransom explains the body's return. It does not explain the shared weeping, the meal, or Achilles lifting the old man up by the arm. Economics is in the room; it is not the only thing in the room.

**Counter 3 — The compassion is partial and temporary, and the poem says so.** The truce Achilles grants is for the funeral — a matter of days (eleven, in the poem's count) — after which the fighting resumes by agreement. Achilles will die before Troy, as he knows; Troy will fall, as Priam fears. The two men share one night of recognition inside a machine that will kill them both on schedule. Cecil concedes this with his own closing words — "at least for a little while" — but the concession deserves more weight than a closing phrase. The passage's greatness may lie precisely in the smallness of what compassion achieves here: a burial, a meal, a night's sleep under safe conduct. Not peace. Not even the end of wrath. A burial.

**Counter 4 — Achilles threatens the suppliant he has just embraced.** This is Cecil's own evidence, pressed harder than Cecil presses it. A suppliant under protection, in the ancient code, is owed safety; Achilles, mid-reconciliation, warns Priam he may kill him after all. The scene therefore does not show wrath overcome by compassion. It shows wrath and compassion alternating in the same breast, with the outcome depending on the old man's tact. Any reading that calls this the defeat of Achilles' anger overstates the text. The anger is outlasted for an evening. It is not defeated.

**Counter 5 — Whose humanity is recognized?** Priam is recognized because he can be mapped onto Peleus: a father, a king, a man of Achilles' own world and rank. The poem's fellow-feeling runs along the lines of likeness — fathers and sons, kings and princes. The unnamed dead of the war, the common soldiers of both armies, get no tent scene. A modern reader should name that boundary honestly: Book 24's compassion is profound, and it is also aristocratic, extended to the enemy who most resembles the self. This does not diminish the scene. It locates it — in its own century, not ours.

## Robert's Response

Robert's own reflection on the lecture is registered here in his voice, as his. The desk does not argue with it and does not endorse it; it is his reading, given at 1:47 in the morning, and it belongs on the record of the night.

He heard the hopeless war of the Iliad and thought at once of Ukraine and Russia — of the hatred between Putin and Zelensky, and of the lectures he had followed on that war, in which, he said, "I didn't see evil really on either side. I saw a clear moral justification on each side."

He noted that these are debates about life and death, and that resolution matters urgently. Of President Trump he said: "Trump's trying… is he perfect, no… is he human, oh yes. And is Putin human, yes." Of Zelensky he recalled that when the attack came, Zelensky went to the office and stayed.

And then his Priam-like point, in his own words: why doesn't Zelensky "pack his kids up and get them the hell out of there — because if he can't secure their safety he's going to be an emotional basket case, and we don't need him. Get them secure, and then he can go ahead and deal with the nation and the invasion."

The desk registers the shape of the thought without grading it: a leader, like a father in a tent, can only carry the unbearable negotiation if the thing he loves most is first made safe. That is Robert's application of the passage to his own century, and it is recorded as his.

## Conclusion

The desk's settled stance, after the questions, the answers, and the counters, is this.

Cecil's conclusion stands. Book 24 of the Iliad is a spectacular moment of fellow human feeling. A king's utter humility — lips to the hand of the man who killed his sons — does bring Achilles from his wrath into shared compassion, and the sound of the two men weeping together in the hut is exactly what Cecil says it is: the recognition, in the middle of a hopeless war, that the enemy is a father, a son, a fellow mortal served from the same two urns.

The qualifications stand beside it, in the open, where this desk keeps them.

The mercy was commanded by Zeus before it was felt by Achilles. The ransom was princely, and the honour economy of the war was satisfied by the exchange. The compassion shared the hour with a death threat. The truce bought a funeral, not a peace — eleven days, and then the war again, and both men dead on the schedule they already knew.

But here is the final weighing, and it is why the desk agrees with Cecil rather than merely acquiescing to him. The qualifications shrink the miracle; they do not dissolve it. An order from Zeus did not have to be obeyed with tears, a meal, and an old man lifted gently to his feet. The economy of ransom did not require Achilles to say "Endure," or to speak of Peleus, or to pity the grey hairs of the king who knelt to him. The surplus over command, over price, over truce — that surplus is the humanity Cecil names, and it is really there in the text.

The passage's lesson, as this desk settles it, is therefore Cecil's lesson, stated at its true size. Compassion between enemies is possible. It is usually partial, usually temporary, usually assisted by powers and pressures outside the two people in the room. It does not end the war. It returns a body for burial; it feeds an old man; it lets two mourners weep in one hut instead of alone. And across 2,800 years, that has been enough to make readers stop — because it is the size compassion usually is, in the wars that actually happen, and the poem is honest enough to show it at exactly that size.

Robert's expectation, when he commissioned this paper, was that the desk would probably wind up with the same conclusion as Wes Cecil. It has — with the counters on the record, which is where conclusions at this desk are required to keep their company.
