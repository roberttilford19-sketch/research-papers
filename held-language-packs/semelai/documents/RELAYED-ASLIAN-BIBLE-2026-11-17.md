## Document 2 — Aslian Bible translation methods

Bible translation into Aslian languages (Jah Hut, Semai, Semelai, and related Orang Asli languages) has been carried out mainly by the Bible Society of Malaysia, with involvement from Wycliffe-related partners and Faith Comes By Hearing for audio. The work follows standard modern Bible-translation practice adapted to small, endangered languages with limited prior written literature.

### Overall approach

Most Aslian translations use a **meaning-based / dynamic equivalence** method rather than strict formal equivalence. The goal is natural, understandable language for mother-tongue speakers, not a word-for-word calque of Hebrew or Greek.

- The Bahasa Malaysia Bible (Alkitab Berita Baik) was itself based on the English Good News Bible and dynamic equivalence; Aslian projects sit in the same tradition.
- Key-term decisions (including the word for God) are made by mother-tongue translators in consultation with consultants, then tested with community readers.
- Drafts go through multiple checking stages: team review, community checking for clarity and naturalness, and consultant checking for exegetical accuracy.

### Specific Aslian projects

| Language | Status | Main agency / format | Notes |
|----------|--------|----------------------|-------|
| **Jah Hut** | Full Bible (2023/2024) | Bible Society of Malaysia — *Bukuq Haba Agan Jahut* | Launched January 2024. Community-used God-term is attested in the published text. |
| **Semai** | New Testament + selected OT portions | Bible Society of Malaysia / Wycliffe-related; audio and apps available | Portions since the 1930s; major volumes 2019; NT completed around 2023. Diglot (Semai + Bahasa Malaysia) editions exist. |
| **Semelai** | New Testament (with audio) | Faith Comes By Hearing | Available as free app with synchronized text and audio. |

### Practical methods used

1. **Mother-tongue team + consultant model** — Local speakers draft; trained consultants (often working in Malay or English) check for accuracy. Back-translations are used so consultants can evaluate meaning without full fluency in the target language.
2. **Oral and audio components** — Many Orang Asli communities are more oral than literate. Faith Comes By Hearing and similar partners produce dramatized or plain audio of the text. Some projects begin with oral translation of narrative portions before committing to print.
3. **Use of existing linguistic work** — Translators draw on published grammars and wordlists (Diffloth on Jah Hut, Kruspe on Semelai, Benjamin on Semai/Temiar, etc.) for phonology, orthography decisions, and morphological patterns (infixation, reduplication, expressives). Orthographies are usually Latin-based and designed for community readability.
4. **Key-term workshops** — Special attention is given to religious vocabulary (God, spirit, sacrifice, sin, etc.). Decisions are recorded and applied consistently. The published Jah Hut and Semelai texts therefore contain attested community renderings of these terms.
5. **Community testing and revision** — Drafts are read aloud to groups of speakers. Feedback on naturalness, clarity, and cultural acceptability drives revisions before final consultant approval and publication.

### Relevance to the current project

For Jah Hut and Semelai, the published Scripture already contains a community-accepted God-word and a body of religious terminology. Those terms can be extracted and cited as attested usage; they do not need to be invented. The same is not true for Kiput or the other languages on the stuck list that lack published Scripture.

The methods above show that a full translation is possible when a mother-tongue team and sufficient lexical/grammatical base exist. Where that base is still too thin, the language-pack approach (research shelf only) remains the correct holding pattern until better data or a God-term surface.

## Document 3 — Semai translation challenges

Semai (Central Aslian, ~60,000 speakers) is one of the better-documented Aslian languages and has a New Testament (completed around 2023) plus earlier portions. Even so, the translation process faced — and still faces — several structural difficulties typical of Aslian languages.

### 1. Dialect diversity and lack of a single standard
Semai has three main groups (Northern, Central, Southern) and historically dozens of local varieties (Diffloth estimated more than forty dialects). Significant phonological and lexical differences exist between them. Choosing one variety (or a compromise) for a written translation inevitably leaves some speakers feeling the text is "not their language." School textbooks already show inconsistent spelling across primary and secondary materials, reflecting the same problem.

### 2. Orthography and phonology
- Semai has a large vowel inventory and pre-stopped final nasals.
- No fully standardized, community-accepted orthography existed when translation work began.
- Earlier missionary transcriptions (e.g., Nabitoepoeloe) were rejected by speakers as too distant from the spoken language.
- School materials still display inconsistent spelling and pronunciation guides, which hinders literacy development and Scripture use.

### 3. Complex morphology
Semai retains productive non-concatenative morphology:
- Coda-copy / "incopyfixation" for imperfective/progressive aspect
- Nasal infix *-n-* for nominalization and other functions
- Liquid infix *-r-* for causative
- Elaborate reduplication, especially in expressives

Translators must decide when to use these templates versus simpler or Malay-influenced forms so that the text remains natural yet accurate. Over-use of Malay calques risks distancing the translation from traditional speech; under-use can make aspect and voice unclear.

### 4. Heavy Malay contact and lexical gaps
Large parts of the modern lexicon (especially higher numbers, many abstract and religious terms) are Malay loans. Translators must decide:
- Which Malay borrowings are already fully naturalized and acceptable
- Where to coin or revive native terms
- How to handle key biblical concepts that have no traditional Semai equivalent

### 5. Oral tradition and limited literacy
Semai remains primarily an oral language for most speakers. Many communities have low functional literacy in any language. This favors audio Scripture (Faith Comes By Hearing and similar productions) and requires careful oral testing, but it also means printed editions reach fewer people and are harder to revise.

### 6. Sociolinguistic pressures
- Language shift toward Malay is underway, especially among younger speakers in more accessible communities.
- Christian work historically met some resistance; conversion and Scripture use remain uneven.
- The language is not endangered in the same way as smaller Aslian languages, yet the written form is still fragile.

### Implications for the current project
Semai demonstrates that a New Testament is achievable in an Aslian language when a mother-tongue team, consultant support, and sufficient lexical base exist. The same challenges (dialect choice, orthography, morphological naturalness, and key-term decisions) appear in Jah Hut and Semelai. For languages on the stuck list that lack even a partial Scripture base (Kiput, Belait, etc.), these difficulties are currently insurmountable without new data or community involvement — hence the language-pack approach on the research shelf.

**Sources**: Bible Society of Malaysia and Joshua Project records on Semai Scripture; Diffloth and Benjamin on Aslian morphology and dialectology; school-textbook orthography studies; Faith Comes By Hearing audio materials.

## Document 5 — Semelai New Testament lexical analysis (relayed paper)

### What exists
- **New Testament text**: Faith Comes By Hearing app presents a Semelai New Testament with synchronized audio. Wycliffe Malaysia confirmed Mark was completed in January 2022. Joshua Project's older "portions only" entry is outdated.
- **Primary linguistic resource**: Nicole Kruspe, *A Grammar of Semelai* (Cambridge University Press, 2004). This is the standard reference grammar. It contains a short vocabulary appendix (pp. 448–465) plus extensive illustrative examples and texts throughout the grammar. The full book is purchase-only or library-restricted; no free public digital wordlist of substantial size is available.
- **No free, comprehensive Semelai lexicon or NT concordance** has been published that can be downloaded and counted without access to the app text or Kruspe's book.

### What a lexical analysis can currently show
Because the full NT text is inside the FCBH app (not freely dumpable as a searchable corpus), and Kruspe's vocabulary is not open-access, a precise unique-key count of the Semelai NT is not yet public. The following points are nevertheless clear from the available sources:

1. **God-word is attested**
   The existence of a completed Mark and an NT app means the translators settled on a community-used term for God. That open seat is closed.
2. **Religious and narrative vocabulary is present**
   A New Testament supplies attested terms for core religious concepts (spirit, sin, sacrifice, believe, kingdom, etc.) and a wide range of narrative and everyday vocabulary. This is a genuine control corpus, though smaller than a full Bible.
3. **Kruspe's grammar supplies the morphological rules**
   Semelai has a dual morphological system:
   - Inherited Aslian non-concatenative processes (infixation, coda copy / incopyfixation, reduplication)
   - Malay-derived concatenative prefixes and suffixes
   Any lexical analysis or translation must respect both systems so that forms remain natural.
4. **Malay loans are heavy**
   As with other Aslian languages, higher numbers, many abstract terms, and some religious vocabulary are Malay borrowings already adapted to Semelai phonology. These can be counted as usable keys once confirmed in the NT text.

### Practical implications for the project
- A full unique-word extraction of the Semelai NT (from the FCBH app text) plus Kruspe's published examples would give the best available key count for both the Quran re-run and a potential Psalms feasibility check.
- Until that extraction is done, the exact number of usable keys remains unknown, but the presence of an NT already places Semelai well above a pure wicket (unlike Kiput).
- The main remaining obstacles for a Semelai Psalms or Quran build are (a) obtaining a clean text dump of the NT for counting, and (b) deciding whether to purchase or access Kruspe's full vocabulary appendix.

**Summary**: The Semelai NT is a real attested corpus that closes the God-word seat and supplies substantial religious and narrative vocabulary. A precise lexical count still requires extracting the app text (and ideally Kruspe's appendix). That count is the single most useful next step for deciding whether Semelai can support a Psalms translation or clear the Quran gate.
