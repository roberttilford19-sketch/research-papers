**Austroasiatic linguistic features** (with focus on the Aslian branch relevant to Jah Hut and Semelai)

Austroasiatic is a major language family of mainland Southeast Asia and parts of India, with roughly 160+ languages. The two largest national languages are Vietnamese and Khmer. The southernmost branch is **Aslian**, spoken by Orang Asli groups on the Malay Peninsula (including Jah Hut and Semelai).

### Core typological profile of Austroasiatic

Austroasiatic languages show strong regional differentiation:

- **Munda** (India): verb-final, agglutinating, with case marking and richer inflection.
- **Nicobarese**: verb-initial with complex morphology.
- **Mainland Southeast Asian branches** (Mon-Khmer broadly, including Aslian, Khmeric, Bahnaric, Katuic, Vietic, etc.): predominantly isolating or weakly morphological, verb-medial (SVO), head-initial, with little inflection. Most of the family falls here.

Common inherited traits across much of the family include:
- Iambic stress (stress on the final syllable).
- Preference for prefixes and infixes over suffixes.
- Sesquisyllabic word structure (a reduced minor syllable + fully stressed major syllable).
- Relatively large vowel inventories.
- Derivational morphology that is often non-concatenative (infixation, reduplication).

### Phonological features (especially Aslian)

- **Consonant inventory**: Typically rich. Aslian languages often have 20–32 consonants. Oral stops at multiple places (labial, alveolar, palatal, velar, glottal), with voiced/voiceless (and sometimes aspirated) contrasts. Nasals at corresponding places. Glottal stop and /h/ are important. Aslian generally lacks implosives (unlike some other Austroasiatic branches).
- **Vowels**: Very large systems are typical. Semelai has ~20 vowel phonemes; some Central Aslian languages reach 30+. Length, nasality, and sometimes phonation (register) distinctions appear. Minor syllables are restricted (often only schwa or a limited set).
- **Syllable structure**: Words are monosyllabic, sesquisyllabic, or (less often) disyllabic. Final nasals are frequently pre-stopped in Aslian (e.g., realized as stop + nasal). Complex onsets are allowed.
- **Suprasegmentals**: Register (phonation type) or tone appears in some branches (especially Vietic). Aslian is largely non-tonal but can show register effects and complex prosody.

### Morphological features (Aslian highlight)

Aslian languages are notable for retaining and elaborating older Austroasiatic morphological patterns:

- **Infixation**: Classic and productive. Nasal infix `-n-` (or variants) is widespread for nominalization, causatives, or other derivations. Other infixes appear for aspect, number, or expressives.
  Examples (from literature):
  - Jah Hut causatives with prefixes/infixes such as *p-*, *pr-*, *pn-*.
  - Nominalizing or enumerative uses of `-n-` across Aslian.
- **Prefixation**: Common for causation, aspect, or derivation. Often combined with reduplication.
- **Reduplication**: Highly productive and varied (full, partial, infix reduplication, alternating reduplication). Used for aspect (imperfective, progressive), intensity, plurality, or expressive elaboration. Aslian is known for intricate template-style reduplication.
- **Expressives / ideophones**: A major lexical class in Aslian (especially well-documented in Semelai, Semai, Jah Hut). These are iconic words that simultaneously describe a predicate and its arguments or sensory qualities. They form a distinct word class alongside nouns and verbs.
- **Mixed systems**: Semelai (and some other Aslian languages) combine inherited non-concatenative Mon-Khmer morphology with concatenative prefixes/suffixes borrowed or calqued from Malay contact.
- **Word classes**: Clear distinction between nominals, verbs, and expressives in most well-described Aslian languages. Verbs often show rich derivational morphology affecting transitivity and aspect.

### Syntax

- Predominantly **SVO / verb-medial**.
- Head-initial: modifiers and relative clauses follow the head noun.
- Little case marking; grammatical relations are handled by word order, prepositions, and verbal morphology (voice/aspect).
- Classifiers appear in some languages, influenced by areal (Malay/Thai) patterns.
- Pronoun systems can include specialized avoidance or honorific registers (documented in several Aslian languages for in-laws).

### Relevance to Jah Hut and Semelai

- **Jah Hut**: Documented primarily in Diffloth 1976 (grammar sketch + vocabulary). Shows classic Aslian sesquisyllables, complex consonant clusters, infixation, and reduplication.
- **Semelai**: The best-described Aslian language (Kruspe 2004 grammar). Features a large consonant and vowel inventory, dual morphological systems (inherited non-concatenative + Malay-influenced concatenative), a rich expressive class, and detailed verbal morphology.

These features make Aslian languages typologically distinct from the surrounding Austronesian (Malayic) languages, even while showing heavy contact influence in lexicon and some morphology.

**Key references**: Jenny, Weber & Weymuth (2015) typological overview in *The Handbook of Austroasiatic Languages*; Kruspe (2004) *A Grammar of Semelai*; Diffloth (1976) on Jah Hut; Burenhult on Jahai; Benjamin on broader Aslian assessment.

## Document 2 — Aslian verbal templates

Aslian languages (Northern, Central/Senoic, and Southern branches) are known for complex non-concatenative verbal morphology. Verbs are typically built from a root by means of **templates** that combine reduplication (especially of consonants), infixation, and fixed segments. These processes are sensitive to the syllabic structure of the root and are analyzed in the framework of Prosodic Morphology (McCarthy & Prince and later Optimality-Theoretic accounts).

The best-documented systems are Temiar and Semai (Central Aslian). Similar patterns appear across the family, including in Semelai, Jah Hut, and Northern Aslian languages.

### Core process: "incopyfixation" (coda copy)

The most characteristic Aslian verbal template copies the final consonant of the root and places it earlier in the word (often into a minor syllable). This is widely used for imperfective / continuative / progressive aspect.

**Basic patterns** (simplified from Benjamin, Diffloth, and later analyses):

| Root type | Template (schematic) | Example language | Function |
|-----------|----------------------|------------------|----------|
| Monosyllabic CVC | C₁C_f.C₁VC_f | Temiar, Semai, Semelai, Batek | Continuative / imperfective |
| Biconsonantal / sesquisyllabic | C₁C_f.C₂VC_f or infix placement | Temiar, Semai | Continuative |
| With initial reduplication | C₁C_f.C₁VC_f | Northern & Southern Aslian | Progressive |

Illustrative examples (approximate orthography from sources):
- Temiar: *kɔːw* 'call' → *kw.kɔːw* 'be calling'
- Semelai: *tʰəm* 'pound' → *tm.tʰəm* 'is pounding'
- Batek: *kɯc* 'grate' → *kc.kɯc* 'is grating'

The copy is usually the root-final consonant only; the vowel is not copied. This creates a characteristic minor syllable + major syllable (sesquisyllabic) shape.

### Other major verbal templates

1. **Simulfactive / punctual** (especially Temiar)
   Fixed vowel *a* + consonant copy:
   CVC → C*a*.CVC
   Example: *kɔːw* → *ka.kɔːw* 'call (punctual)'
2. **Causative**
   Often involves the liquid infix *-r-* (or prefix *par-/tar-*), sometimes combined with reduplication. Productive in Temiar and Semai.
3. **Nominalizing / other derivations**
   Nasal infix *-n-* (classic Austroasiatic) is widespread and often interacts with the reduplicative templates.
4. **Expressives**
   More elaborate consonant-copying templates appear in the expressive word class (especially Semai), sometimes copying both ends of the root without the vowel.

### Structural generalizations

- **Prosodic sensitivity**: Placement of the copied material and fixed segments depends on whether the root is mono- or disyllabic / has a complex onset. Monosyllabic roots typically receive a prefixed minor syllable; longer roots more often show infixation.
- **Iambic preference**: Stress falls on the final (major) syllable; the reduplicant is unstressed.
- **No full vowel copy in most aspectual forms**: Only consonants are reduplicated in the core imperfective templates.
- **Branch variation**:
  - Central Aslian (Temiar, Semai) has the richest and most regular verbal paradigms.
  - Southern Aslian (Semelai) retains coda-copy imperfectives but also has a large borrowed Malay concatenative system.
  - Northern Aslian shows similar progressive templates, sometimes with additional phonological reductions.

### Theoretical notes

These patterns have been extensively analyzed as:
- Prosodic templates (McCarthy & Prince)
- A-templatic reduplication emerging from constraint interaction (Gafos and others)
- Iambic reduction and historical retention of consonants in the reduplicant (Phillips on Semai)

The same formal devices (coda copy, fixed-vowel infixation, nasal infix) recur with different functions across Aslian, making the family a classic case for the study of non-concatenative morphology.

**Key sources**: Benjamin (Temiar grammar and Aslian surveys), Diffloth (Semai expressives and Aslian morphology), Kruspe (Semelai), Burenhult (Jahai), Gafos (Temiar prosodic morphology), and the overview in Matisoff's Aslian survey.

## Document 3 — Austroasiatic nasal infixes

The nasal infix is one of the most characteristic morphological features of the Austroasiatic family. The best-reconstructed form is **\*-n-**, which is primarily a nominalizer. Related nasal elements (\*-m-, or nasal increments on prefixes) also appear.

### Proto-Austroasiatic reconstruction

Paul Sidwell and others reconstruct the following core set of Proto-Austroasiatic (pAA) affixes:

| Affix | Function | Type |
|-------|----------|------|
| \*-n- | Nominalizer (instrument, result, action, agent in various branches) | Infix |
| \*-m- | Nominalizer (often agentive) | Infix |
| \*-r- / \*-l- | Nominalizer / iterative / reciprocal | Infix |
| \*-p- | Nominalizer (instrumental) | Infix |
| \*p- / \*pC- | Causative | Prefix |
| \*t- / \*tN- | Reciprocal | Prefix |

The nasal infix \*-n- is inserted immediately after the first consonant of the root (or after the initial cluster). It is one of the oldest and most widely retained derivational devices in the family.

### Functions and distribution

- **Primary function**: Derives nouns from verbs (instrument, result, action, or agent).
  Classic examples across branches:
  - Bahnaric: *par* 'to fly' → *pənar* 'wing'
  - Katuic: *caː* 'to eat' → *canaː* 'food'
  - Khmeric: *poəl* 'to try' → *pnoəl* 'wager'
  - Monic: *cɛʔ* 'to eat' → *kənaʔ* 'food'
  - Aslian (Semai): *ca* 'to eat' → *caʔna* 'food'
- **Secondary or extended functions** (especially in Aslian and some other branches):
  - Imperfective or aspectual marking
  - Enumerative or collective forms
  - Kinship and relational terms
  - Count vs. mass noun distinction

The infix remains productive in some peripheral branches (especially Aslian and parts of Katuic/Bahnaric) but is largely fossilized in the more isolating central branches (Khmeric, Vietic, Monic).

### Aslian developments

In Aslian languages the nasal infix is especially active and frequently combines with other processes (coda copy / "incopyfixation", other infixes, reduplication):

- **Nominalizer**: Temiar *cɛr* 'to pare' → *cənɛr* 'knife'; Semai and others show similar patterns.
- **Agentive**: Jah Hut uses *mʔ-* or related forms for agentive nominalizations.
- **Aspectual / imperfective**: Some Northern Aslian languages use nasal forms for imperfective aspect.
- **Combined with coda copy**: Common for action nominalizations or progressive forms (e.g., prefix *n-* + coda copy).
- **Other uses**: Count nouns from mass nouns (Semai), enumeratives (Jahai), kinship distinctions.

Placement remains consistent with the family pattern: after the leftmost consonant(s).

### Phonological and historical notes

- Infixes always attach to the right of the initial consonant(s), creating a minor syllable.
- Nasal place of articulation may assimilate to a following consonant.
- In many languages the infix has become less transparent due to vowel reduction in the minor syllable or historical sound changes.
- Some researchers have speculated that nasal infixes may ultimately relate to older prefixal material or even implosive series, but the \*-n- nominalizer is securely reconstructed as an infix in Proto-Austroasiatic.

### Summary

The nasal infix \*-n- (and related \*-m-) is a core inherited feature of Austroasiatic morphology. Its main role is nominalization of verbs. It is most productive and morphologically elaborate in Aslian, where it interacts with the family's characteristic reduplicative and template-based processes. In many other branches it survives mainly as fossilized residues in the lexicon.

**Key references**: Sidwell (comparative reconstructions), Shorto (*A Mon-Khmer Comparative Dictionary*), Jenny et al. (*The Handbook of Austroasiatic Languages*), Matisoff (Aslian survey), and branch-specific grammars (Kruspe on Semelai, Benjamin on Temiar, Burenhult on Jahai, Diffloth on Jah Hut and Semai).
