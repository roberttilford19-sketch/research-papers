# Kiput — A Held-Language Research Pack

This pack gathers everything assembled at this desk about the Kiput language in the course of Robert Tilford's Quran translation project. It is a research pack, not a translation. No Kiput Quran translation exists, and none is claimed here or anywhere in this project. What follows is the record of an honest search: what was looked for, what was found, and the one thing that was not found, named plainly so that no one mistakes this shelf for a finished work.

## The language

Kiput (ISO 639-3: kyi) belongs to the Berawan–Lower Baram branch of the North Sarawak languages, spoken along the Baram River in Sarawak, Malaysia, at Long Kiput and Kuala Tutoh and in the Muslim settlements of Kampung Benawa and Lubok Nibong. About 450 people speak it; the wider Kiput community numbers roughly 1,400 to 2,000. Ethnologue grades the language EGIDS 6b, Threatened, and catalogues its writing status in a single word: "Unwritten." Kiput has never been a language of school or print. Its children are schooled in Malay, its church life runs bilingually with Iban, and its entire published record, worldwide, is one linguist's work.

## The base, and why the lane is held

The sole published lexical base for Kiput is Robert Blust's *A short morphology, phonology and vocabulary of Kiput, Sarawak* (Pacific Linguistics 546, The Australian National University, 2003): a vocabulary of 931 items distilled from twenty to twenty-five hours of elicitation in 1971. Read generously against the project's key set, that vocabulary grounds roughly 370 keys, well below the floor a scripture draft requires. More decisive still is what the book does not contain. Across its whole vocabulary there is no attestation for God, heaven, hell, angel, prayer, mercy, prophet, Lord, or soul. Its only spirit word is *akaaŋ*, "ghost, spirit of the dead" — a real word, honestly recorded, and not a word on which a translation of the Quran could rest. A draft built on this base would have to invent its God-word and its whole theological register, and this house does not invent. The lane is therefore HELD.

## The open seat

The pack's status can be stated in one line, and it is stated here verbatim so that it travels with the pack wherever the pack goes:

**Open seat: "God-term unverified — community usage not yet located."**

That the term exists is not in doubt. The Kiput of Kuala Tutoh have been Catholic for more than a century, and a peer-reviewed ethnography published in March 2026 records Sunday worship conducted in Kiput, with hymns and liturgy in the language. A community that has prayed in Kiput for a hundred years has a word for God. But three independent searches — the desk's wicket pass over Blust's vocabulary, the desk's wider second hunt across the catalogues, and an independent Grok research return commissioned for the purpose — agree that the word appears in no reachable print: no Bible portion, hymnal, catechism, schoolbook, or second dictionary exists in any catalogue searched, and Ethnologue's whole entry for Kiput's language development is two items, "Dictionary. Grammar." — both of them the one book already in hand.

## The one road not taken

If any printed Kiput sacred text exists on Earth, it would be uncatalogued parish material at Kuala Tutoh — a typed hymn sheet, a catechist's notebook — and reaching it would mean contacting the parish or the Diocese of Miri. That is outreach to a small community, and under this project's standing rules it is Robert Tilford's call alone. It has not been taken. Until it is taken, or until a base surfaces by some other road, this pack is the lane's honest resting place: everything known, gathered in one place, with the gap named rather than papered over.
