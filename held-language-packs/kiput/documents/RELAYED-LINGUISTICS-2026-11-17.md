## Document 1 — Clear direction on Kiput (relayed memo)

You do **not** have to invent a God-word. The community already has one (or more). The task is to find the term they actually use, not to supply one.

### Why this is not a dead end

Kiput is spoken by a small community (roughly 450–2,500 people) centered on Long Kiput / Kuala Tutoh on the Baram River in Sarawak, with additional Muslim settlements (e.g., Kampung Benawa). A substantial portion of the Long Kiput community has been Christian (largely Catholic) since the mid-20th century. They hold services in the language. A people who have been worshipping in Kiput for decades already have an established term for God used in prayer, hymns, and liturgy. That term exists; it simply has not yet been located in the materials currently on the desk.

They also have a published linguistic description: Robert Blust's *A Short Morphology, Phonology and Vocabulary of Kiput, Sarawak* (2003), which contains roughly 900 items. Traditional animist terms for spirits or higher powers may appear there and can be evaluated for suitability or rejection. Muslim community members will use Islamic terminology already present in the local Malay-influenced environment.

### Recommended path forward

1. **Primary hunt (highest priority)** — Search specifically for Catholic materials connected to Long Kiput and the Diocese of Miri: hymn books, prayer books, catechisms, liturgy sheets, or any recorded services that use Kiput. School materials (even primary-level) from Sarawak's Orang Ulu or Baram-area programs are secondary but still useful for general vocabulary. Dewan Bahasa publications and any community-produced texts at Long Kiput itself remain in scope.
2. **Check existing published sources** — Review Blust's 2003 vocabulary for any terms related to spirit, creator, sky, prayer, or the divine. Note them without forcing them into theological use. Joshua Project lists Bible translation as "started," so confirm whether any draft portions or term lists exist.
3. **Related-language controls (support only)** — Kiput is closest to Belait (Brunei) and other Lower Baram languages (Miriek, Narum, etc.). Check those for God-terms used in Christian or Islamic contexts, but treat them as supporting evidence only. Local Kiput usage takes precedence.
4. **Decision rule (unchanged)** — Do not invent. If the cupboard is truly empty after the above, the language stays held with a named open seat ("God-term unverified — community usage not yet located"). That is cleaner than landing a book with an artificial term.

### Practical implications

- This is a low-volume, high-precision case. One confirmed local term unlocks the lane.
- The Catholic longhouse community is the most promising single source.
- Malay is already in daily use alongside Kiput, so "Allah" or "Tuhan" may already be the working terms; that is acceptable if it is what the speakers themselves use.
- School materials will help build the rest of the glossary even if the God-term takes longer.

The wider hunt already running is the right move. Continue it with the sharper focus above (Catholic community materials first, Blust vocabulary second). If a term surfaces, it can be locked under the same gate standard used for the rest of the Malayic and Bornean work. If nothing appears after a thorough search, document the absence and hold the lane rather than invent. That keeps the rule intact and gives the language a real path instead of an artificial one.

## Document 2 — Blust's Kiput morphology (2003), summary

Summary drawn from *A Short Morphology, Phonology and Vocabulary of Kiput, Sarawak* (Pacific Linguistics 546). The full description is short; Blust notes that the overall complexity of affixation is roughly comparable to Malay.

### Core affixes and processes

| Form | Function | Notes / examples |
|------|----------|------------------|
| **Verbal ablaut** | Active vs. passive/perfective distinction | Characteristic of northern Sarawak languages. In stems with penultimate schwa, the vowel is replaced (typically schwa → /u/ or similar for active, other patterns for passive). Historically derived from the older infixes *-um-* and *-in-*. |
| **-em-** | Active verb (transitive or intransitive) | Infix. |
| **-en-** | Passive / perfective verb | Infix. Often signals completed or patient-oriented action. |
| **me-** | Stative verb | Prefix. |
| **ŋ-** | Active verb (usually transitive or causative; sometimes intransitive) | Prefix; involves nasal substitution on the initial consonant of the stem. Distinct from true nasal substitution in some cases ("pseudo nasal substitution" historically from *-um-*). |
| **ke-** | Causative verb | Prefix. |
| **pe-** | Nominalizer **or** causative | Two functions; context distinguishes them. |
| **man-** | Imperative | Prefixed to the verb stem or active form. |
| **se- / sek- / sel- / sep-** | Reciprocal or reflexive | Allomorphs conditioned by the initial sound of the base. *sel-* and *sep-* appear mainly with vowel-initial bases. Some forms show a refined vs. coarse distinction (e.g., *sel-abit* vs. *sep-abit* "hold each other"). |
| **sep-** (simulative) | "Act like / pretend to" | Less common function of the same prefix shape. |
| **Full reduplication** | Various (intensity, iteration, etc.) | Productive. |

A few other affixes appear only once or twice and are treated as marginal (some are clear Malay loans, e.g., *be-* in *be-lagu'* "to sing").

### Notable features

- **Ablaut** is the most distinctive morphological trait relative to Malay. It is widespread in North Sarawak languages and is the historical residue of the Proto-Austronesian actor and patient infixes *-um-* and *-in-*.
- The system is largely prefixing and infixing; true suffixes are rare or absent in the recorded data.
- Reciprocal/reflexive marking is relatively rich, with several allomorphs of *se-*.
- Many stative verbs are zero-derived (no affix).
- The overall inventory is modest — comparable in complexity to colloquial Malay rather than to the heavier Philippine-type voice systems.

### Implications for translation work

- The morphology is manageable. Most derivations can be handled with a relatively small set of regular rules once the ablaut patterns and the *ŋ-* / *-em-* alternations are mapped.
- Because ablaut and the infixes *-em-* / *-en-* carry voice and aspect information, any translation of narrative or legal language in the Quran will need consistent treatment of these forms.
- Existing vocabulary in Blust's ~900-item list already contains many affixed forms, so the morphological patterns can be reverse-engineered from the published data without waiting for new fieldwork.
- The language is not "too hard" on morphological grounds; the bottleneck remains lexical coverage (especially abstract and religious terms) and verification of community usage, not the shape of the grammar.

This is the main morphological picture available from Blust's description. The full PDF is openly available from the Australian National University repository if a deeper extraction of examples is needed.

## Document 3 — Kiput vs. Standard Malay affixation

Blust (2003) explicitly notes that Kiput's overall morphological complexity is roughly comparable to Malay. The two systems are similar in scale and in being predominantly prefixing, but they differ in which historical elements they preserved and how they mark voice.

### Side-by-side comparison of major verbal devices

| Function | Kiput (Blust 2003) | Standard Malay / Indonesian | Notes |
|----------|--------------------|-----------------------------|-------|
| **Active / actor voice** | *-em-* (infix) or *ŋ-* (prefix with nasal substitution) | *meN-* (prefix with nasal substitution) | Closely related historically. Kiput keeps both the older infix and the nasal-prefix strategy. |
| **Passive / patient voice** | *-en-* (infix) or ablaut | *di-* (prefix) | Major difference. Kiput preserves the older infix; Malay replaced it with the later prefix *di-*. |
| **Stative** | *me-* | *ber-* / *ter-* (partial overlap) | Partial functional match only. |
| **Causative** | *ke-*, *pe-* | *per-*, *meN-…-kan* | Both have causative prefixes; Malay also uses the suffix *-kan*. |
| **Reciprocal / reflexive** | *se-*, *sek-*, *sel-*, *sep-* | *ber-…-an*, *saling*, or zero | Kiput has a dedicated set of prefixes; Malay mostly uses *ber-* + reduplication or the free word *saling*. |
| **Nominalizer** | *pe-* | *peN-*, *ke-…-an* | Similar origin; Malay has a richer set. |
| **Imperative** | *man-* | usually bare stem or *meN-* | Kiput has a dedicated prefix. |
| **Ablaut** | Yes (systematic in many stems) | No | Distinctive North Sarawak feature. Historically from the same *-um-* / *-in-* system that Malay largely lost. |
| **Suffixes** | Rare or absent in the data | *-kan*, *-i* (highly productive) | Clear difference. Malay has productive applicative suffixes; Kiput does not. |
| **Reduplication** | Full reduplication | Full and partial reduplication | Both use it productively. |

### Key points of similarity

- Both systems are modest in size (neither is a heavy Philippine-type voice language).
- Both rely heavily on prefixes and nasal substitution for active verbs.
- Both have a *se-* type element (reciprocal/simulative in Kiput; "one/same/as" in Malay, with some reciprocal uses).
- Complexity level is comparable; neither language will require a large morphological apparatus for translation work.

### Key points of difference

1. **Voice marking** — Kiput still uses the older Austronesian infixes *-em-* (actor) and *-en-* (patient/perfective) plus ablaut. Malay has replaced the patient voice with the prefix *di-* and largely abandoned the infix system.
2. **Ablaut** — This is the most noticeable surface difference. In Kiput, many verbs change a penultimate schwa (or other vowel) to signal active vs. passive. Malay has no equivalent.
3. **Suffixes** — Malay's *-kan* and *-i* are highly productive for adding arguments or changing valency. Kiput appears to lack a comparable productive suffix system in the recorded data.
4. **Reciprocal marking** — Kiput has a relatively rich set of *se-*/ *sel-*/ *sep-* prefixes. Malay prefers *ber-* + reduplication or the free adverb *saling*.

### Practical implications for translation

- A translator who already works with Malay will find Kiput's morphology familiar in scale and mostly prefixing.
- The main new habits to acquire are: (1) recognizing ablaut as a voice marker, and (2) treating *-en-* / *n-* as the regular passive/perfective instead of expecting *di-*.
- Because Kiput lacks Malay's productive *-kan* / *-i* suffixes, some constructions that are single words in Malay may need to be expressed with periphrastic or multi-word structures in Kiput (or vice versa).
- The system is regular enough that, once the ablaut patterns and the *-em-* / *ŋ-* alternation are mapped from Blust's examples, most new verbs can be generated predictably.

Overall, Blust's assessment holds: the two systems are of similar complexity. The differences are real but concentrated in voice marking and the absence of productive suffixes, not in an explosion of new morphology.

## Document 4 — Blust's ablaut patterns in Kiput

Ablaut is one of the most distinctive morphological features of Kiput (and of several other North Sarawak languages). Blust treats it in detail in his 2003 morphology sketch and in the broader 1997 paper "Ablaut in Northwest Borneo." The pattern is regular, historically transparent, and limited in scope.

### The basic pattern

In stems that contain a **penultimate schwa**, Kiput forms a three-way alternation:

| Form | Vowel in penultimate syllable | Function | Historical source |
|------|-------------------------------|----------|-------------------|
| Base / neutral | ə | Nominal, imperative, or unmarked stem | Original *CeCVC |
| Active | u | Actor voice (transitive or intransitive) | Earlier *-um- infix + later sound changes |
| Passive / perfective | i | Patient voice + perfective | Earlier *-in- infix + later sound changes |

**Historical pathway** (simplified from Blust):

1. Original forms: *CeCVC (base), *C-um-eCVC (active), *C-in-eCVC (passive).
2. Schwa deleted in the environment VC_CV.
3. Resulting consonant clusters reduced.
4. Outcome: CeCVC → CuCVC (active) → CiCVC (passive).

Stems with a penultimate vowel other than schwa generally keep the older infixes (*-em-* / *-en-*) instead of undergoing ablaut.

### Recorded examples (from Blust's Table 5)

| Base | Active | Passive | Gloss |
|------|--------|---------|-------|
| — | duket | — | stick, adhere |
| se-denek | dunek | dinek | press down |
| /esie/ | usie | isie | give |
| se-getep | gutep | gitep | bite |
| /peput/ | m-u-put | n-i-put | sting (as a bee) |

Additional notes from the data:

- Some verbs combine ablaut with other marking (e.g., nasal substitution + u-ablaut, or n- + i-ablaut).
- Labial-initial bases sometimes lose the initial CV- when the passive infix is added, producing surface forms such as *n-iput*.
- The passive form with i-ablaut is typically perfective as well as patient-oriented.

### Scope and productivity

- Ablaut is **not** used on every verb. It is largely restricted to stems that historically had penultimate schwa.
- It coexists with the still-productive infixes *-em-* and *-en-* and with the prefix *ŋ-*.
- In some cases the same stem may appear with both ablaut and an affix (redundant marking).
- The system is regular enough that, once the underlying shape of a stem is known, the active and passive forms are predictable.

### Practical implications for translation

- Voice and aspect information that Malay expresses with *meN-* / *di-* is often carried in Kiput by the vowel itself (u vs. i).
- When reviewing or generating verb forms, first check whether the stem has (or historically had) penultimate schwa; if it does, expect the u/i alternation.
- The pattern is small and closed. It does not create an open-ended morphological problem; it is a finite set of alternations that can be listed and applied consistently.

This is the full picture of Kiput ablaut as described by Blust: a historically regular, phonologically conditioned vowel alternation that marks active vs. passive/perfective on a defined subset of verbs.

## Document 5 — Proto-North Sarawak ablaut origins

The verbal ablaut found in Kiput and other North Sarawak languages is a secondary development. It did not exist in Proto-Austronesian or Proto-Malayo-Polynesian. It arose inside the North Sarawak subgroup from the older Austronesian actor and patient infixes under specific phonological conditions. The standard account is Blust (1997), "Ablaut in Northwest Borneo."

### Starting point

Proto-North Sarawak (and earlier stages) still had the classic Austronesian verbal infixes:

- *-um-* — actor voice (active)
- *-in-* — patient voice / perfective

These were productive on many stems. At this stage there was no ablaut.

### The critical phonological conditions

Ablaut developed only in stems that had a **penultimate schwa** (*ə*). The sequence of changes was:

1. **Schwa deletion** — Schwa was deleted in the environment VC_CV. This turned forms such as *C-um-əCVC* and *C-in-əCVC* into consonant clusters.
2. **Cluster reduction** — The resulting clusters simplified. The vowel of the old infix survived as the new penultimate vowel of the stem.

Outcome for stems of the shape *CəCVC*:

| Original form | After changes | Resulting vowel | Function |
|---------------|---------------|-----------------|----------|
| *CəCVC* | CəCVC | ə | Base / unmarked |
| *C-um-əCVC* | CuCVC | u | Active |
| *C-in-əCVC* | CiCVC | i | Passive / perfective |

This produced the three-way alternation still visible in Kiput: **ə** (base) : **u** (active) : **i** (passive).

Stems that did **not** have penultimate schwa kept the older infixes (which later became *-em-* / *-en-* or *m-* / *n-*). The two strategies therefore exist in complementary distribution in many of the languages.

### Compound ablaut

In some North Sarawak languages a further development occurred. Homorganic nasal substitution of the initial consonant combined with the vowel change, producing what Blust calls **compound ablaut**. This arose through metathesis motivated by the avoidance of certain consonant sequences. Not every language with simple ablaut also has the compound type; Kiput shows mainly the simple pattern.

### Distribution

Simple ablaut is found in at least seven North Sarawak languages (including Kiput, various Berawan dialects, and others in the Berawan–Lower Baram and related groups). It is a shared innovation of this area, not an inheritance from higher levels of the family.

### Summary of the origin

| Stage | System | Mechanism |
|-------|--------|-----------|
| Proto-Austronesian / early Malayo-Polynesian | Infixes *-um-* and *-in-* | Affixation only |
| Proto-North Sarawak | Same infixes still present | No ablaut yet |
| After schwa deletion + cluster reduction (North Sarawak languages) | Ablaut (ə / u / i) on schwa-penultimate stems | Phonological reduction of the old infixes |

The ablaut system is therefore a clear case of morphology arising from phonology. The original grammatical markers (*-um-* and *-in-*) were not lost; their vowels were reanalyzed as part of the stem once the surrounding phonological conditions destroyed the earlier structure. This is the standard historical account of how Proto-North Sarawak (and its daughter languages) acquired verbal ablaut.

## Document 6 — Northwest Borneo linguistics: overview

The languages of northwestern and northern Borneo form a well-studied but internally diverse part of the Austronesian family. The main modern framework comes from the work of Robert Blust, later refined by Alexander Smith and others. For the Region 1 translation work, the most relevant units are **North Sarawak** (which includes Kiput) and the broader **Greater North Borneo** hypothesis that places Malayic languages in the same larger group.

### Core classification

**1. North Sarawak subgroup** (Blust 1969, 1991, 2010)
Defined by a single highly distinctive phonological innovation: the split of Proto-Austronesian voiced stops into a plain series and a second "phonetically complex" series (voiced aspirates or implosives). This is one of the strongest phonological subgroups in western Indonesia.

Primary branches (Smith 2017 refinement):
- **Berawan–Lower Baram** (includes Kiput, Belait, Narum, Miriek/Miri, Berawan dialects, etc.)
- **Dayic** (Kelabit, Lun Bawang/Lun Dayeh, Sa'ban, etc.)
- **Kenyah** (many dialects across central Borneo)
- **Bintulu** (position still somewhat uncertain)

Kiput belongs squarely inside Berawan–Lower Baram.

**2. North Borneo** (Blust)
Groups North Sarawak together with the languages of Sabah:
- Northeast Sabah (Idaanic, Bonggi, etc.)
- Southwest Sabah (Dusunic, Murutic, Paitanic, etc.)
- North Sarawak

**3. Greater North Borneo** (Blust 2010; Smith 2017)
A larger proposed unit defined mainly by lexical innovations (most famous is the replacement of the word for "seven": *tuzuq instead of the older *pitu). It includes:
- North Borneo
- Central Sarawak (Melanau, Kajang, Punan–Müller-Schwaner)
- Kayanic
- Malayic (Malay, Indonesian, Iban, Minangkabau, Banjar, Kendayan, etc.)
- Land Dayak (in some versions)
- And languages outside Borneo: Chamic, Sundanese, Rejang, Moken, etc.

Important caveat: later work by Smith has treated Greater North Borneo more cautiously as a zone of lexical diffusion rather than a strict genetic node in every case. The evidence is primarily lexical, not phonological.

### Distinctive linguistic features of the area

- **Extreme phonological innovation** — Northern Sarawak languages (especially Kiput and Berawan) are famous for highly unusual sound changes, including intervocalic devoicing, low-vowel fronting conditioned by voiced obstruents, and the development of verbal ablaut.
- **Verbal ablaut** — Widespread in North Sarawak. It developed from the older Austronesian infixes *-um-* and *-in-* under specific phonological conditions (Blust 1997). Kiput is a clear example.
- **Relatively modest morphology** — Most languages in the area have simpler affix systems than Philippine-type languages. Complexity is often closer to Malay.
- **Heavy Malay influence** — Especially in coastal and lower-river communities. Many languages show substantial Malay loanwords and, in some cases, structural influence.

### Relevance to the translation project

| Unit | Relationship to Malayic | Practical notes for Region 1 |
|------|-------------------------|------------------------------|
| **Malayic proper** (Iban, Banjar, Minangkabau, Kendayan, etc.) | Core of the cluster | Highest transferability of vocabulary and structures. |
| **Lower Baram** (Kiput, Belait, Miriek, etc.) | Sister group inside North Borneo / GNB | Morphologically manageable; ablaut is the main new feature. Limited published materials outside Blust. |
| **Dayic / Kenyah** | More distant within North Sarawak | Useful as secondary controls; more independent development. |
| **Sabah languages** (Dusunic, Murutic) | North Borneo | Outside the current Malaysian Sea focus but part of the same larger picture. |

### Key references

- Blust (1991, 2010) — North Sarawak and Greater North Borneo hypotheses
- Blust (1997) — Ablaut in Northwest Borneo
- Blust (2002, 2003) — Kiput phonology and morphology
- Smith (2017) — Comprehensive classification of Borneo languages
- Lobel (2016) — *North Borneo Sourcebook* (large comparative wordlists for Sabah and adjacent languages)

**Bottom line**: Northwest Borneo is not a single tight family, but it is a coherent research area with clear internal subgroups. For the work already underway, the Malayic languages remain the highest-leverage cluster. Kiput and the other Lower Baram languages sit one level further out — genetically related, morphologically similar in overall complexity, but with their own distinctive phonological and ablaut patterns that need to be handled separately.

## Document 7 — Kiput vs. Berawan ablaut

Both languages belong to the Berawan–Lower Baram branch of North Sarawak and inherited the same historical source of verbal ablaut (reduction of the older infixes *-um-* and *-in-* on stems with penultimate schwa). The systems remain recognizably the same, but they have diverged in detail.

### Shared core

| Feature | Kiput | Berawan | Notes |
|---------|-------|---------|-------|
| Historical origin | *-um-* / *-in-* on *CəCVC stems | Same | Shared North Sarawak innovation (Blust 1997) |
| Basic pattern | ə (base) : u (active) : i (passive) | ə (base) : u (active) : i (passive) | Same three-way alternation |
| Trigger | Penultimate schwa | Penultimate schwa | Same phonological condition |
| Coexistence with infixes | Yes (*-em-*, *-en-*, *ŋ-*) | Yes (*ng-*, *-en-*, etc.) | Both keep residual infix marking alongside ablaut |
| Passive often perfective | Yes | Yes (typical in the group) | Shared tendency |

### Documented Berawan examples

From recent descriptions of Berawan lects (Long Teru, Batu Belah, Long Jegan, Long Terawan):

- Base with penultimate schwa → passive formed by replacing the schwa with **i**:
  *d-i-kkep* / *d-i-kkiep* / *d-i-kkâ′* "was held by someone"

This matches the Kiput passive *i*-ablaut exactly (cf. Kiput *gitep*, *isie*, *n-i-put*).

### Points of difference

| Aspect | Kiput | Berawan | Practical note |
|--------|-------|---------|----------------|
| Clarity of the pattern | Very clear and regularly illustrated in Blust's data | Present, but less fully tabulated in published sources | Kiput is currently the better-documented of the two for ablaut |
| Interaction with other changes | Relatively straightforward once schwa class is identified | Interacts with Berawan's heavy consonant lengthening, gemination, and extreme sound changes (*b > k* intervocalically, etc.) | Berawan surface forms are often more obscured |
| Compound ablaut | Mainly simple ablaut | Also shows compound ablaut in some forms (nasal substitution + vowel change) | Berawan leans more toward the compound type in places |
| Overall phonological opacity | High (unusual sound changes), but ablaut itself remains transparent | Even higher; many cognates are heavily disguised | Ablaut is one of the more stable morphological signals in both |

### Summary

- The **historical mechanism and the basic ə / u / i alternation are the same**.
- Kiput preserves the pattern more transparently in the published record.
- Berawan has the same system but embeds it inside a phonology that has undergone even more radical changes (especially intervocalic devoicing and gemination), so the ablaut is sometimes harder to see on the surface.
- Both languages still use residual infix marking (*-em-*/*-en-* or their descendants) in complementary distribution with ablaut.

For translation or glossary work, the practical rule is identical in both languages: if a stem has (or historically had) penultimate schwa, expect the active form to show **u** and the passive/perfective form to show **i**. The difference is mainly one of surface opacity, not of underlying structure.

## Document 8 — North Sarawak languages

North Sarawak is a well-established subgroup of Austronesian languages spoken mainly in northern Sarawak (Malaysia), with some extension into adjacent Brunei and Indonesian Kalimantan. It was first proposed by Robert Blust (1969, 1974, 1991, 2010) and is now standard in classifications of Borneo languages (including Smith 2017).

### Defining innovation

The subgroup is defined by one highly distinctive phonological change: the split of the Proto-Austronesian voiced stops into two series:

- A plain voiced series (*b, *d, *j, *g)
- A second "phonetically complex" series (voiced aspirates *bʰ, *dʰ, *jʰ, *gʰ, or implosives in some daughter languages)

This second series is rare typologically. Its presence (or clear historical traces) is the main diagnostic for North Sarawak membership. Other widespread features in the group include low-vowel fronting after voiced obstruents and the development of verbal ablaut from the older infixes *-um-* and *-in-*.

### Internal classification

Current consensus (Blust + Smith 2017 refinements) recognizes four primary branches:

| Branch | Main languages / groups | Notes |
|--------|-------------------------|-------|
| **Berawan–Lower Baram** | Berawan (Long Terawan, Batu Belah, Long Teru, Long Jegan), Kiput, Belait, Narum, Miriek/Miri, Tutong, Lelak (extinct) | Closest relatives of Kiput. Heavy phonological innovation. |
| **Dayic** (Apo Duat) | Kelabit (Bario and other dialects), Lun Bawang / Lun Dayeh, Sa'ban, Tring | Best-known for retaining true voiced aspirates (Kelabit). |
| **Kenyah** | Highland Kenyah and Lowland Kenyah (including many Penan varieties) | Large dialect continuum in central Borneo. |
| **Bintulu** | Bintulu | Position somewhat isolated within the subgroup. |

(The Melanau–Kajang languages were once sometimes included but are now generally placed outside North Sarawak, in a Central Sarawak grouping.)

### Geographic and speaker notes

- Core area: northern Sarawak, especially the Baram River basin and its tributaries (Tutoh, Tinjar), plus the highlands of the Apo Duat range.
- Most languages have small speaker populations (hundreds to a few thousand). Several are endangered.
- Kiput (~450 speakers) and the Berawan dialects (~3,000–4,000 total) sit inside the Berawan–Lower Baram branch.

### Distinctive linguistic traits of the group

- **Voiced aspirates / implosives** — the defining innovation; best preserved in Kelabit.
- **Verbal ablaut** — ə / u / i alternation on stems with historical penultimate schwa (shared by Kiput, Berawan, and others).
- **Low-vowel fronting** — *a* often fronts after a voiced obstruent.
- **Extreme phonological change** — especially in Berawan and Kiput (intervocalic devoicing, unusual consonant shifts, heavy reduction).
- Relatively modest morphology compared with Philippine-type languages; overall complexity closer to Malay.

### Relation to the broader picture

North Sarawak sits inside Blust's larger **North Borneo** proposal (together with the languages of Sabah) and, more broadly, inside the **Greater North Borneo** hypothesis (which also includes Malayic). The evidence for Greater North Borneo is primarily lexical; the evidence for North Sarawak itself is phonological and stronger.

### Practical relevance

For work focused on the "Malaysian Sea" / Region 1 languages, the most immediately relevant part of North Sarawak is the **Berawan–Lower Baram** branch (Kiput, Belait, Miriek, Narum, etc.). These languages share the ablaut system already examined and are the closest non-Malayic relatives in the area. Dayic and Kenyah are more distant within the same subgroup and show more independent development.

**Key references**: Blust (1969, 1974, 1991, 2010, 1997, 2002, 2006); Smith (2017).
