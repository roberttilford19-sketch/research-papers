# BASE HUNT 2 — KIPUT (KYI) — THE WIDER HUNT, 2026-11-17

**Commission:** Robert Tilford's direction, evening of 2026-11-17, after the wicket wave stopped the Kiput lane on the thinness of Blust's PL-546 (931 items, no attestation for God, heaven, hell, angel, prayer, mercy, prophet, Lord or soul — see `WICKET-RECORD-KYI.md` in this folder). Robert's reasoning, in his words: a living language has materials somewhere — "there's gotta be a dictionary somewhere… second grade, third grade, fourth grade class — can we get some of those materials?" This hunt went looking, in the five areas he named: Malaysian school materials, Catholic mission materials from Long Kiput / Kuala Tutoh and the Diocese of Miri, SIL publications, the union catalogues and archives, and the current speaker and vitality record.

**Verdict, stated first: still not found.** No reachable source beyond PL-546 attests the sacred vocabulary in Kiput's own usage, and no second Kiput lexicon, text collection, schoolbook, hymnal or Bible portion exists in any catalogue this hunt searched. The God-word remains unattested in every published Kiput source there is. The base is therefore **not sufficient to re-serve the lane to Robert as a decision**. What the hunt did establish — and this is the honest difference from the first stop — is *why*: the absence is not a gap in the desk's searching. It is a documented fact about the language's written history, set out below.

---

## 1. The catalogue of record says the language is unwritten

The single most decisive find is Ethnologue's current entry for Kiput (kyi), the catalogue the house already treats as its reference of record. Under "Language Development" it lists exactly two items: **"Dictionary. Grammar."** Both of those are PL-546 itself — the one book the desk already holds. Under "Writing" it gives one word: **"Unwritten."**

That one word answers Robert's schoolbook question directly. Kiput has never been a language of schooling. It does not appear in Malaysia's mother-tongue (Pupils' Own Language) teaching programme, Dewan Bahasa dan Pustaka has published no Kiput title that any catalogue records, and no Kiput primer, reader or textbook at any grade was found in the DBP, Kementerian Pendidikan, WorldCat, OLAC or web searches run for this hunt — in English or in Malay. The grade-school materials Robert pictured do not exist, not because they are hidden, but because Kiput children are schooled in Malay (and use Iban widely), as Sarawak children of small language communities generally are. The Jamaican lesson — that a living language has print somewhere — holds where a language has a print tradition. Kiput's tradition is oral, and the catalogues say so plainly.

## 2. The complete published bibliography of Kiput is five items long

Glottolog 5.4, the scholarly bibliography of record, lists **exactly five references** for Kiput in the whole of the literature:

1. **Blust 2003 — PL-546**, *A short morphology, phonology and vocabulary of Kiput, Sarawak* (931 items). Already in hand.
2. **Blust 2002 — "Kiput Historical Phonology"**, *Oceanic Linguistics* 41(2). A phonology paper built on the same 1971 data; it carries no lexicon beyond PL-546. LIBRARY-ONLY (journal holdings; subscription).
3. **Ray 1913 — "The Languages of Borneo"**, *Sarawak Museum Journal* 1. Contains the first Long Kiput wordlist. Blust's own preface to PL-546 measures it: "a vocabulary of a little over 200 items in a phonemically inadequate transcription," and states that PL-546 is "considerably longer and more accurate." Ray is therefore a smaller, superseded subset of what the desk already holds. LIBRARY-ONLY (research-library holdings of the 1913 journal).
4. **Ethnologue, 18th edition (2015)** — a comparative catalogue entry, not a Kiput text.
5. **Blust 1971 — fieldnotes on the languages of northern Sarawak** — the raw material of items 1 and 2 (see §4 below).

WorldCat tells the same story from the library side: the only Kiput-language book record in the union catalogue is PL-546 (OCLC 54984089); Open Library and Japan's CiNii hold the same single title and no other. OLAC, the federated language-archive catalogue, lists for Kiput only PL-546, the Glottolog entry, the Ethnologue entry, and Blust's 1971 deposits. SEAlang holds no Kiput dictionary. There is no second dictionary, wordlist or text collection beyond PL-546 anywhere in the catalogues. The dictionary Robert was sure must exist does exist — exactly one, and the desk already has it.

One caution for future searches, recorded so nobody chases it: the Diocese of Miri's communities page describes a neighbouring Baram people — the Kejaman of Long Liten and Long Segaham — who "produced a picture dictionary to preserve their dying language." That dictionary is Kejaman, not Kiput. No Kiput picture dictionary exists in any source found.

## 3. The mission shelf: worship in Kiput is real; Kiput print is not found

The Catholic mission at Kuala Tutoh is old and well documented. The Diocese of Miri's own history (rcdmiri.org) records that the Kiput of Kuala Tutoh celebrated the **106th anniversary of the Catholic mission in December 2017** — a Mill Hill mission reaching back to about 1911, planted by Fr. Henry Jansen's generation, in a community that is still largely Catholic, with a 72-door longhouse at Kuala Tutoh; roughly a third of the Kiput converted to Islam decades ago and live at Kampung Benawa and Lubok Nibong. The diocese's communities page profiles the Kiput ("Lakiput," self-name *Lepo Pu'un*, "the earlier tribe") at about 2,000 people.

And the worship itself is real. A peer-reviewed ethnographic study of the two Kiput settlements published in March 2026 (*Tamaddun* journal: "Sociocultural Perspective Among the Kiput of Baram") records that in Christian Kuala Tutoh, "religious practice centred on Sunday worship services conducted in the Kiput language, with Christian hymns and liturgy representing significant contexts for Kiput language use." So Kiput is sung and prayed aloud every Sunday. Robert's instinct — a Catholic community of a century's standing must handle the sacred words somehow — is right about the practice.

But no printed Kiput mission material of any kind was found: no Bible portion, lectionary, hymnal, catechism or prayer book in Kiput appears in WorldCat, OLAC, the Bible Society of Malaysia's orbit, the United Bible Societies' channels, Scripture Earth (which has no Kiput page), Find.Bible, or the Diocese of Miri's own site. The Bible catalogues are unanimous and specific: Joshua Project's language record for kyi gives Bible Portions **"None Reported,"** New Testament "None Reported," Complete Bible "None Reported," and **"No reported resources in this language"**; its people-group profile adds Jesus Film: No, Audio Recordings: No, Ministry Resources: No. Global Recordings Network holds no Kiput recording. SIL — which works in Sarawak on Bidayuh, Kelabit, Bonggi and other languages — has published nothing on Kiput at all; no SIL Kiput item exists in OLAC or SIL's own channels.

Two honest qualifications ride with that finding. First, Joshua Project carries Kiput's scripture status as "Translation Started" (status 2 on its scale). That flag is the weakest on their scale and is attached to nothing: no portion, no agency, no year, no text is named for it anywhere, and every resource column beside it reads none. It cannot be counted as a Bible portion, and no portion answering the wicket record's reopening condition — "a Kiput Bible portion or lectionary from the largely Catholic Kiput community" — can be produced from any source this hunt could reach. Second, what is sung at Kuala Tutoh may rest on locally made, uncatalogued parish material — a typed hymn sheet or a catechist's notebook would never reach WorldCat. If any Kiput sacred print exists on Earth, that is where it would be. Reaching it would mean contacting the parish or the diocese, which is outreach — outside this hunt's fences, and a step only Robert can direct.

How the community in fact operates, to answer Robert's question directly: bilingually. Ethnologue records Kiput speakers as also using **Iban** — the church and trade language of the Baram — and Sarawak Malay serves school and official life. The printed books in the Kuala Tutoh longhouse church are, on all the evidence available, Iban and Malay books; Kiput carries the singing and the praying by voice.

## 4. The one new digital find is Blust's own 1971 notebook — the same well, not a second one

The hunt did surface one Kiput holding the first pass had not named: **Blust's 1971 field notebook, digitised at the Kaipuleohone archive, University of Hawai'i (ScholarSpace item RB2-003-B, handle 10125/33160)**, freely downloadable as page scans, in which pages 61–81 are the Kiput section — word lists, phonetic transcriptions, kinship terms, morphology, taken down from John Malang, the same 17-year-old principal speaker named in PL-546. OLAC also lists a 1971 Blust audio recording from the same fieldwork at PARADISEC (RB1-003).

Access class: IN HAND DIGITALLY. But its value must be stated plainly: this is the raw notebook from which PL-546's 931 items were distilled. It is the same 20–25 hours of elicitation, from the same speakers, in 1971. Blust read all of it, published its vocabulary, and published no God-word, no heaven, no prayer, no mercy — because the elicitation never reached that register, not because he trimmed it. The notebook cannot settle what its own published distillation could not. It deepens the desk's copy of the base; it does not widen the base.

## 5. Speakers and vitality — Robert's "are there no living speakers no more?"

There are, and the community is active — the language is small and threatened, not gone.

- **Speakers: about 450** (Blust 2002, carried in Ethnologue's current entry and the Malay Wikipedia). Ethnologue's language-use note: "Some young people, all adults" speak it; location given as Benawa and Kuala Tutoh villages south of Marudi, on the Baram. The ethnic community is larger than the speaker count: about **2,000** Kiput people (2000 census figure carried in the reference literature), with Joshua Project's people-group population at 1,400.
- **Status: threatened and shifting.** Ethnologue grades Kiput **EGIDS 6b (Threatened)**; Glottolog's endangerment assessment is "shifting"; the Endangered Languages Project lists it as Threatened/Endangered. The 2026 ethnography documents the pressure directly: market forces and limited opportunity "constrain intergenerational transmission, especially of the Kiput language," and the community itself is pushing back — a formal community resolution on the Kiput name in 2024, the *Adiit Mucu'ut Kiput* heritage festival in 2025, and work to codify Kiput customary law (written, so far as any source shows, in Malay).
- **Where it lives:** the home and longhouse at Kuala Tutoh (Christian) and Kampung Benawa and Lubok Nibong (Muslim), and — per the 2026 study — Sunday worship at Kuala Tutoh. Not in school, not in print, not in media.

So the picture is a living language of a few hundred speakers whose entire written record, worldwide, is one linguist's 1971 notebook and the 102-page book he made from it thirty years later.

## 6. Access classes, gathered

- **IN HAND DIGITALLY:** PL-546 (held since the wicket stop); Blust's 1971 field-notebook scans, Kaipuleohone RB2-003-B (free download) — same data as PL-546, no new attestation; Blust 1971 recording catalogued at PARADISEC (RB1-003) — audio of the same elicitation.
- **PARTIAL:** none that adds sacred vocabulary. The Joshua Project "Translation Started" flag is a status without a text — recorded here so it is never mistaken for a portion.
- **LIBRARY-ONLY:** Ray 1913, *Sarawak Museum Journal* 1 (~200 items, superseded by PL-546); Blust 2002, *Oceanic Linguistics* 41(2) (phonology of the same data).
- **PURCHASE-ONLY:** nothing that changes the position — PL-546's print is offered secondhand (e.g. Masalai Press via AbeBooks), but the desk already holds the book digitally in full.
- **NOT FOUND:** DBP / Kementerian Pendidikan school materials in Kiput at any grade; any Dewan Bahasa dan Pustaka Kiput title; Diocese of Miri Kiput publications of any kind; a Kiput Bible portion, lectionary, hymnal, catechism or prayer book in any catalogue; SIL Malaysia / SIL International Kiput publications; a SEAlang Kiput dictionary; Scripture Earth, GRN, YouVersion or Faith Comes By Hearing Kiput content; any dictionary, wordlist or text collection beyond PL-546 in WorldCat, OLAC, Glottolog or archive.org.

## 7. The test that matters, answered

The commission's test was whether any reachable source attests the sacred vocabulary — the God-word above all — in Kiput's own usage. The answer is no, and it is now a searched no rather than an assumed one: five catalogue systems (Glottolog, WorldCat, OLAC, Ethnologue, SEAlang), the Bible-distribution record (Bible Society of Malaysia's orbit, UBS channels, Scripture Earth, Joshua Project, GRN), the diocese's own publications, the Malaysian education publishers, and the digital archives (Kaipuleohone, PARADISEC, archive.org) were all checked, and the sacred register appears in none of them. The only Kiput "spirit" word on record anywhere remains PL-546's *akaaŋ*, 'ghost, spirit of the dead' — a word for the dead, not a name for God.

**Closing verdict: the base is still not sufficient. The lane should not be re-served to Robert as a build decision, because there is nothing new to build from.** The wicket's reopening condition — a fuller Kiput source, above all a community Bible portion — was hunted directly and does not exist in print anywhere reachable. The two roads that could ever change this are both outside desk acquisition: unpublished parish material at Kuala Tutoh, reachable only by outreach to the community or the Diocese of Miri (fenced; Robert's call alone, and under the house's Quiet Protocol posture for small communities, a call to make slowly if at all), or new linguistic fieldwork, which is not the desk's to commission. The honest shelf entry for Kiput stays what the wicket record wrote: a living language, a stopped lane, and the reason on the record.
