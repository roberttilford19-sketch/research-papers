# The Moscow Rules

## The Mendezes, the City, and the Code That Came Out of Both

*Awen by Muse by Meta*

---

## Introduction — A Card in Hays, Kansas

A man in Hays, Kansas, keeps a small card from the International Spy Museum among his things. On it are ten rules, once the survival law of American intelligence officers working in Moscow at the height of the Cold War. He did not keep the card as a souvenir. He kept it because he recognized it.

A card, though, is not a story. Behind those ten short sentences stand two people — a Kansas-raised photographer and a Colorado artist — who between them gave more than fifty years to the Central Intelligence Agency, much of it spent changing other people's faces so those people could walk through the most watched city on earth and come home alive. Behind the people stands the city itself: Moscow under the KGB, a place the CIA formally classified as a "denied area," where the surveillance was so complete that an unwritten code of conduct had to evolve, officer by officer and year by year, simply to keep operations — and the Russians who risked everything to help — alive. And behind the city stands a book, published in 2019, in which the code was finally written down in full by the two people best entitled to write it.

What follows is that whole story: the people, the setting, the rules themselves — carried, wherever the record allows, by the stories Jonna Mendez herself tells around them, in her own words — the book and its reception, and, at the end, in a section plainly marked as his own, the account of the man in Hays who carried the rules for years and tried to live by them when nobody would teach him anything at all.

One thing about those stories should be said at the start, because this edition is built on it. Since the book appeared, Jonna Mendez has carried it from room to room — a Commonwealth Club stage in San Francisco, the International Spy Museum in Washington, a public-radio studio in Connecticut, an interviewer's questions in San Diego. The spine of the talk holds from room to room: the same life, the same city, the same rules. But no two tellings are identical. Each room draws something out of her that the others do not have — a name the other versions leave out, a doll bought in Bangkok, a warning about a broom closet in the White House — and those variants are not decoration. Again and again, the detail that appears in exactly one telling is the detail that carries the reason a rule existed: why it was written in the first place, and what it cost when it was not followed. This edition therefore lays her tellings side by side. Where a story or a detail stands in only one of them, the text says so, at the place where it stands — hers, from that telling alone.

## Part One — The People

### Jonna Mendez

Jonna Mendez was born Jonna Hiestand in 1945 in Campbellsville, Kentucky, the second of four girls. Her father was an airplane mechanic, and his work moved the family to Wichita, Kansas, where she graduated from high school in 1963 and attended Wichita State University. She then went to Frankfurt, West Germany, and worked for Chase Manhattan Bank. There she met John Goeser, a newly recruited CIA officer; they married in 1966, and he persuaded her to take employment with the agency so that his tours abroad would not separate them.

She has never dressed that entrance up. Asked on stage in San Francisco how she came to the agency, she said: "I kind of wandered in a side door." And of the Kansas she came from: "I left Kansas with this keen burning desire to be a spy. I did not." She went to Europe, bumped into a community of spies she took for soldiers, married into the agency, and did pickup jobs. What kept her, she says, was the chance to protect the people who were working for the United States at mortal risk — and the discovery that the work mattered.

She opened her talk at the International Spy Museum, in that same season of telling, as a Wichita woman far from home, looking for her ruby slippers — the line is that telling's alone, and it is the truest short biography she ever gave.

So one of the most consequential technical officers of the late Cold War entered the CIA as a "CIA wife," working primarily as a secretary, accompanying her husband on tours in Europe, the Far East, and the Subcontinent. The agency's good fortune was that she watched everything. Reassigned to the Office of Technical Service in the early 1970s — the office the agency itself compares to Q Branch in the James Bond films — she found the secretarial duties too small for her and applied for a position as a technical operations officer, specializing in clandestine photography. The work put her in rooms with the CIA's most highly placed foreign assets, in some of the least hospitable countries in the world, teaching them to use subminiature spy cameras and processing the intelligence those cameras gathered. She has said of certain meetings, on the Indian subcontinent and in Bogotá, Colombia, that she was unsure whether she would leave them alive.

The toughness was trained, not assumed. She tells — only in the San Diego Metro interview of 2024 — of the agency's hostile-interrogation course, in which the instructors, told she was claustrophobic, sealed her into a box eight inches deep where she could not turn. What carried her through it was a Vietnam prisoner's practice — every prisoner had a project, a place to go in his head — and where she went was home: "I went back to Kansas, where I'm from." It got her through the exercise, she says. "It was excruciating."

In 1982, blocked from further promotion in her current line — the record states plainly that interagency resistance to professional women was part of the wall — she transferred to the CIA's disguise laboratory and retrained as a disguise technician, traveling constantly and working directly with officers and assets in the field, down to procuring the disguise materials herself. In 1986 she was promoted into denied-area operations, the small unit of experienced officers and technicians who oversaw agents in the most heavily surveilled countries on earth, the Soviet Union and Cuba among them; her performance there was rated exceptional, and the same year she became Deputy Chief of the disguise operation. When the disguise laboratory was made a full division of the agency, she became its first female chief — the Chief of Disguise — responsible for a worldwide staff and a multimillion-dollar program creating identity transformations for CIA officers and for the foreign assets whose lives depended on them. Among her signature projects was the development of a prosthetic mask, building on the pioneering work of CIA contractor John Chambers, the Hollywood makeup artist, that an officer could apply in seconds and wear through a surveillance gauntlet. With few women on the staff, she often served as the model when female masks were field-tested.

Her authority over this material was demonstrated once, memorably, at the very top of the government, when she took a new mask to the White House and briefed a president while wearing another woman's face. That demonstration is told in full where it belongs — in the rules section of this edition, among the craft that made the rules executable — because it is the proof of everything the disguise shop claimed.

She retired in 1993, after twenty-seven years of service, at the rank of GS-14, with the CIA's Intelligence Commendation Medal. Retirement, in her case, meant a change of theater rather than an end of work: she continued as a fine art photographer — photography had been her first craft inside the agency and remained her own outside it — and as a lecturer, consultant, and author. She is a founding board member of the International Spy Museum in Washington, where several of her masks are displayed. In March 2024 she published her memoir, *In True Face: A Woman's Life in the CIA, Unmasked*. In June 2023 she received the Daughters of the American Revolution's Patriot Award. The arc of the career is worth pausing on, because it is the arc of the rules themselves: the agency hired her to type, and she left having run the shop that decided what its officers' faces would be in the one city where a wrong face was a death sentence — sometimes the officer's, more often the asset's.

### Tony Mendez

Antonio Joseph Mendez was born on November 15, 1940, in Eureka, Nevada, to John George Mendez, of Mexican descent, and Neva June Mendez. His father died when he was young, and he grew up largely cut off from his father's Mexican-American culture. When he was twelve the family moved to Colorado; he graduated from Englewood High School and studied art at the University of Colorado, then worked as an illustrator and tool designer for the aerospace firm Martin Marietta, supporting a young family and taking commissions — among them two large oil paintings of nineteenth-century Denver streetscapes for a Denver hotel in 1964.

In 1965 he answered a blind help-wanted advertisement — "Artists to Work Overseas — U.S. Navy Civilians" — and found himself interviewed in a dingy motel room and handed a recruitment guide for the Central Intelligence Agency's Technical Services Division. The agency wanted his eye and his hand. He became an espionage artist: a forger of identity documents so skilled that he could reproduce anything he saw, and then a maker of disguises, whose talent for transformation he had discovered as a schoolboy in Englewood, when, neither he nor his friend having dates for a dance, he dressed as a girl and attended as the friend's sweetheart — "my first cover operation," he liked to call it. He served in South Asia, Southeast Asia, and the Middle East, rose to Chief of Disguise and later Chief of the Graphics and Authentication Division, and became the agency's master of exfiltration — the art of getting people out of countries that intended to keep or kill them. During the Vietnam era he once moved an Asian diplomat and a Black CIA officer out of danger disguised as two Caucasian businessmen.

The operation that made his name public came in January 1980. When Iranian militants overran the United States embassy in Tehran in November 1979, six American diplomats escaped the compound and took refuge with Canadian diplomats. Mendez designed their way out: the six would leave as Canadians — a film crew scouting locations for a science-fiction picture called *Argo* that did not exist. The Canadian government supplied genuine passports; Hollywood supplied the convincing apparatus of a production company; Mendez flew into Tehran, rehearsed the six in their new identities, and on January 27, 1980, walked them through Mehrabad Airport and onto a Swissair flight to Zurich. Not a shot was fired; nobody was harmed; the concealment existed entirely to deliver six people home. On March 12, 1980, he received the Intelligence Star for valor for the mission the world now calls the Canadian Caper. The operation stayed classified until 1997, when the CIA declassified it on the agency's fiftieth anniversary and named Mendez one of fifty officers awarded the Trailblazer Medallion, as an officer who "helped shape the history of the CIA." In 2012 his book about the operation became Ben Affleck's film *Argo*, which won the Academy Award for Best Picture. His own summary of his life, repeated so often it became his epitaph, was: "I've always considered myself to be an artist first, and for 25 years I was a pretty good spy."

He retired in 1990 as a Senior Intelligence Service officer, after twenty-five years, holding the Intelligence Medal of Merit and two Certificates of Distinction alongside the Intelligence Star. Then the artist resumed full possession of the life. On some forty acres in the Blue Ridge foothills near Knoxville, Maryland, he and Jonna built a compound of studios and a gallery in and around a red carriage house: he painted — Maryland landscapes, Antietam Creek, the Potomac, cherry blossoms at the Tidal Basin — Jonna shot photographs, and his son Toby sculpted, the three of them famously leaving one another alone while they worked. He wrote his memoirs: *The Master of Disguise* in 1999, *Spy Dust* with Jonna, *Argo* in 2012, and finally *The Moscow Rules*. Parkinson's disease took his hands' steadiness before it took anything else — the cruelest theft, for a forger and painter — and he died on January 19, 2019, at seventy-eight, painting nearly to the end and surrounded by his family. *The Moscow Rules* was published four months after his death.

### The Two of Them Together

They met inside the work. Jonna, then Jonna Goeser, was a technical operations officer; Tony Mendez was one of the senior disguise and forgery officers of the Technical Services world she had entered, and the public record places their meeting while she was assigned to Bangkok; between their postings they spent years working the same Asian theater, in the same small fraternity of people who made false faces and true documents. Both were married to other people when they met, and both marriages belonged to their earlier lives. After Tony retired in 1990, they married, in 1991, and had a son together. Between them they had devoted more than fifty years to the agency.

Retirement nearly killed the men of their office — Jonna tells audiences that in their men's organization, the average lifespan after retirement was eighteen months, because the work had been the whole of their lives. Tony, as she quotes him, used to say that going to work was like drinking from a firehose, and "retiring was like jumping off a moving train." The Mendezes survived it because they had two very active lives outside the agency's walls, and because they built a second joint career of disclosure — carefully bounded, agency-authorized disclosure, but disclosure all the same. They wrote together: *Spy Dust*, their joint account of the Moscow years; *Argo*; and *The Moscow Rules*, the book this edition takes as its canon. They lectured together to the intelligence community and the public, took part in more than two dozen television documentaries, and served as founding board members of the International Spy Museum, helping to plan and design the institution whose wall now carries the ten rules on a card small enough to keep in a pocket in Hays, Kansas. The pattern of the partnership matters for everything that follows in this edition: the spy's whole art is concealment, and these two spent their last decades practicing its opposite — telling, on the record and under their own names, what the concealed work had actually been, so that the record would belong to the people who had done it.

## Part Two — The Setting: Moscow, the Denied Area

To understand why the rules exist at all, a reader has to understand what Moscow was to the Central Intelligence Agency. In the agency's own classification, carried into the Mendezes' book, Moscow was a *denied area*: "a country with such strict internal security that foreign intelligence agents dare not contact informants in person." Former KGB general Oleg Kalugin put the scale of the opposition in a figure the book repeats: during the 1970s there were more than fifty thousand KGB officers in Moscow alone.

What the Americans were doing inside that embrace, she states in her museum talk with a plainness that needs no embroidery: the mission was simple — collecting foreign intelligence for the country's policymakers, and learning the "plans and intentions" of its enemies. Everything in the pages that follow — the craft, the code, the cost — existed to keep that simple mission possible in a city arranged to make it impossible.

The surveillance was not occasional; it was architectural. The KGB's Seventh Chief Directorate was the surveillance arm, and it dedicated a team to follow each suspected American intelligence officer around the clock, on foot and in a rotating stable of cars. The teams did not merely follow — they studied. They built daily profiles of each officer's behavior patterns and demeanor, so that any deviation from the pattern was itself a signal worth reporting. The net reached into every corner of ordinary life: bartenders in the hard-currency bars and restaurants were on the Seventh Directorate's payroll, taxi drivers were their men, and, as Tony Mendez was briefed when he arrived, the woman shoveling snow and the man selling ice cream in Gorky Park were to be assumed connected to the apparatus. Apartments were bugged around the clock and the feed transmitted live; telephone lines were tapped; the embassy staff lived knowing that, as Mendez was told, "not only do the walls have ears — they also have beady, bloodshot eyes." Any break in an officer's cover behavior — running a red light, bolting across traffic, driving the wrong way down a one-way street — could bring additional surveillance teams marshaling onto him.

Jonna Mendez, describing that embrace to the audience at the International Spy Museum in June 2019, called surveillance in Moscow, against Americans in general, "a smothering embrace" — the phrase is her own, from that talk: bugs in the apartment walls, tails on the car, teams rotating so that they were with you wherever you went. The published report of the same talk records the working vocabulary the pressure produced. Officers survived by "working in the gaps" — the momentary breaks in the coverage, as when two right turns in a car could open just enough darkness for a passenger to slip out and a substitute to take his place — or, on the best nights, by "getting black": shaking surveillance entirely, usually only for moments, and usually only after hours of being led on the chase. And every false identity traveled with its paper trail in miniature, the corroborating clutter the trade calls pocket litter — the school identification, the notebook, the library card that made a cover story touchable.

Against that embrace, the agency's quietest weapon was a camera so small it is hard to credit. Jonna Mendez describes it in her Commonwealth Club conversation: a handmade film camera, built by one man in his garage, whose strip of film developed into fifty black dots, each one a page of text. In her museum telling — at this length in no other telling — the camera and its maker receive their names: the camera was the Tropel, the man was Paul Howell, and fifty or sixty of his cameras were in play. It rode in a lipstick that remained usable as lipstick, and it rode in a pen. Satellites could photograph missile fields, she grants, but "that little camera was collecting plans and intentions — what they were going to do, what was coming, what they're thinking about." The optical industry of two continents could not duplicate it; the agency tried every major maker in America and then the world, schematics in hand, and never found a second source. She tells of issuing those cameras on the far side of the world, running low, and then watching a local television clip of Cuban counterintelligence parading the equipment of twenty arrested double agents — and recognizing her own cameras on their shelves by their body numbers, a detail she gives in that telling alone. That was the technical war inside the denied area: intentions, carried out of meeting rooms one invisible page at a time, by an instrument no factory on earth could copy.

The reason a written doctrine became necessary is written in what the surveillance protected against, and in what it cost when it won. On the American side, an officer caught breaking cover faced expulsion and a ruined operation. On the Russian side, the stakes were absolute. Consider Adolf Tolkachev, a Soviet aviation engineer who for six years, from 1979 to 1985, photographed tens of thousands of pages of secret documents from his design bureau and passed them to the CIA — intelligence American analysts later valued in the billions of dollars of weapons research. In June 1985, a CIA case officer named Paul Stombaugh was arrested by the KGB in the middle of a surveillance-detection run, preparing for a meeting; Tolkachev, betrayed from inside the American intelligence community, was already in Lefortovo Prison. He was executed on September 24, 1986. The Mendezes write of that season in *The Moscow Rules* with the flatness of people reading a casualty list: "The majority of Soviet citizens working for us had been arrested and executed, most of them betrayed by Americans inside the intelligence community."

That is the environment in which the Moscow Rules were forged, and it explains their peculiar form. They were never issued. No one sat in a Langley office and composed them. As Tony Mendez wrote in *Spy Dust*: "Although no one had written them down, they were the precepts we all understood for conducting operations in the most difficult of operating environments: the Soviet capital. By the time they got to Moscow, everyone knew these rules. They were dead simple and full of common sense." The phrase itself traveled an odd road into the language: as the Mendezes note in the book, it was John le Carré's fictional spymaster George Smiley who first invoked the term "Moscow Rules" — fiction naming a reality that had not yet named itself — and the working code the fiction named kept evolving in practice through the 1960s, when American and British officers ran the Soviet agent Oleg Penkovsky through the Cuban Missile Crisis, and into the 1970s and 1980s, when a new generation of officers, and a new kind of technical officer — scientists, graphic artists, photographers, people like the Mendezes themselves — was trained specifically for the Soviet capital, and new techniques and disguise scenarios were perfected that would let an officer "go black": vanish from the Seventh Directorate's coverage entirely, if only for the few minutes a meeting required.

A doctrine learned that way — absorbed from briefings, from watching veterans, from mistakes that were sometimes fatal to other people — has a character no manual can reproduce. Every rule on the list is a scar. The section that follows gives the rules as Jonna Mendez herself carries them in her tellings: the craft beneath them, the ten the world knows, the fuller working code behind the ten, and the two rules the code grew afterward, when the scars were fresh.

## Part Three — The Rules, As She Tells Them

### A Code of Comportment

Jonna Mendez defines the rules more plainly than anyone else has. In the San Diego Metro interview of 2024, she said: "The Moscow rules were rules of comportment if you were operating in Moscow." Comportment — how you carried yourself, every hour, in a city where your behavior was the only text the opposition needed to read. The rules were not techniques bolted onto an officer's day; they were the day, conducted properly. That is why they lived unwritten for so long, and why, when Jonna Mendez tells them now, she does not recite them. She tells what happened, and the rule stands up out of the story — because that is how the station learned them in the first place.

### The Craft Beneath the Code

The rules could not have existed without the shop that made them executable, and the shop's work divided in two. What she calls traditional disguise was the surface kit — wigs, facial hair, glasses, fake tattoos, piercings — everything that can change how a person reads at a glance. The interesting part, she says, was advanced disguise: the full-face animated mask, built with chemists and with Hollywood. Tony Mendez had gone out to California and worked with John Chambers, the makeup artist whose ape masks for *Planet of the Apes* had fitted around the eyes and mouth and *moved* — the first masks the agency used were Hollywood stunt-double masks, cast from aluminum molds in three sizes, the large one a latex replica of Rex Harrison's face, which the shop could then finish as a South Indian, as an African American, as a woman, by the way the hair goods were applied. In the questions after her museum talk — that part of the evening stands in that telling alone — she adds the apprenticeship behind the connection: Chambers had taught Tony mask-making on a monster-film set, and Tony called him one of the few real geniuses he ever met. The field imposed its own requirements that Hollywood never had to meet. A latex mask in a humid climate, she explains, is "like being underwater" — the skin cannot breathe, perspiration has nowhere to go — and a mask an officer might have to remove in an alley had to collapse into nothing, so that "if you had to take it off and stick it in your armpit, it would do that." The agency's chemists went through material after material until they found ones that were comfortable, that wore well, and that would fold away to nothing.

The proof of that program — the day the craft stopped being a claim — came at the very top of the government, and she tells it in full in her Commonwealth Club conversation of June 2019. A new mask had just been finished, and because she was chief of disguise, she got the first one. The first version made her an African American man; it looked right, but she could not talk in it, and a briefing cannot be given in silence. She took it to the Director of Central Intelligence, William Webster. In her later telling of that meeting, in the San Diego Metro interview, Webster's verdict was immediate: "Oh my God, we have to go to the White House and show this to the president." She objected that she had no identification to match the face. His answer, in the same telling: "Oh, no. You're with me. You won't need any ID to get through the Secret Service." The safer course was chosen anyway: a second mask, a woman's — younger, prettier, a face she liked wearing. On public radio she adds the detail the other tellings leave out: the face was a real one, lent by a young colleague named Becky who was leaving the shop — "she gave me her face," hair and all, and Jonna wore it to the White House.

On the morning of the briefing she went to Webster's house in her own true face, and his little dog met her at the door barking, disliking her on sight. She went into the powder room and put the mask on. Officers had wondered whether dogs would reject a mask the way dogs reject hats. She came out, and the dog would not leave her alone. The first examiner had been passed.

At the White House she waited outside the Oval Office while a meeting ran long, wearing the first new anything — and, as she admits, "there's a little paranoia when you wear the first new anything." Then she went in, first to brief, before a ring of men around the President's desk: George H.W. Bush himself, Brent Scowcroft, John Sununu, Robert Gates, and Webster. She opened with photographs the shop had made of Bush in disguise during his own time as Director of Central Intelligence, and told him, "We've gotten a lot better" — she was there to show him the latest thing. He looked for the bag of tricks. There was no bag. In her San Diego telling, when she said she would remove the disguise and show it to him, the President stopped her — "Oh, don't remove it. Hold on" — and rose and walked around her, looking, as she puts it, for a seam, "but he was looking for an edge." He could not find one. "Okay, take it off," he said. She peeled the mask away in the gesture she has since made famous — "I did the Tom Cruise peel before Tom Cruise did" — and held the face up in her hand. Sununu, who had been bent over his notes preparing to speak next and paying no attention at all, nearly fell out of his chair; in her later telling, what escaped him was a little squeak, because the woman across the desk was suddenly holding what looked like a decapitated head. The President loved it.

A White House photographer had circled the room through the whole demonstration. Afterward, in the secretary's office, where Jonna was playing on the floor with the Bushes' dog Millie and her puppies, the photographer found her and demanded: what did you do, what was that? Her whole answer, as she tells it: "I can't tell you. It's classified." The public-radio telling alone preserves Tony's part in the morning: he had been in that room before her, and he warned her to fix in her mind the door she had entered by and leave by the same one, because the wrong door was a broom closet — and one of their own people, on just such a morning, had walked into it. The photograph itself took ten years to reach her, and her tellings differ on its fate — at the Commonwealth Club she says they never brushed the mask out of it, and that it hangs in their library; on public radio she says that when it finally came, they had airbrushed the mask out — and adds her own suspicion that the long wait was personal, the price of having answered the photographer as she did. Both versions stand in her tellings; the desk blends neither away.

Why does a mask demonstration belong inside the rules? Because the rules' hardest demand — step away from total surveillance and live — was impossible until the craft made it possible. In her own sentence from that same evening: nobody else built this capability, because nobody else needed it. "It was the only way we could step away from surveillance, if we could convincingly look like someone else." The ten rules are the conduct of officers for whom that sentence was true.

### The Ten, As She Tells Them

The International Spy Museum in Washington displays the rules as ten sentences, and it is those ten, on Robert's card, that the world knows. They are given here with the ground each one stands on — and wherever Jonna Mendez tells a story that carries a rule, the story leads and the rule follows, the way the station learned it.

**Assume nothing.** The book's own story begins in 1963, when a Soviet officer named Oleg Penkovsky stepped forward and offered to help the West — and the CIA discovered it had almost nothing with which to help him back: no equipment, no tradecraft, no way to keep him safe. They partnered with the British, ran him for eighteen months, and lost him to arrest and execution.

Her museum telling adds the approach itself, and the additions are that telling's alone. Penkovsky came to the embassy four times before anyone would take his package. The compromise that destroyed him she lays — as her stated belief, "we think" — at the door of George Blake. And she draws the consequence out to its full length: after Penkovsky, face-to-face meetings in Moscow stopped for about a decade, while the tools that could replace them were built. The museum telling carries the count and the decade; the Commonwealth telling carries the discipline the case taught. Each stands as she told it.

The discipline that case burned into the agency was the vetting of everyone who came after: the dangles, the double agents the Russians sent pretending to want to help, sent in fact to learn what America needed to know. Her account of it is procedural and absolute: "You have to check their bona fides. You have to make sure that they are who they say they are, and then you have to see that they give you actionable intelligence" — because a dangle will give you bits and pieces forever, and never anything actionable. The rule's pathology also has her telling: James Jesus Angleton, the chief of counterintelligence for twenty-some years, whose job was finding the dangles. "He ended up believing that everyone was a dangle," she says, and nearly shut down the Soviet operations entirely — assume-nothing curdled into assume-everything, which is the same failure wearing the other coat. Even Moscow's horror stories fell under the rule: the rumor that captured agents were fed feet-first into crematorium fires was repeated for years, and, she says, no one ever knew whether to believe it — they thought it a KGB-generated myth to keep their people from working with the West. The one assumption the station did permit ran the other way: assume the surveillance is with you everywhere outside the embassy's secured ground, and behave accordingly. On public radio she carries the same discipline to the embassy's own front door, in a detail that telling alone supplies: a man who walks in off the street offering information may be exactly what he claims — or may have come only to see which American shows up to debrief him, and to follow that American home and learn where he lives, whether he has children, how he drives to work. That is why every officer who passed through the shop left with something — a wig, a mustache, glasses, a small disguise that fit in a dopp kit — just in case.

**Never go against your gut** — and **don't look back; you are never completely alone.** Two of the ten live inside a single minute of her telling — a story she gives in full only in her Commonwealth Club conversation — in a Sheraton hotel on the subcontinent. A terrorist — "an honest-to-god terrorist, a really, really bad guy" — had telephoned the chief of station, a man he knew from somewhere else, offering intelligence about an American airliner to be brought down. The chief did not want to meet him alone, so seven of them went, and Jonna disguised every one, herself included — the chief of station above all, a tall, distinctive, really blonde American of six foot three, whom she put into a shalwar kameez and sandals and gave a cigar. Tony's maxim governed the work, and she quotes him on it still: "It's not the disguise. It's the way you wear it." The chief, a great actor, gave the terrorist the chance to be sized up before he could size up anyone else. Jonna took the overwatch position in a rug shop, looking through a glass wall across a corridor into a newsstand, counting knots in the rugs — looking, and looking, and looking. The gut is the thing that made her look up. In the newsstand stood the man himself, small, mean, flanked by two huge men with AK-47s, and she made eye contact with him. "That's like rule number one," she says, "don't look him in the eye." Interpol was after him; the local police were after him; his own organization was after him; he was fleeing for his life, and she was the countenance across the corridor. Her thought, told flatly all these years later, is the composure the rules were built to produce: "It won't be personal. At least if they shoot me, it won't be personal." They did not shoot. The tail you must not look back for and the watcher you are never without were the same fact in that corridor: everyone in it was being watched by someone, and the work went on inside the watching.

**Everyone is potentially under opposition control.** This sounds like suspicion and functions as its opposite. It says: the person across from you may be acting under compulsion you cannot see — handled, frightened, leveraged, owned — so his behavior may not be his. Judge the control, not the controlled. The agency studied motivation under its acronym — money, ideology, compromise, ego — and her summary of the postwar record is unsparing in both directions: the Americans who betrayed their country in her era did it almost exclusively for money, while the best of the Russians who worked for the United States did it for ideology, and the very best were never recruited at all. "We don't even recruit them," she says of those. "They volunteer." Against a state that owned its citizens' choices, the rule was a discipline of accuracy before it was anything else: treat every encounter as potentially scripted by the Seventh Directorate's masters, and you will neither trust wrongly nor blame wrongly.

**Go with the flow, blend in.** The KGB studied profiles; a man moving against the grain of the city's rhythms drew the eye the way a wrong note draws the ear. The disguise shop enforced the rule as a rite of passage. Officers were fitted for their disguises "just like getting a suit made," she says, and at the final fitting — because the shop knew the men, who mostly did not want to wear disguise at all, would otherwise leave it in a safe drawer — the fitter sent them out into the world in it: go to lunch, go to the 7-Eleven, buy me something. Everyone was the same the first time, certain the store would take him for a shoplifter. They came back converted. "He didn't know who I was," one reported of his own boss, sat beside at lunch. On public radio she tells of the officer who solved the problem from the other direction entirely: bald as an egg, he took to wearing his wig every day of his life, so that the disguise, when he needed one, was the bald head he left at home — "he was inhabiting his disguise," she says, with plain admiration. In her 2024 telling she states the shop's standing order plainly: whatever kind of disguise they gave people, "we insisted that they wear it in public." A disguise untested in the flow was not a disguise; it was a costume in a drawer.

---

**Vary your pattern and stay within your cover.** The surveillance built its profiles from routine, so routine was both shield and trap — and the shop's most famous answer to the trap began, as she tells it, with Tony Mendez sitting in a bar somewhere on the subcontinent, drinking Kingfisher beer and eating samosas, deciding that Moscow needed a diversion: a way to make surveillance believe there were still two people in a car when there was only one. The device became the jack-in-the-box — the JIM. Her museum telling gives it a birth certificate the other tellings do not carry: Tony sketched it on a napkin, in a café in India, over a beer and a samosa. It began at home with two engineers sent to Al's magic shop down by the White House to buy inflatable party dolls, a procurement that became its own small ordeal; the published report of her Spy Museum talk records that as the dolls kept freezing and shattering, the engineers had to keep going back to buy them in embarrassing quantities. The first models rode in briefcases, top halves only, inflated by gas canisters — and the gas came out so cold that when a prototype was sent behind the Iron Curtain, a case officer's wife practicing with it in her car hit the button and the whole thing blew up in the vehicle. On public radio she adds what the San Francisco telling leaves out: the dummy in that car had begun life as a doll someone purchased in Bangkok. The museum telling adds the failure's mechanics: the first air-filled prototypes froze and shattered in a test car, and it was that failure that put the scissor mechanism in the gas canister's place. And the museum telling alone gives the finished device its first Moscow triumph: its first use, on a Tolkachev meeting, produced — in her telling, and the figure is hers — a billion dollars of Pentagon savings. The two-for-one ran on foot as well as in cars, and the pedestrian version is also hers from the public-radio telling alone: you step into a newsstand, your double steps out and walks on down the street with the surveillance, and you are still inside — and because the watchers never knew it had happened, it could be done over and over. The magicians fixed what the engineers could not: on public radio she tells how the builders took the device away — a hundred and five pounds, a ridiculous mechanism — and returned it six months later as an elegant scissor mechanism that weighed almost nothing and could fit into almost anything. The finished JIM wore your blue coat, your tie, your wig, your very face from the mask shop, its head swiveling to talk to the driver; the choreography ran on two right turns — you bail out, the button is hit, and the watchers rounding the corner still see two people in the car. Its finest hour, in her telling, was pure Moscow theater: two couples driving out with a birthday cake on a wife's lap, having chattered about the party on the telephone all day because they knew the Soviets were listening. The men jumped out. The wife put the birthday cake on the seat and pushed the button, and, in her words, "the thing comes up out of a birthday cake." The pattern the watchers had been given all day — a birthday party, a cake, four people — was the cover; the variation was sewn inside it, never worn outside it.

**Lull them into a sense of complacency.** Surveillance is a human activity performed by human beings who grow bored. An officer who behaved with perfect, boring consistency taught his watchers to relax — and a relaxed surveillance was a surveillance that could be slipped, for the minutes an operation needed. The fuller code holds the paradox openly: an officer was to establish a distinctive, dynamic profile and stay consistent over time, to make sure the watchers could anticipate his destination, because predictability, manufactured and maintained, was itself a weapon. Complacency in the opposition was an asset the officer manufactured himself, with patience as the raw material — the birthday cake worked precisely because the pattern beneath it had been made, all day and for months before, completely uninteresting.

**Don't harass the opposition.** The Seventh Directorate's teams were professionals doing a job, and the doctrine forbade taunting, provoking, or humiliating them; harassment bought nothing and risked escalating a professional contest into a personal one. She hangs the rule on a single scene, and she hangs it there herself: her museum talk opens with it, as the book opens with it — June 6, 2016, a diplomat beaten at the doors of the American embassy in Moscow, his collarbones broken, medevaced out the next day; he never went back. The date and the broken collarbones are the museum telling's own. On public radio she gives the scene its grain: a grainy security video from outside the embassy, three in the morning, in which a man arriving by taxi — one of theirs — was attacked by a Russian who threw him down, got on top of him, and beat him bloody while the man worked his way on his back to the embassy's electronic doors and slid through them onto American soil, the Russian still beating him. The agency never acknowledged he was theirs. She showed the film to Tony. In the museum telling his verdict comes in substance — the man must have provoked him; he had broken the rule. On public radio she repeats the verdict as a scolding, in his own words: "You're not supposed to piss them off." The temperature of the game was not a courtesy. It was armor — for the officer at the door, and for everyone coming after him.

**Pick the time and place for action.** In Moscow the right act at the wrong hour was simply the wrong act, and the deadliest minutes of all were the meetings — which is why so much of Moscow craft existed to make meetings unnecessary. The dead drop was the answer: a pre-established site where an officer could leave a package the agent would collect an hour or a day later, under a choreography of signals, the two never in the same place at the same time. The package had to be something nobody else would pick up — innocuous, or disgusting. Her catalog of it is the trade at its most earthy: crushed cans covered with motor oil, the contents protected inside; fake bricks on construction sites, convincing enough that you could lose a couple that way; and the shop's favorite, dead rats, cleaned, fitted with Teflon closures, and treated against animal theft. "To make sure that an animal wouldn't pick up our dead rat," she says, "we would dip them in Tabasco" — and dry them on clotheslines, and keep them on the shelf, ready to be turned around. The grotesquerie is the doctrine: the time and place of the exchange were chosen by the officer, spread across hours, and armored in revulsion, because circumstance in Moscow was usually arranged by the other side.

**Keep your options open.** The final rule governs all the others. A burned option in Moscow was a grave — sometimes literally someone else's. The officer who cornered himself, who spent his last exit, who let a plan harden into a single road, had failed before he began. Every plan carried its own abandonment inside it, ready to be used — which is why the fuller code pairs this rule with its operational twin, the abort doctrine, and why the whole list can be read backward from here: every earlier rule exists to keep this one true.

### The Fuller Code

The ten on the wall are the code compressed for a museum card. In *The Moscow Rules*, the Mendezes set down the working code at the length officers actually carried it — some forty precepts in the book's full gathering, of which the ten are the skeleton.

Around the gut, the code doubles down: *always listen to your gut; it is your operational antenna* — and then states the operating condition that makes the antenna necessary: *Murphy is right.* Whatever can go wrong is presumed to be going wrong somewhere in the plan, and *technology will always let you down.* The shop that built the little camera and the animated mask also built a dummy that exploded in a colleague's car; its officers needed no persuading on this point. Around movement, the code refines the flow into technique: *go with the flow; use the terrain; take the natural break of traffic; maintain a natural pace.* Around knowledge it is blunt: *know the opposition and their terrain intimately* — the Seventh Directorate's habits, vehicles, rotations, and limits were studied as carefully as the KGB studied the Americans — and opportunity itself was rationed: *build in opportunity, but use it sparingly*, because every use of an opening taught the opposition that the opening existed.

The quick-change end of the craft had a name of its own — disguise on the run — and it survives in three renderings that do not match, which is itself instructive. On public radio she tells it as her son Jesse's, filmed on a New York street at lunch hour to prove that anybody could do it: a young man in a suit steps out of a building, pulls his velcroed tie and his sleeveless shirt-front away as he walks, shrugs off his jacket into a plastic bag, and keeps walking — black tank top, full sleeve tattoos, chunky watch, sunglasses, beanie — a different person inside a single block, the crowd itself part of the disguise. The published report of her museum talk renders the same demonstration with Tony performing it: a complete change of person in well under a minute, ending — as the report has it — as an old woman with a grocery cart, with Jonna watching. But in the museum talk itself she does something better than either: she reads Tony's own written account of the maneuver aloud, his text carried in her voice — the South Building hallway, forty-five seconds and forty-five steps, the attaché case becoming a grocery cart, the reversible coat turned as he walks, the businessman gone and a little old lady with a cart in his place. Three renderings, one doctrine: the change happens in motion, in the open, inside the flow of ordinary life, because that is where surveillance is least able to swear to what it saw.

The museum telling carries one more disguise on the run, worked at full operational scale and in Moscow itself, and it stands in that telling alone. A diplomat she names, Jim Olsen, walked into a Moscow forest in a three-piece suit and came out an old Russian man reeking of vodka, his tools hidden inside a foam-rubber book — and so disguised he went down a manhole on the ring road to reach the newly laid cables beneath it. She tells it without pausing over its codeword, and sends her listeners to look the operation up for themselves.

Around the moment of action stands the abort doctrine that kept officers and assets alive: *any operation can be aborted; if it feels wrong, then it is wrong.* And its mirror twin: *if your gut says to act, overwhelm their senses.* The craft of the act belonged to the magicians' world Tony Mendez had apprenticed himself to: *use misdirection, illusion, and deception; hide small operative motions in larger non-threatening motions.* Her example of the principle perfected is a brush pass, proof-tested at the Mayflower Hotel in Washington with observers posted: a man walks in with a raincoat over his arm, shifts the coat from one arm to the other as the doorman holds the door, and in that ordinary motion a small package changes hands — "just sleight of hand," she says, so easy to do and so hard to see. The magicians taught the doctrine to the officers in person, and on public radio she preserves the lesson: a demonstrator filled an amphitheater stage with thrown knives and popping balloons, then stepped to the microphone and confessed that he had never thrown a knife at all — a rigged board and an assistant across the stage had done the work, and the audience had supplied the rest. "You are the glue in the middle," he told them: you expect to see the knife fly, and so you see it. And on public radio she tells the principle at full operational scale, in a story no other telling in hand contains: a team she served on entered a foreign compound on the subcontinent and stole a communications machine, at great risk and with great ingenuity — key impressions taken, buildings emptied by government invitations, the machine lowered down a circular staircase into a waiting crate. Only afterward did Tony take her aside, a pitcher of beer in his hands, and correct her understanding of her own operation. "How about I tell you about the operation," he said. The machine had never been the object. Somewhere on the other side of the world, a man who used such a machine was suspected of spying for America; by stealing one at such elaborate, visible length, the agency demonstrated that it needed the machine itself — and the suspicion lifted off him. The theft was the misdirection; the operation was the cover story, performed for an audience of counterintelligence officers, and even its own team had not been told. The code's one flourish of poetry belongs to this cluster: *float like a butterfly; sting like a bee.* And after the act: *when free — in obscura — immediately change direction and leave the area; break your trail and blend into the local scene.*

Around meetings, the code is at its most granular. The approach was prepared by craft: *execute a surveillance detection run designed to draw them out over time; avoid static lookouts; stay away from chokepoints where they can reacquire you; select a meeting site so you can overlook the scene.* The asset was protected by separation — *keep any asset separated from you by time and distance until it is time* — and by a rule that admitted no exceptions: *if the asset has surveillance, then the operation has gone bad.* The officer approached only on certainty: *only approach the site when you are sure you are clean.* And afterward, *close the loop at a logical cover destination* — the evening had to end somewhere the cover story could explain. Over all of it stands the code's arithmetic of suspicion, the Moscow multiplication table: *once is an accident; twice is a coincidence; three times is an enemy action.*

This fuller list is the code as a working officer's body knew it: not ten commandments but a climate. And it should be recorded, in honesty, that the lists have never been perfectly stable — the rules were unwritten for decades, and they vary somewhat from source to source and officer to officer, as living doctrine does. What does not vary is the city that produced them and the price of ignoring them — nor, as the next section shows, the code's habit of growing new rules out of new scars.

### The Rules the Code Grew Afterward

A living code does not close. Twice in Jonna Mendez's tellings, the Moscow Rules can be watched in the act of growing — each time over a grave.

The first growth is the book's own last rule. Losing agents, she says, is the wound the whole apparatus was built around: whenever one was arrested, everyone who had worked the case went back over it — was it something we did, something we should fix, something we can fix? The professional discipline was to keep the wound from becoming the judgment: "Don't let it get personal, because it can bump into your judgment." What the agency had not disciplined itself to imagine was the source of the worst losses. Aldrich Ames, a CIA officer, sold the names of the agency's Soviet assets for money — about a dozen of them, she says, in the Commonwealth Club telling — knowing they would be killed; some of them were his friends, and some he had recruited himself. When the arrests began in 1985, Ames sent his handlers a message the Americans later intercepted, and she repeats its sense with undisguised contempt: don't kill them so fast — it will lead back to me. "And so that became a new Moscow rule," she says. "It's the very last rule in the book, and the rule is that betrayal can come from within." Edward Lee Howard and Robert Hanssen stand beside Ames in her accounting. The agency had refused to believe one of its own could do such a thing; the rule is the refusal's headstone. On public radio she gives the loss its size: "We lost almost all of our Russian agents because of him. One guy, one bad guy."

The second growth came after the book — and it is the rule she now numbers forty-two. In the San Diego Metro interview she introduces it inside her very definition of the code: "We added another rule about four years ago, and it's never fall in love with your agent. It didn't mean romantic love. It meant don't get too close. They're counting on you to protect them. We're doing everything we can to keep them safe; even then, we still lose some." On public radio the same rule carries its reason in her own words: it is a professional relationship, based on trust — "But don't get too close to them. Because when you lose them — and you do, it's built in — it's just really painful."

She tells, in the San Diego interview alone, of the loss that stands behind the rule — of an agent everyone who worked with him loved, arrested, and pressed to write a confession. He agreed, on one condition: "I'll write the confession, but I want to do it with my own pen." They brought him his pen. He took it from its case, put it in his mouth, and bit down on the end where the shop had embedded an L-pill — a cyanide pill. He had made the pen his price from the beginning: he would work for the Americans only if they gave him a way to take his own life if he was arrested. The agency first refused; he refused in turn; "We caved and gave him a pen. We never thought he'd use it, but he did." What he believed awaited him was the old rumor — that captured traitors, after trial, would be put into a crematorium feet first, alive. In her telling he is never named, and he is not named here. "He believed it. That's why he wanted a cyanide pill."

Beside that telling — beside it, not inside it — stands the museum telling of the pen, and in that telling alone the story has names. Alexander Ogorodnik, codename Trigon, made the pill his condition for working at all: headquarters resisted, and in the end the pill went into a pen. He was betrayed by the translator Karl Koecher, arrested, and executed. Tolkachev received one as well; he was arrested in his car, and his pen sat in his office desk — he never got to use it. Those two, she stresses, are the only two she knows of in her whole career. The two tellings are not to be merged into one: in San Diego the agent is never named, and the pen's hour comes as he sits down to write his confession; at the museum the names are given and no confession appears. The desk does not adjudicate between them. They stand as she told them, in the rooms where she told them.

So the code's growth rings read, from the outside in: first the city taught the officers how to survive it; then their own institution taught them that the knife could come from inside the house; and last — latest, and tenderest — the work taught them to govern their own hearts, because the people they were protecting counted on officers whose judgment love had not clouded, and because the losses were built in and would come anyway. *Never fall in love with your agent* is the Moscow Rules turning inward, and it is the proof that the code was never really about Moscow. It was about what it costs to do dangerous work among human beings without ceasing to be one.

## Part Four — The Book and Its Reception

*The Moscow Rules: The Secret CIA Tactics That Helped America Win the Cold War*, by Antonio J. Mendez and Jonna Mendez, was published by PublicAffairs on May 21, 2019 — four months after Tony Mendez's death, and plainly intended by its authors as a final accounting. Its stated purpose runs through everything the couple said about it: to set down, before the generation that lived it was gone, how the highest level of CIA tradecraft for the Soviet capital had actually been developed and practiced — a story, as Joe Weisberg, the creator of the television series *The Americans*, put it in his endorsement, that "even inside the CIA, very few know the whole story of." The book carries the endorsement of the sitting Director of Central Intelligence, Gina Haspel, on the primacy of human intelligence: "Technical forms of collection are vital, but a good human source is unique and can deliver decisive intelligence on our adversaries' secrets — even their intent." In her Commonwealth Club conversation that June, Jonna Mendez quoted the Director to the same effect in plainer words: there will always be a need for human intelligence, for the person-to-person work — "to look people in the eye, to give them the courage to go and do what needs to be done."

The reception was warm across an unusually wide front. The *New York Times Book Review* called the book "devilishly fun." NPR said it "opens a rare window into how the CIA works in the Russian capital." *Kirkus* judged it "eye-opening entertainment," adding that "fans of le Carré and other spinners of secret-agent tales will find this of considerable interest" — while noting honestly that the narrative of quotidian spycraft has a choppy feel in places, boredom punctuated by episodes of real excitement, much like the work itself. *The Cipher Brief*, the intelligence community's own publication, called it "a gripping, interesting and relevant read" that "reads like a spy novel yet tells a true tale of the darkest days of the espionage war." *Military Review* praised the Mendezes as having "contributed more to helping the public better understand the reality of the intelligence business" than almost anyone, and called the book "a reassuring testament to just how resourceful U.S. intelligence officers can be when faced with a determined and skillful adversary." The endorsements gathered around it read like a roll of the profession: former CIA Director George Tenet ("Jonna and Tony Mendez are American heroes"); Stella Rimington, former Director General of MI5 ("intriguing true stories of the techniques of CIA spying on the dangerous front line of the Cold War"); Jason Matthews, the former CIA officer who wrote the *Red Sparrow* trilogy; and Jack Downing, former Deputy Director for Operations, who called it "a gripping read" in which "the good, the bad, and the ugly" are "unflinchingly revealed."

The book has settled, in the years since publication, into the position its authors built for it: it is the standard citation for the Moscow Rules — the place where the unwritten code of a dead empire's capital is preserved in the words of two of its principal practitioners, one of whom did not live to hold the finished volume. Together with *Spy Dust*, it forms the Mendezes' joint testimony; Tony's *The Master of Disguise* and the *Argo* book and film carry his solo account; Jonna's *In True Face* (2024) carries hers. The museum card that travels the world in visitors' pockets is the ten-rule compression of this book — which is to say that the little card in Hays, Kansas, is a page torn, quite legitimately, from a much longer document, and this edition's work has been to put the page back between its covers.

One date in the book's long road home belongs to the questions after her museum talk, and to that telling alone. The agency's Publications Review Board approved the book on January 18 — and Tony died on January 19, the next day. She tells it plainly, unsure herself whether the two dates are related, and glad of the one thing their order secures: he knew the book was through the gate.

The craft itself had an afterlife, and she tells of it only on public radio. One of the backbones of the mask program — in her judgment, probably the best of them at the work — carried the skill into a second vocation: he builds prosthetic pieces for people whom cancer, or the attacks of September 11, 2001, left missing an eye, a nose, fingers, an ear. Not closures, she insists, but "beautiful, beautiful things," made to be worn in public — for one woman who lost an ear, he made two: her ordinary one, and her dress-up ear, pierced, with a little diamond in it. The shop that once made faces so that officers could disappear now makes faces so that the wounded can appear. She tells it with open pride, and it stands in no other telling in hand.

## Part Five — What the Rules Argue, and the Hays Amendment

Argued as philosophy, the ten rules compose a complete small doctrine for the powerless. *Assume nothing* is humility with its sleeves rolled up: the admission that the map is not the territory, enforced not by seminar but by consequence. *Never go against your gut* honors the body's intelligence — reason's sediment, trusted above the brief. *Everyone is potentially under opposition control* doubles, without changing a word, as a discipline of compassion: judge the control, not the controlled. *Don't look back; you are never completely alone* is double-edged doctrine — a warning about the tail that is always there, and, taken the other way, a parable of never being unwatched by one's own side. *Go with the flow* and its companion rules preach inculturation: truth that arrives shouting in a foreign accent is refused at the border, while truth that rides the bus and knows the bus schedule gets where it needs to go. And the final pair — *pick the time and place for action; keep your options open* — raise patience to doctrine: the right act at the wrong hour is the wrong act, and a burned option is a grave. The rule the code grew last of all — the forty-second — completes the doctrine by turning it inward: even love, in the denied area, had to be governed, so that it could protect rather than endanger.

Set beside that doctrine, professional tradecraft and the practice of the man who keeps the card in Hays share a skeleton and differ in exactly one joint. The spy conceals in order to *extract*: something is taken out of the denied area — information, a person, an advantage — and the concealment is designed never to be discovered by the people it was practiced on. The Hays practice conceals in order to *endow*: something is carried into a life — a relic, a recognition, a credit, a cause — and the concealment is designed from the first day to be discovered, by the very people it was practiced for, at the moment of maximum good. Its secrets have reveal dates written on them. A surprise is a deception with a disclosure scheduled — categorically different goods, though they share tools, the way a scalpel is shared by the surgeon and the assailant.

The amendment shows in its standing rules. Where Moscow says everyone is potentially under opposition control, the Hays reading adds: therefore handle no one roughly. Where Moscow says keep your options open, the Hays practice extends the open option to the other person. Where Moscow says the officer's identity must stay secret, the Hays secrecy protects not the practitioner but the gift. And the professionals' own finest hour runs the same way: Argo extracted six people from a revolution without a shot, and the disguise shop's proudest products were faces that let the hunted walk out of danger unrecognized. The machinery of concealment reached its best day's work when its cargo was human life and its purpose was delivery, not acquisition — which is why the amendment is best understood not as a corruption of the rules but as a return of them to their finest use.

The implication, for everyone who will never hold a platform, is the one the rules were always carrying: obscurity is not the same as impotence. The Moscow Rules describe the other physics of influence — how a single unarmed person, presumed watched and presumed powerless, nonetheless moves real things through real territory by humility, patience, disguise, timing, and flow. They are, in effect, the operating manual of the insignificant, written by professionals — and a manual, unlike a memoir, is meant to be used by whoever picks it up.

## Closing Section — Robert's Account

*The account that follows is Robert Tilford's own, in his words as he gave them. It is labeled as his because it is his: the desk has set down what he said, and nothing beyond what he said.*

He carried the rules for years. The sheet of paper — the rules themselves, the small printed set of them — was among his things, and he tried to model his conduct on it, and tried to learn from it, in the years when nobody would teach him. That is the whole of the account he gave: a man with no teacher who found, in the survival code of officers in a watched city, a set of instructions he could study alone. He modeled himself on it as best he could, by himself. The card was the teacher he had.

The talk also touches the Oval Office mask demonstration in a single breath — the mask took her into the Oval Office; she briefed the president, took the mask off, and he was impressed — corroborating in passing the story the Commonwealth telling carries in full. The published report of the same talk in *ClearanceJobs* (June 2019) remains a source for what this edition still takes from it alone — the working vocabulary of gaps and getting black, the pocket litter, and the engineers' embarrassing errands for the inflatable dolls — and that material is presented as reported wherever it enters. Two further tellings of hers are quoted with their venues named in the text: her interview on *Audacious*, the Connecticut Public Radio program hosted by Chion Wolf, published by PRX, which carries the beaten officer at the embassy doors, Tony's verdict on it, and her numbering of the last rule as the forty-second; and her interview with the *San Diego Metro* of April 2024, which carries her definition of the rules as rules of comportment, the story of the agent and his pen, and Director Webster's answer about identification at the White House gate.

Beneath those tellings stands the desk's verified record: the Moscow Rules report built at this desk on 2026-10-28 from the public record — the reference entries for Tony Mendez and Jonna Mendez and their cited references; the International Spy Museum's published biography of Jonna Mendez and its published list of the ten rules; the publishers' records for *The Moscow Rules* (PublicAffairs/Hachette, 2019), including the review notices of the *New York Times Book Review*, NPR, *Kirkus*, *The Cipher Brief*, and *Military Review*, and the printed endorsements of Gina Haspel, George Tenet, Stella Rimington, Jason Matthews, Joe Weisberg, and Jack Downing; reported profiles and obituary coverage of Tony Mendez; the documented record of the Tolkachev case and the Seventh Chief Directorate, including *The Main Enemy* and the *Government Executive* history of the operation; and the Wall Street Journal's account of the Oval Office demonstration, from which her best-known sentence about it is carried. The quotation discipline is simple enough to state: quotation marks in this edition enclose only words verified in the sources named above — hers from the Commonwealth Club transcript, hers from the museum talk's own spoken text, hers again from the two interviews where their venues are named, and passages from the Mendezes' books and the verified public record. The uncertainties are carried in the open, as they were found. And her tellings of the Oval Office morning differ on one small point — whether the White House photograph, when it reached her ten years later, still showed the mask or had it airbrushed out; both versions stand in her tellings, and this edition has blended neither away. Where this edition was unsure, it has said so, in the place where the unsure thing stands.
