# Dig Two — CUSAS 03, 0250 = CDLI P324343 (the twin mourning tablet)

*Robert Tilford's pick, 2026-10-19 — the scout's recommended candidate, the twin of the First Dig's passed piece. This dossier is the dig's deliverable for Robert's eyeball. Nothing in it has been published, posted, uploaded, or sent. The translation it carries is labeled DRAFT on its face, and the Author's Apology is carried in place at the end, still a draft, awaiting Robert's editing pass.*

## Identification

- **Object:** A clay tablet from Garšana (Ur III period), 55 × 46 × 21 mm, still inside its clay envelope; the envelope's obverse repeats the tablet's obverse, and the envelope bears the impression of Šu-Kabta's royal gift seal (CDLI seal S006097). Museum number CUNES 49-15-337, formerly Cornell University, Department of Near Eastern Studies; BDTNS now lists the Iraq Museum, Baghdad (IM number not yet assigned in the record), the Garšana corpus having been returned to Iraq in August 2021. The photographs and transliteration remain publicly accessible through the catalogues regardless of where the clay sits.
- **Text:** A one-day labour-withdrawal record: eighteen fullers of the Garšana estate, graded in four rate classes, stood down for a day of breast-beating — the mourning rite — on the day recorded in reverse line 1: the day Nawir-ilum died. Fifteen lines on the tablet (obverse 7, reverse 8); seven lines on the envelope's obverse; the seal legend in two columns of four lines.
- **Basis of this work:** The transliteration published by Owen & Mayr in the primary edition (CUSAS 3, 2007, transliteration and photograph, no translation), as carried in CDLI's catalogue record for P324343 (ATF; revisions there approved across two decades of named hands, most recently Firth 2019 and Pagé-Perron 2024) and confirmed line by line against BDTNS record 163023 (normalized sign values). The photographs were studied directly in this dig — see Phase 1. Stable identifiers: CDLI P324343; BDTNS 163023; CUSAS 03, 0250; CUNES 49-15-337; seal S006097.
- **Status of what follows:** A **DRAFT** translation, derived line by line from the published transliteration, the two catalogue witnesses, the photographs named in Phase 1, and published dictionaries (ePSD2, the electronic Pennsylvania Sumerian Dictionary, 2nd edition, via ORACC; the CDLI Ur III token lists). Restorations are marked as restorations, in the transliteration by the catalogues' brackets and in the translation and apparatus at the lines where they stand. Uncertainties are marked [?] in place.
- **The provenance caveat, in the same words as the First Dig's:** these tablets were looted at Garšana in antiquity's aftermath, their excavation context is lost, and their coherence as an archive is the editors' reconstruction.

## Phase 1 — Receipts: the photograph, taken

The scout could report only that CDLI's data facets, read across a four-record set, showed photographs for three of the four records, without resolving which records they belonged to. This dig resolves the point for this tablet. **The photograph receipt is taken, in full.** The CDLI record for P324343 carries three photographs, each fetched and studied in this dig on 2026-10-19:

1. **The tablet photograph** (CDLI photo P324343): the tablet itself, obverse and reverse with edges — seven ruled lines on the obverse, eight on the reverse, the reverse's surface worn toward its top, exactly where the transliteration's brackets cluster.
2. **The detail photograph** (CDLI photo P324343_d, a high-resolution composite): the obverse at reading size. The first line's numeral group is legible on it — the ten-sign followed by a single unit wedge, 11 — and the four graded lines that follow it begin with their smaller numerals, 2, 4, and 1. No summation line stands anywhere on the obverse; the crew is named, not totalled, in line 5. This is the photographic footing of the fuller-count settlement in Phase 2.
3. **The envelope photograph** (CDLI photo P324343_e): the envelope's faces, with the seal impressions rolled across them. The seal legend's two columns are visible in the small, fine script of the impression — the king's column and the recipient's column — matching the legend as the catalogue transmits it. The envelope's reverse is damaged, which is the condition the two catalogues describe differently (see the collation note).

Behind the CDLI photographs stands the print edition's plate in CUSAS 3 (2007), the edition's uniform format, verified on the twin text in the First Dig. The dig therefore does **not** rest on transliteration alone: the First Dig declined a candidate (the Kültepe letter) over exactly this gap, and no such gap exists here. What the photographs cannot do is replace the editors' collation — signs the ATF marks collated or damaged (#) are reported with their marks, not re-read from the pictures by this desk.

## Phase 2 — The residual named checks, run

The scout named two residual checks for this dig. Both were run; a third item the scout left as arithmetic is settled here from the tablet itself.

### (a) Heimpel (2009) and Tsouparopoulou, re-read for any rendering of reverse 1

**Tsouparopoulou, CDLN 2014:9 — read in full in this dig (2026-10-19), and the road closes further than the scout expected.** Her note "A note on Ur III administrative procrastination" establishes the death's date in these words: Nawir-ilum "died in Šu-Suen 8 x; see CUSAS 3, 251 rev. 3, **and possibly also CUSAS 3, 250, on tablet Rev. 1**." She therefore *cites this tablet's reverse 1 by number* as evidence for the death — and prints no transliteration of it and renders no line of it. The death line of this tablet has been pointed at in print, with a scholar's "possibly," and never translated. Her ZA 110 (2020) article, as established in the First Dig's verification, quotes the twin's reverse 3 in transliteration and renders nothing; it belongs to the same death dossier and is recorded here for completeness.

**Heimpel (2009) — could not be re-read in full, and the limit is stated, not hidden.** Wolfgang Heimpel's *Workers and Construction Work at Garšana* (2009) is a print volume; no openly readable copy was located on the roads run (an archive.org title search returned no copy; the volume is not openly posted). What bears on the question, on the record: his one published rendering in this dossier, at p. 332 as quoted by Sallaberger (2011) n. 30, attaches to the **twin's** reverse 3 — the hard line — not to this tablet's plain one. Sallaberger's 2011 study, read in full text by the scout, treats the Nawir-ilum death expressly under "(no. 251)"; finds for "azlag" in that article return nothing, and no rendering of this tablet appears there. Neither catalogue's bibliography for P324343 lists any Heimpel item: CDLI's complete bibliography is the primary edition and the seal study (Mayr & Owen 2004); BDTNS's reference list is Mayr & Owen 2004, Owen & Mayr 2007, the CDLI photograph resource, the ePSD2 transliteration resource, and Tsouparopoulou 2014 as a study. The residual risk is therefore exactly this, and no wider: if Heimpel rendered reverse 1 somewhere inside his 2009 volume without the catalogues recording it, the roads available to this dig would not have caught it. The label at the end of this dossier is worded to survive that possibility, as the First Dig's label was worded to survive its own unread chapter (Kleinerman 2011).

### (b) The fuller count, settled from the tablet

The scout left this as a derivation: the First Dig's report said eighteen fullers; CDLI's first obverse line reads 11 workers; the scout reconciled by arithmetic — four rate-classes, 11 + 2 + 4 + 1 = 18, the first line a class and not a total. **The settlement: the reconciliation is correct, and it now rests on the tablet, not on arithmetic alone.** The transliteration's own structure shows four graded lines (obverse 1–4) predicated jointly by line 5, "they are fuller-men"; the fractions written in lines 2–4 (2/3, 1/2, 1/3 of the labour rate, each) are the descending grades of one crew, and line 1, carrying no fraction, is the ungraded head of the same series. The detail photograph confirms the physical record: the first line opens with the numeral 11, the following lines with 2, 4, and 1, and no total is written anywhere — the scribe counted classes, and the count of men is computed, as it was for the twin ("the entries as read sum to 13 workers, but that figure is computed from the list — the scribe wrote no total"). The eighteen are therefore: 11 + 2 + 4 + 1 **men**, of whom the labour booked, at the written rates, is 11 + 2×(2/3) + 4×(1/2) + 1×(1/3) = **14⅔ day-rates** — both figures computed from the list and labeled computed. One caveat rides with the settlement: line 1's final sign is collated in the ATF (sag#-[tag]), and the envelope's copy of the line restores its numeral in part ([1(u)] 1(disz)); the numeral 11 itself is not the collated element and stands clear on the photograph.

**Collation note — the second witness.** BDTNS record 163023 (normalized sign values) agrees with the ATF in substance on every line of tablet and envelope. It differs in three particulars, all recorded: (i) it writes reverse 1's verb with its final vowel, *ba-uš2-a*, where the ATF brackets *[dingir ba-usz2]* without the final *-a*; (ii) it brackets the tablet's reverse more heavily than the ATF throughout — a more conservative statement of the same worn surface the photograph shows; (iii) where the ATF marks the envelope's reverse simply "broken," BDTNS records a single envelope reverse line — the death line itself, heavily restored: *[u4 Na-wi-ir-DINGIR ba-uš2-a]*. On BDTNS's record, then, the death sentence stood twice on the sealed object, once on the tablet and once on its envelope; on CDLI's, the envelope copy is lost. The readings are not in conflict; the statements of preservation are, and both are carried here.

### The draft translation, line by line

**Status: DRAFT.** Derived from the transliteration above, the two catalogue witnesses, the photographs, and the published dictionaries. Restorations are flagged at the lines where they stand.

#### Obverse 1
**Draft:** "11 workers, of the sag-tag class [?]"

*Apparatus.* The numeral 1(u) 1(disz) = 11 stands clear on the detail photograph. *gurusz* — ePSD2 *ĝuruš* [WORKER], "(adult) male worker." *sag#-[tag]* — a compound written with its first sign plain and its second collated (#) and partly restored on both tablet and envelope; BDTNS normalizes it as one compound, *sag-tag*, and the form is a recognized Ur III word form (the CDLI Ur III token list carries *sag-tag* and *sag-tag-ga*). No compound gloss was retrievable from the dictionaries on the open roads run — ePSD2 enters *sag* [HEAD] and *tag* [TOUCH] separately — so the term is carried over untranslated, as the evidence leaves it. Its position is its own evidence, and it is labeled inference: it heads the graded series that continues in lines 2–4 with explicit rate fractions, so it designates the crew's un-fractioned class, the men booked at the whole rate. The [?] marks the term, not the count.

#### Obverse 2
**Draft:** "2 workers, each at a rate of 2/3"

*Apparatus.* *a2* — ePSD2 *a* [LABOUR], the labour rate of a worker for a day; the fraction with *-ta* is the distributive, "at (the rate of) 2/3 each" — the standard Ur III notation for graded labour, in which a man booked at 2/3 renders two-thirds of a full day's labour value. The rendering follows the twin dossier's convention of stating the notation rather than converting it.

#### Obverse 3
**Draft:** "4 workers, each at a rate of 1/2"

*Apparatus.* As obverse 2, at the half rate.

#### Obverse 4
**Draft:** "1 worker, at a rate of 1/3 each [the final -ta is supplied]"

*Apparatus.* As obverse 2, at the third rate. The distributive *-ta* is not written at the line's end on either witness as transmitted: the ATF supplies it in angle brackets, <ta>, following the series; BDTNS's text as transmitted prints the fraction without it. The sense is not in doubt; the writing is recorded as it stands.

#### Obverse 5
**Draft:** "the men are fullers"

*Apparatus.* *lu2* — ePSD2 *lu* [MAN], "man, person." *azlag2* — ePSD2 *azlag* [FULLER], "fuller," the cloth-finisher who cleans and thickens woven cloth. *-me-esz2* — the plural copula, "they are." The line predicates the whole list: the four graded lines are one crew of fullers, and this line is the hinge on which the fuller count of Phase 2 turns — without it, lines 1–4 would be four separate bookings; with it, they are one crew of eighteen.

#### Obverse 6
**Draft:** "For 1 day"

*Apparatus.* *u4* — ePSD2 *ud* [DAY], "day"; *-sze3* terminative, of duration: "for (one) day." The phrase governs the whole list and the assignment in obverse 7, exactly as its counterpart governs the twin's list from the reverse.

#### Obverse 7
**Draft:** "stationed to beat the breast"

*Apparatus.* *gaba* — ePSD2 *gaba* [BREAST], "breast, chest." *sag3* — the "beat" verb; the reading convention is the twin's recorded one (CDLI's ATF reads *sag3*; BDTNS normalizes the same sign *sig3*; the disagreement is one of reading convention for one sign, not of sign identity, and the translation is unaffected). *-ke4-de3* — the purposive non-finite form, "in order to beat," carrying the *-ke4-* element that binds the nominalized clause to its subject, the fuller crew of line 5; the twin's counterpart (0251 rev. 2) writes the plainer *sag3-de3*. *[gub-ba]* — ePSD2 *gub* [STAND], "to stand, be stationed, be on duty," in its nominalized form: "(those) stationed." On the tablet the word is restored; on the envelope (obverse 7) it is written in full — the sealed duplicate supplies the tablet's loss, which is what a duplicate is for. The compound *gaba* + "beat" is not a dictionary-fixed idiom; the rendering stays literal, as in the twin, and the mourning sense rests on the occasion stated in reverse 1 — here, unlike the twin, stated in the same tablet in so many words.

#### Reverse 1
**Draft:** "on the day Nawir-ilum died [the name's last sign and the verb are restored]"

*Apparatus.* *u4* — temporal, "on the day (when)," heading a clause closed by a nominalized verb. *na-we-er-[dingir]* — the personal name Nawir-ilum, Akkadian in form, its final theophoric element ("god," Akkadian *ilu*) written without the divine determinative; the preserved signs na-we-er- stand on the tablet, and the name's completion is a restoration — a secure one, since the man is the Garšana medical practitioner (so Tsouparopoulou, CDLN 2014:9, "Nawir-ilum the medical practitioner") whose death this dossier dates, and no other name fits the traces or the dossier. *[ba-usz2]* — ePSD2 *uš* [DIE], "to die"; *ba-* the middle prefix: "died." The verb is restored in the ATF; BDTNS writes it *ba-uš2-a*, with the subordinating *-a* the ATF's bracket omits, and records the same line, heavily restored, on the envelope's reverse as well. The restoration's footing is threefold, and stated: the surviving name stem; the twin tablet, where the same death is the occasion of the same duty on the same date (0251, whose own death clause is the disputed one — the plain statement slept in this tablet); and Tsouparopoulou's citation of this very line, "possibly also CUSAS 3, 250, on tablet Rev. 1," as the death's evidence. The line has never been rendered in print on the roads checked — the full record is Phase 2(a) and the label below. What the line denotes it denotes plainly: a death, on a day, and the tablet is dated by that day (month and year, reverse 7–8). No funeral, burial, or circumstance is stated, and none is supplied.

#### Reverse 2
**Draft:** "Ea-šar was the commissioner"

*Apparatus.* *e2-a-szar* — the personal name Ea-šar, in the formula's name slot. *maszkim* — ePSD2 *maškim* [COMMISSIONER], "commissioner, responsible official." The line is verbless, name and function in apposition, as the formula runs. One difference from the twin is recorded: there (0251 rev. 4) Ea-šar carries the further title *dub-sar*, "the scribe"; here he is named with the commissioner's function alone. The man is the same; the booking desk styled him as the booking needed.

#### Reverse 3
**Draft:** "Withdrawal of Šu-Kabta"

*Apparatus.* *zi-ga* — the nominal form of ePSD2 *zi(g)* [REMOVE], "to remove, withdraw, deduct"; as a rubric, the standard Ur III accounting term "withdrawal, booked-out entry," heading the whole transaction. *szu-kab-ta2#* — the personal name Šu-Kabta, the Garšana householder, its final sign collated (#). As the twin's apparatus established: the final *-ta2* is part of the written name, not the ablative ending — the line does not say "from Šu-Kabta" — and the relation between *zi-ga* and the name is grammatically unmarked, the genitive *-ak* unwritten, as is common in this orthography.

#### Reverse 4–5
**Draft:** "from Puzur-Akkum it was withdrawn"

*Apparatus.* The source formula that closes a *zi-ga* entry, spread over two lines here where the twin compresses it into one (0251 rev. 6). *ki … -ta#* — *ki* [PLACE] in the fixed phrase "from (the charge of)," with *-ta* the ablative ending — the true ablative, in pointed contrast to the name-final *-ta2* of reverse 3; the ending's sign is collated (#). *puzur4-a-ku-um* — the personal name Puzur-Akkum (the CDLI genre note spells the name "Puzur-Akum"; the transliteration's *puzur4-a-ku-um* is followed here). *ba-zi* — *ba-* + *zi(g)*, "it was withdrawn": the finite verb that makes reverse 3's *zi-ga* a completed booking; its understood patient is the fullers' labour listed on the obverse. Where the labour was withdrawn *from* differs between the twins, and the difference is a fact of the dossier: the twin's booking drew on the charge of Iškur-illat; this one draws on Puzur-Akkum's.

#### Reverse 6
**Draft:** "In Garšana"

*Apparatus.* *[sza3]* — ePSD2 *ša* [INSIDE], "in, within," the locative frame, restored at the line's head. *gar#-sza-an-na{ki}* — the place name Garšana with the place determinative, its first sign collated (#). The identification of the place is secure from the archive.

#### Reverse 7
**Draft:** "Month: 'Great Festival'"

*Apparatus.* *iti* — ePSD2 *iti* [MONTH], "month." *ezem-mah#* — *ezem* [FESTIVAL] + *mah* [GREAT]: the month name "Great Festival," rendered as written and not converted to a month number on the tablet's own evidence; the sign is collated (#). The catalogues' equation of the name with the tenth month is handled in the date section below.

#### Reverse 8
**Draft:** "Year: 'The great barge was fashioned'"

*Apparatus.* *mu* — ePSD2 *mu* [YEAR], "year," introducing the year formula. *ma2-gur8-mah#* — *ma2-gur8* [BARGE], the great processional barge, + *mah* [GREAT]; the name's last sign is collated (#). *[ba-dim2]* — the verb restored entire: *ba-* + *dim2* [FASHION], "was fashioned, was made" — the standard verb of this year name as the formula is attested across the corpus. A variance between the twins' witnesses is recorded, not harmonized: the twin's ATF (0251 rev. 9) reads its verb *ba-du3#*, "was built," itself collated. The two tablets name the same year; the catalogues transmit its verb in two forms, one of them restored here and the other damaged there, and the dossier prints both as they stand.

#### The envelope

**Draft (of what the envelope adds):** "The envelope repeats the tablet's obverse word for word — the same four graded lines, the same 'the men are fullers,' the same day and duty — with the small variants the transliteration shows: its first numeral is partly restored, several signs are collated where the tablet's are plain and plain where the tablet's are restored, and obverse 7 writes *gub-ba*, 'stationed,' in full where the tablet's copy is restored. Its reverse is broken (CDLI); BDTNS records one line there, the death line, heavily restored."

The envelope is therefore not a second text but the tablet's own duplicate, sealed around it — the built-in collation witness the scout promised, and in obverse 7 it earns the name.

#### The seal legend (seal 1, CDLI S006097)

**Draft:** "Šū-Suen, strong man, king of Ur, king of the four quarters — gave (this seal) to Šu-Kabta, the general, his servant."

*Apparatus.* Column 1 is the king's titulary: *{d}szu-{d}suen*, the divine Šū-Suen; *nita kal-ga*, "strong man"; *lugal uri5{ki}-ma*, "king of Ur"; *lugal an-ub-da limmu2-ba*, "king of the four quarters (of the world)." Column 2 is the gift: *szu-kab-ta2*, the recipient; *szagina* — ePSD2 *šagina* [GENERAL], the military title, "general" — the title by which the seal styles the householder, who in the literature is physician and general both; *ARAD2-da-ni-ir*, "to his servant," the dative of *arad* [SERVANT] with the possessive; *in-na-ba*, "he gave to him" — the king the giver, the seal the gift. This is the royal gift seal studied by Mayr & Owen ("The Royal Gift Seal in the Ur III Period," HSAO 9, 2004, pp. 145–174, citing this tablet at pp. 162 and 167 n. 10) — a study of the seal the envelope bears, not an edition of the tablet's text, and the one secondary item in the tablet's whole bibliography besides its edition.

### The draft translation, read straight through

> Obverse: 11 workers, of the sag-tag class [?]. 2 workers, each at a rate of 2/3. 4 workers, each at a rate of 1/2. 1 worker, at a rate of 1/3 each. The men are fullers. For 1 day, stationed to beat the breast.
>
> Reverse: On the day Nawir-ilum died. Ea-šar was the commissioner. Withdrawal of Šu-Kabta. From Puzur-Akkum it was withdrawn. In Garšana. Month: "Great Festival." Year: "The great barge was fashioned."

Fifteen lines on the tablet; the envelope repeats the obverse; the seal legend is translated above. The translation's total length is the tablet's own: about ninety words of English for the tablet, forty for the legend.

### The date, stated with its caution

The tablet dates itself by the month name "Great Festival" and the year formula "The great barge was fashioned." Both catalogues assign that combination to Šū-Suen year 8, month 10 — CDLI's record under "Dates Referenced: Šū-Suen.08.10.00," BDTNS under "SS08 – 10 – 00" — and **neither catalogue queries the assignment**, where both query the twin's. The asymmetry is the catalogues' own and was reported by the scout unexplained; it is reported the same way here. The assignment remains what it is — a catalogue inference from a month name and a year formula — and is cited in this dossier as: Šū-Suen 8, month 10 (the catalogues' assignment, unqueried in both). Tsouparopoulou's dating of the death, "Šu-Suen 8 x," agrees. The archive's dated span runs 2031–2024 BC.

### What the tablet records

A one-day withdrawal of labour at Garšana. Eighteen fullers — eleven of the sag-tag class, two booked at two-thirds of the labour rate, four at half rate, and one at a third, the rates together booking fourteen and two-thirds day-rates of labour, though both totals are computed from the list and the scribe wrote neither — were stationed for one day to beat the breast, the mourning rite, on the day Nawir-ilum died. The withdrawal was made under the responsibility of Ea-šar, acting as commissioner; it was booked as a withdrawal of Šu-Kabta, the dead man's own householder; and it was drawn from the charge of Puzur-Akkum, at Garšana, in the month "Great Festival," in the year "The great barge was fashioned." The tablet was then sealed inside an envelope that repeats its face, under the impression of the householder's royal gift seal.

Set beside its twin, the pair documents a single morning from two desks of one household: the twin stood down thirteen workers of mixed crafts — women at the sesame press, a fuller, leatherworkers, reed-workers, a potter, a smith, doorkeepers — and circled the death in a clause the scholarship still parses three ways; this tablet stood down the fuller crew entire, graded man by man, and said the thing plainly: *on the day Nawir-ilum died*. The plain sentence is the one the scholarship dates the death by, citing it — with a "possibly" — from this tablet.

### Lines judged least certain

1. **Reverse 1** — not for its sense, which is plain, but for its state: the name's last sign and the verb are restored in the ATF, BDTNS brackets the line's envelope copy entire, and the restoration, though threefold-footed (traces, twin, citation), is a restoration. It is marked as one everywhere it appears in this dossier.
2. **Obverse 1** — the compound *sag-tag*: carried untranslated because no dictionary gloss for the compound was retrievable on the open roads run; its class sense is a labeled inference from its position in the graded series.
3. **Reverse 8** — the year formula's verb is restored entire ([ba-dim2]), and the twin's witness transmits a different verb (ba-du3#) for the same year name; both are printed as they stand.
4. **Obverse 4** — the distributive *-ta* is editorially supplied, not written, on the witnesses as transmitted.
5. **Obverse 7** — the *sag3/sig3* reading is a convention for one sign, as in the twin; the breast-beating compound is not a dictionary-fixed idiom and its rendering stays literal.

## Contradictions and open seams

Four, carried openly, none smoothed.

**First, the two catalogues' statements of preservation for the reverse.** CDLI's ATF brackets parts of the tablet's reverse and marks the envelope's reverse "broken"; BDTNS brackets the tablet's reverse more heavily throughout, and records a full (heavily restored) death line on the envelope's reverse where CDLI records none. The readings agree line for line; the damage reports differ. This dossier follows the ATF for the text and prints BDTNS's envelope line as BDTNS's record, and the disagreement stands here rather than being silently resolved in either direction.

**Second, the year formula's verb.** This tablet's year verb is restored entire — [ba-dim2], "was fashioned," the formula's standard verb — while the twin's witness transmits ba-du3#, "was built," under a collation mark. Same year, two transmitted forms, one restored and one damaged. The dossier prints both as they stand and translates each tablet by its own witness.

**Third, the supplied -ta of obverse 4.** The graded series requires the distributive ending; the witnesses as transmitted do not write it at line 4 (the ATF supplies it in angle brackets; BDTNS prints the fraction bare). The sense is certain and the writing is not, and both halves of that sentence are in the apparatus at the line.

**Fourth, the dating asymmetry.** Both catalogues query the twin's date assignment and neither queries this tablet's, though both tablets give the same month name and year formula and stand or fall by the same inference. The scout reported the asymmetry unexplained; this dig reports it the same way. Nothing in the dig's work turns on it — the date is cited as the catalogues' assignment in either case — but a reader comparing the two dossiers should find the seam showing in both.

And one open item, named rather than buried: the compound *sag-tag* (obverse 1) has no dictionary gloss on the roads run, and Heimpel's *Workers and Construction Work at Garšana* — the one study whose scope covers Garšana crew classes directly — is the same book Phase 2 could not re-read in full. If a rendering or a gloss for the term sleeps there, this dossier's roads would not have woken it. The term is carried untranslated, the inference from its position is labeled an inference, and the gap is stated here, in the open, where a future reader with the book in hand can close it.

## Part — The Man: Nawir-ilum

*Addendum, added 2026-10-19 by Robert's order of that morning. The dig's parts above stand unaltered; this part was gathered after the dig closed, from the published scholarship named in its sentences, and it is written as what Robert named the whole dossier to be: a memorial to the man whose death-day closes the tablet. Nothing here has been published, posted, uploaded, or sent. Where the record ends, this part ends; the silences are named as silences, not dressed.*

### The name

His name is Akkadian, and it is a sentence: Nawir-ilum, "the god is radiant" — the element *nawir* means "radiant, shining," as Audrey Pitts's onomastic survey of the period lists it among the name elements of the Ur III record. The scribes wrote it more than one way. This dossier's tablet writes *na-we-er-dingir*; the twin and the catalogues write *na-wi-ir-ilum* and *na-wi-ir-DINGIR*, the final element — *ilum*, "god" — written with the divine determinative in some witnesses and without it in others, as the First Dig's apparatus already recorded for this tablet. The variation is orthography, not identity: every spelling in the census below is the one man, and the identifications rest on the contexts the scholarship sets out — his titles, his household, his dog-handler Puzur-Enlil, his partner Anaa — and not on the name alone, since a name by itself, in an archive of ninety-five thousand tablets, proves nothing.

### The census of his attestations

Thirty-seven tablets that name him are identified individually, by their publication designations, in the scholarship consulted for this part. The count reconciles as follows, and the groups must not be added together a second time, because they overlap: the thirty-seven are the union of three gatherings, and several tablets stand in two gatherings at once.

Six of the thirty-seven are Garšana tablets. They are CUSAS 3, 250 and CUSAS 3, 251, the mourning twins this house has dug; CUSAS 3, 270, which carries the seal of his partner Anaa; CUSAS 3, 982, which records his funerary offerings; CUSAS 3, 1116, which carries the seal of his servant, a scribe; and the grain account cited as SAT 3, 1316, in which the physician Ubartum is the sole recipient of grain provisions save for Nawir-ilum himself. Alexandra Kleinerman, whose 2011 study of the Garšana physicians rests on the published concordance to the Garšana archives, counts seven Garšana texts that mention him, so at least one more Garšana tablet stands in her note beyond the six identified here; the note's full list could not be recovered entire from the copy of her article consulted for this part, and the count of seven is carried as her count, honestly labeled.

Twenty-one of the thirty-seven belong to the dog archive of Puzriš-Dagan, the royal livestock agency at Drehem, and they are enumerated in the tables of Christina Tsouparopoulou's study of the healing goddess, her dogs, and her physicians. Seventeen of the twenty-one are lifetime records, running from Amar-Suen 4 to the month of his death: AUCT 2, 89; TRU 333; PDT 2, 1351; PPAC 4, 207; BIN 3, 243; TLB 3, 34; AUCT 2, 15; MVN 13, 422; Torino 1, 266; PDT 1, 409; SACT 1, 178; and AUCT 1, 543, from the consignment tables, and MVN 13, 476; BCT 1, 92; BCT 1, 93; RA 9, 55 (also cited as SA 228); and Hirose 288, from the table of equid deliveries. Three more name him after his death, and one, PDT 1, 515, is an undated summary of the skins of animals the dogs had eaten, sent from him through his dog-shepherd Puzur-Enlil. The three posthumous tablets are treated below, with his death.

The remaining ten are the wider record of his life beyond Garšana and the kennels. AUCT 1, 241 (Šū-Suen 2, month 7) names him conveyor of a royal votive to Nippur. The text published as SMS 2–3, p. 13, no. 8 (Amar-Suen 9, month 4) names Anaa as his lukur on the occasion of a ritual he performed. MVN 15, 244 and JCS 39, 122, no. 6 (Amar-Suen 7, month 8, days 20 and 23) record him traveling to his city from the royal center at Tummal. CST 399 (Amar-Suen 9, month 11, day 23) names him commissioner for a sheep offered to the god Enki. MVN 11, 183 designates Anaa as his lukur. SANTAG 6, 228 (Šū-Suen 1) and RIAA 115 (Šū-Suen 3, month 9) record royal gifts to him, and BPOA 6, 1422 and BPOA 1, 378 record him receiving sheep. One further text stands behind a caution: Kleinerman reports that he once delivered animals as a royal delivery, a *mu-DU lugal*, in the company of a provincial governor, a prince, and Šu-Kabta himself, but the footnote citation for that text is only partly legible in the copy consulted — the designation Ontario 1, 127 stands in the footnote sequence at that point — and the tablet is therefore counted as a probable thirty-eighth, not a certain one.

Against these gatherings stands Kleinerman's own count of his professional attestations, and it is larger than any list this part can enumerate. She states that he is labeled explicitly as a doctor, an *a-zu*, in fourteen accounts, besides other documents that are certainly his but do not name his profession. Nine of the fourteen can be matched to tablets in the census above: the eight whose texts, as Pitts quotes them, style him *a-zu* in so many signs — SANTAG 6, 228; RIAA 115; BPOA 6, 1422; BPOA 1, 378; the SMS text; MVN 15, 244; JCS 39, 122, no. 6; and AUCT 1, 241 — and a ninth, CUSAS 3, 1116, where his own servant's seal styles him "Nawir-ilum, the *a-zu*." The remaining five stand in Kleinerman's footnote, which the consulted copy does not preserve entire. The honest summary of the census is therefore this: thirty-seven tablets identified by designation in the sources read, thirty-eight counting the royal-delivery text whose designation is uncertain, and the true total higher than either figure, since Kleinerman's fourteen and her seven are counts made from the full concordance, and this part could not re-derive them tablet by tablet from the open copies.

### His offices, as the record gives them

He was a physician. That is the title the record gives him most often and most explicitly: *a-zu*, "doctor," in fourteen accounts by Kleinerman's count, spread across the archives of Garšana, Puzriš-Dagan, and Umma. What the title meant in his daily life the record does not show, and the memorial must not pretend otherwise. Kleinerman's survey of the period's physicians ends in a caution this part adopts as its own: there is, so far, no evidence that any Ur III physician spent time practicing medicine as a modern reader pictures it; what the texts show is men and one woman of considerable status, occurring in contexts with other high-ranking professionals. Of Nawir-ilum's healing — a patient treated, a remedy given, a cure wrought — not one line survives. His medicine is a title, a standing, and a goddess's service, and the next paragraphs give exactly those, in the record's own terms.

He held a second title, and a rare one: *sukkal i3-du8*, rendered "Secretary of the Doorkeepers." Kleinerman establishes it from the seals of his own household — the seal of Anaa, his lukur, impressed on CUSAS 3, 270, styles him so, and the seal of his son Takil-ilissu gives the father the same title — and Tsouparopoulou, citing Wu Yuhong's 2008 study of the three men of this family across the three archives, likewise gives him both titles together, physician and Secretary of the Doorkeepers. The rarity of the title matters to his kinship, and is treated there.

He was, for the crown's livestock agency at Puzriš-Dagan, the overseer — *ugula* — of the dog handlers of the healing goddess Gula. This is the office the twenty-one dog-archive tablets document. The consignments were a standing charge of the state: sheep by the month for Gula's cult at Ur, and dead animals for her dogs, received by a dog handler, disbursed by a named official, and booked under an overseer. From Amar-Suen's reign the overseer is Nawir-ilum. His dog-shepherd, in text after text, is Puzur-Enlil: the CDLI catalogue's translation of Hirose 288 (Amar-Suen 8, month 12) runs, "Under the authority of Puzur-Enlil, the shepherd of dogs, whose chief was Nawir-ilum." In BIN 3, 243 (Šū-Suen 5, month 12) he himself is the conveyor of a year's account of the skins of the animals the dogs had eaten — 768 animals in the year's total as the table gives it, 222 and 55 and 75 and 32 and 384 in its parts — and in MVN 13, 422 his own seal closes a dog-consignment entry. Tsouparopoulou's conclusion from this dossier is measured, and this part adopts her measure: as the generals oversaw the dogs that belonged to the army, so the physicians oversaw the dogs and their handlers that belonged to the temples of the healing goddess, and the physician of the record belonged, in her words' sense, to the higher echelons of his society.

Two things he was not, on the evidence, and the memorial says them plainly because the questions arise naturally from the two digs. He was not the foreman of the fullers. No text consulted titles him over the fuller crew or any craft crew; the eighteen fullers of CUSAS 3, 250 were booked as a withdrawal of Šu-Kabta, under Ea-šar's commission, and Nawir-ilum stands in that tablet only as the occasion — the man whose death-day it was. And he is nowhere titled a general. Tsouparopoulou once remarks, in a footnote of her study of the army's dog handlers, that he "could well be a general as well," since he is attested in many texts as a doctor and his kinsman Šu-Kabta held both titles; that is her speculation, printed here as speculation, and no tablet read for this part gives him the general's title. His son held it, and his brother held it. He did not, so far as the record shows.

His remaining recorded functions are single acts, and they are the acts of a trusted man at the crown's business. In AUCT 1, 241 he is the conveyor — the text's word is *ĝiri3*, "by the foot of" — of a royal votive of silver to the healing gods at Nippur: to Nintinuga, and, as Pitts's citation of the text gives the list, to Šumaḫ, Damu, and Gunura besides; Kleinerman describes the same act as his authorizing the delivery of royal offerings to Nintinuga and Damu, and the difference between "conveyor" and "authorizer" is a difference of the scholars' English for the one Sumerian function-word, recorded here rather than harmonized. Once, in CST 399, he serves as commissioner, *maškim*, for a single sheep offered to Enki, the god of the incantations that medical practice used — Pitts notes the fitness of the assignment and says only that it perhaps increases the likelihood that this commissioner is the physician, and her "perhaps" is carried with the claim. And once, on Kleinerman's report, he delivered animals as a delivery of the king in the company of a governor of an outlying province, a prince, and Šu-Kabta — company that is itself a statement about where he stood.

### His household

The record gives him a household, and its outline is firmer than a memorial usually gets — though parts of it stand on inference, and the inferences are labeled where they stand.

His partner was Anaa. She is designated his *lukur* in MVN 11, 183 and on the evidence of her own seal on CUSAS 3, 270; Kleinerman calls her, with a scholar's care, his wife or mistress — a lukur was a cloistered priestess, a status normally bound to gods and kings, and the word itself is the record's, not this part's embroidery. What a lukur meant for his standing is stated in the next section. In the SMS text of Amar-Suen 9, Anaa, "lukur of Nawir-ilum the *a-zu*," received silver coils together with two boatmen, on the occasion of a ritual Nawir-ilum performed — the ritual's signs are partly illegible in the copy Pitts cites — and the release was authorized by Riṣ-ilum, the queen's mounted courier, acting as commissioner. Whatever he was doing that day, the crown was paying attention to it; that is Pitts's inference from the courier's presence, and it is a fair one, labeled hers.

He had a son: Takil-ilissu, a general. Kleinerman reports the filiation and the title, and reports further — citing Heimpel's Garšana volume at page 4, note 9 — that the son may have succeeded Šu-Kabta at Garšana after the great man's death. The son's own seal, like Anaa's, styles the father Secretary of the Doorkeepers: the household remembered his titles on its seals even where the accounts omit them.

He had a father, by the record's strongest inference. Šu-Kabta's seals name Šu-Kabta's father outright: Naram-ili. Nawir-ilum's seals do not name his. But Nawir-ilum held the same rare second title that Šu-Kabta held, Secretary of the Doorkeepers, and Wu Yuhong's study — as Tsouparopoulou reports its frame — treats Naram-ili himself as a bearer of that doorkeeper title, close to Šu-Kabta. From the shared rare title and the shared household world, Kleinerman concludes that the evidence is strong that the two physicians were brothers. Piotr Steinkeller, as quoted by Pitts, calls Nawir-ilum "undoubtedly Šu-kabta's close relative (either his uncle or brother)." The kinship is therefore as certain as inference gets in this record — two scholars, independently framed, land on close kin — and the exact degree is not settled: brother, on Kleinerman's reading of the shared title; uncle or brother, on Steinkeller's. Both are printed; neither is crowned. If the brotherhood holds, his father was Naram-ili the doorkeeper, and the doorkeeper's title ran in the family like a trade.

The household had servants and substance. Kleinerman records that he had a cook of his own, and that he purchased a slave for that cook — a man who buys a slave for his cook is a man with a table worth cooking for. His servant, a scribe, sealed tablets in his name at Garšana, and it is that servant's seal on CUSAS 3, 1116 that preserves his doctor's title in the home archive where the accounts themselves omit it. Of Ubartum, the woman physician of the same Garšana generation, Kleinerman writes that it is tempting to speculate she was the two men's sister; the one hard datum beneath the speculation is the grain account in which she is the sole recipient save for Nawir-ilum. The speculation is printed as speculation, with its datum, and no more is claimed.

### His standing in life

Standing in the Ur III record is not praised; it is booked. By that measure his was high, and the bookkeeping is specific. The crown gave him gifts, and the gift formula names them royal: five sheep in SANTAG 6, 228, "a royal gift" in the text's own phrase as Pitts quotes it; fruits and fish in RIAA 115, likewise a royal gift; sheep in BPOA 6, 1422 and BPOA 1, 378, which Kleinerman counts among the accounts that style him doctor. When he traveled — twice in three days in the eighth month of Amar-Suen 7 — he drew supplies for the journey to his city from the royal center at Tummal, and the texts that record it style him *a-zu* as they provision him. He performed at least one ritual of sufficient moment that the queen's own mounted courier authorized the gifts that attended it. Steinkeller's general finding, as Pitts reports it, is that the Ur III physicians worked for the crown rather than for temples or private patrons, and frequently enjoyed high social status, and that Nawir-ilum fits the pattern; the record above is the pattern, item by item.

The rarest mark of his standing is Anaa. Pitts's dissertation surveys the men of the period who had lukurs of their own, and the company is very small: apart from gods and deified kings, the men attested with lukurs are the general Ur-nigar, Arad-Nanna — the realm's second man, heir of the grand viziers — the governor Ur-Lisi, and, in her sentence, "the fourth man to have his own lukur was Nawir-DINGIR, a physician." A cloistered priestess-partner was an honor that tracked royalty and the highest office; that a physician stands fourth in such a list is the record's own testimony to where he stood, and it needs no adjective from this part. Pitts adds, labeling it herself as temptation, the speculation that the honor may have followed a successful exercise of his craft upon an ailing king or a member of the royal family. It is a beautiful guess and it stays a guess; the memorial prints it in her own framing and builds nothing on it.

### The death, and what followed it

He died in the tenth month of Šū-Suen's eighth year — ŠS 8, month 10, in the catalogues' assignment of the twins' own month name and year formula, an assignment neither catalogue queries for this tablet and both query for its twin, an asymmetry the dig has already carried openly and carries still. What the two tablets record is the household's answer to the death. CUSAS 3, 250 stood down the fuller crew entire — eighteen men in four rate classes, eleven of them whole-rate, two at two-thirds, four at half, one at a third — for one day of breast-beating. Its twin, CUSAS 3, 251, stood down thirteen workers of the mixed crafts on the same occasion. Between the two accounts, thirty-one workers of the estate were taken off their labour for the mourning, and both totals are computed from the lists, as the digs have labeled them; the scribes wrote rosters, not sums.

The scholarship lets the stand-down be measured against its own kind, and the measure is the closest thing to an obituary the record affords. Pitts's dissertation, in a footnote gathering the period's breast-beating assignments, sets three Garšana mourning accounts of that single year side by side. In the seventh month, when the prince Šulgi-rama died, three male workers were stationed at breast-beating for one day (CUSAS 3, 246). In the tenth month, for Nawir-ilum, the eighteen fullers stood one day. In the eleventh month's account, for Šu-Kabta himself — the general, the physician, the princess's husband — ninety-five full-time women weavers and fifteen more at a third rate beat the breast for nine and a half days. The scale is the record's own language: a prince of the blood drew three men for a day; the physician drew eighteen; the great householder drew a hundred and ten women for over a week. Nawir-ilum's mourning stands between a prince's token party and his brother's state occasion, nearer the one in size and the other in kind — a whole craft crew, stood down entire, for a man who was not royal. That the stand-down evidences high standing is this part's inference from the comparison, and it is labeled inference; the comparison itself is the record.

The twin's death clause — reverse 3 of CUSAS 3, 251, the hard line of the First Dig — has now been treated in print four ways, and the memorial gathers them in one place because the clause is this man's only epitaph. The First Dig's draft, adopted on contextual grounds and ranked by its verifier as the weakest of the three then on record, reads it as the day he was laid before the god. Heimpel, at page 332 of his 2009 volume as quoted by Sallaberger, reads the breast in the line: the day the breast of Nawir-ilum was made to settle by the god. Sallaberger himself, in his 2011 study at section 4.5.4, reads a god placed opposite Nawir-ilum, with his own question mark standing and his own verdict that the phrase is almost unintelligible. And Tsouparopoulou, in her study of the healing goddess, transliterates the line with the verb read as DUH — *u4 diĝir-re DUH na-wi-ir-DINGIR ba-a-ĝar-ra* — and renders it not at all, using it only to fix the death's date. Four treatments, none crowned here; the First Dig's parsings stand in its own dossier, and this part adds only the fourth witness and the observation that the dispute itself is a kind of fame: scholars are still arguing, in print, over the grammar of the day this man died.

The administration, meanwhile, did not know — or did not say — for almost a year. Tsouparopoulou's note of 2014, "A note on Ur III administrative procrastination," and her later study of the goddess's physicians establish the sequence from the dog archive itself. In Šū-Suen 8, month 12, two months after the death, MVN 13, 89 still books the Gula consignments under Nawir-ilum as overseer. In Šū-Suen 9, month 3, UDT 171 does the same. In Šū-Suen 9, month 9, PDT 1, 7 still does the same. Only in Šū-Suen 9, month 10 — one year to the month after the death month — do AUCT 1, 224 and TSDU 94 enter a new overseer, Šu-Mamitum, in his place. A dead man's name thus closed the accounts of the goddess's dogs for eleven further bookings across three seasons, and the delay is not a rumor about the bureaucracy but its own dated handwriting, three tablets deep. Who Šu-Mamitum was, the record does not settle: a doorkeeper of that name is attested, and a carrier of animals of that name appears together with Šu-Kabta, and Tsouparopoulou raises — as a possibility only — that the doorkeeper-overseer may himself have been a physician, which would make the succession a physician's for a physician's. The possibility is printed as hers, and it ends there.

And the estate kept feeding his ghost. CUSAS 3, 982, dated to Ibbi-Suen 2, month 12 — more than three years after the death month — records sheep for his regular funerary offerings: the *sa2-du11 ki-a-nag*, the regular offerings at the funerary chapel, in Kleinerman's citation of the text's fourth line. Kleinerman's sentence on the family's mourning deserves its place here in substance: the relationship between Šu-Kabta and Nawir-ilum is made explicit, she writes, by the Garšana resources dedicated first to the mourning of Nawir-ilum at the end of Šū-Suen's eighth year and later to the regular funerary offerings provided for him on the estate. A *ki-a-nag* was not a pauper's portion; Pitts's survey of such funerary offering places notes that they are attested for kings, governors, priestesses, and their families, the common denominator being wealth and high status. His chapel offerings were still being booked when the dynasty itself had less than two decades to live.

### The world around him, briefly

Garšana was the estate of Šu-Kabta — general by his royal gift seal, physician by the same record that names his brother — and of Šu-Kabta's wife, the king's daughter Simat-Ištaran, who after his death managed the estate herself. Its economy was substantially cloth: Kleinerman records that the estate's textile industry was managed by a woman named Ataqqar, and the two mourning tablets show the craft world whole — fullers who cleaned and thickened the woven cloth, women at the sesame press, leatherworkers, reed-workers, a potter, a smith, doorkeepers. The fuller, the *azlag2*, was a cloth-finisher, and the eighteen men of this dossier's tablet were that crew entire, graded from whole-rate men down to a single man at a third of the rate. Of the two officials the tablets name around the mourning, the wider record consulted for this part says nothing that ties them to Nawir-ilum beyond these two bookings: Ea-šar, commissioner on both twins and styled scribe on the twin, is the booking officer of the estate's desk, and Puzur-Akkum is the holder of the account from which this tablet's withdrawal was drawn, as Iškur-illat is on the twin. Searches for a sourced account of either man's relation to the physician returned nothing this part can print; the silence is recorded, not filled.

Beyond Garšana stood the other half of his working life: the state livestock agency at Puzriš-Dagan and the cult of Gula at Ur, where thirty sheep a month had gone to the goddess's consignment since before his tenure and the dogs ate at the cult's charge. It is the earliest documented association, in Tsouparopoulou's showing, between a healing goddess, her dogs, and a physician — the pattern that later ages would carve in stone at Isin, where Gula's temple kept its dog burials. Nawir-ilum stands at the head of that documentary line not because anyone praised him, but because the kennels' accounts were kept under his name for a decade.

### What cannot be known

The honest close must first correct the frame this memorial was commissioned in. It is not the case that no family survives: a partner, a son, a father by strong inference, a brother by contested degree — the record holds a household, and this part has printed it. What does not survive is everything else a life is made of. No birth is recorded, and no childhood, and no place he was born. No word of his own survives — not a letter, not a judgment, not a prescription; the seals that speak in his name speak the formulae of his household's servants. No act of his medicine survives: no patient, no remedy, no cure, and Kleinerman's caution stands over the whole profession in his period, that the texts show status and context and never practice. No cause of death is recorded, and no age at death; the career the tablets show runs from Amar-Suen 4 to Šū-Suen 8, some thirteen years of bookings, but a man is not thirteen years old when his bookings begin, and how much of him stands before Amar-Suen 4 the record does not say. No tomb is identified; the *ki-a-nag* that received his offerings is a word in an account, not a place on any map this part can point to. His face, his voice, his character, his faith — the record holds none of it, and this memorial invents none of it.

What survives is this. A name that is a small sentence of praise: the god is radiant. Two titles — a physician's, and a secretaryship of doorkeepers rare enough to argue kinship from. An overseership of the healing goddess's dogs, documented month by month for years. A partner whose status was itself an honor roll of four names. A son who bore the general's title his father never bore. A death fixed to its month in an age that did not record even its kings' death-days in the administrative hand — Pitts, following Hallo, notes that among the tens of thousands of Ur III administrative texts, not one gives the actual death date of any king — and a mourning booked twice, by two desks of one household, in rosters that still add up. A name kept working the goddess's accounts for almost a year after the man was in the ground, and a funerary portion still delivered three years on. That is the whole of it, and it is not nothing. His song is sung here from exactly this evidence and no other; where the record ends, the song ends, and the silence after it is left as it was found.

### Contradictions and open seams of this part

Five, carried openly, none smoothed.

**First, a seam that touches the dig itself.** Pitts's dissertation cites the mourning account of this dossier's tablet under a second designation — Fs. Pettinato 162, 167, no. 10 — and describes it in English in her footnote: eleven full-time, two two-thirds-time, four half-time, and one one-third-time male fullers for one day, assigned to breast-beating when Nawir-ilum the physician died. The roster matches this tablet's, man for man and rate for rate, and on that footing this part treats the two designations as one tablet cited two ways, the identification labeled as resting on the match. The seam is this: the Fs. Pettinato edition of the text and Pitts's English description of its operative lines were not among the roads the dig's primacy record walked, and the dig's label — no published translation of this tablet, in whole or in part, in any language, found after research — therefore stands in this dossier exactly as the dig wrote it, unaltered by this part, with the new fact standing beside it for Robert's ruling. An edition and a footnoted summary are not a translation of the tablet; but the desk that wrote "in whole or in part" should know precisely what Pitts's footnote does, and now it does, in the open.

**Second, the kinship degree.** Kleinerman's shared-title argument makes the two physicians brothers; Steinkeller, as quoted by Pitts, makes Nawir-ilum Šu-Kabta's close relative, uncle or brother. The difference is printed in the household section and is not resolved here; Wu Yuhong's 2008 study, which frames the family entire, could not be read openly for this part and might resolve it, and that road is named below.

**Third, the conveyor and the authorizer.** For AUCT 1, 241, the text's own function-word is *ĝiri3*, and Pitts and Tsouparopoulou render the role "conveyor"; Kleinerman describes Nawir-ilum as authorizing the delivery of the royal offerings. The two English words are not reconciled here; the Sumerian is given, and both renderings stand with their authors' names.

**Fourth, the second Nippur text.** Tsouparopoulou names Nawir-ilum and Šu-Kabta together as the physicians who acted as conveyors for the votive silver offerings to Nintinuga at Nippur, across TCL 2, 5550 (Šū-Suen 6) and AUCT 1, 241 (Šū-Suen 2), without assigning each text to each man; Kleinerman assigns TCL 2, 5550's authorization to Šu-Kabta. This part therefore counts AUCT 1, 241 as Nawir-ilum's and does not count TCL 2, 5550 for him, and the unassigned pairing in Tsouparopoulou's sentence is recorded rather than resolved in his favor.

**Fifth, the counts that could not be re-derived.** Kleinerman's fourteen doctor-accounts and seven Garšana mentions are her counts, made from the Garšana concordance; the copy of her article consulted here — a Scribd posting of the symposium paper — interleaves her footnotes imperfectly, and the full lists in her notes 6 and 14 could not be recovered entire. Nine of the fourteen and six of the seven are matched to tablets in this part's census; the remainders are carried as her counts, labeled as hers, and the census's own total of thirty-seven individually identified tablets is stated as a floor, not a sum of her figures. A related small variance: the grain account of Ubartum and Nawir-ilum is cited by Kleinerman as SAT 3, 1316 and stands in the First Dig's dossier as CUSAS 3, 1316; both designations are printed where the text is named, and the variance is the record's, not this part's to settle.
