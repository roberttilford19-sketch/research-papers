# Nawir-ilum — The Full Report

*Consolidated at Robert Tilford's order of 19 October 2026. This report gathers into one document everything the house's Nawir-ilum work has produced: the published Dig Two dossier on CUSAS 03, 0250 with its memorial part and its dated Pitts addendum; the companion memo on why the man mattered and on the neighbouring fragments, as repaired on 19 October 2026; the desk's verification of the Grok bot's "Nawir-" search report; and the desk-checked return of the Perplexity panel. It is a house document. It alters nothing that stands published, it proposes its changes in one place and as proposals only, and it moves nowhere until Robert rules on it.*

## 1. What this report is, and how it was made

The method of this report is Robert's own, named by him on the morning it was commissioned: aggregation. Fill out the picture, attribute every source, and pull it all together. Nothing in these pages is a discovery made by the hand that compiled them; every factual passage traces to a named researcher, a named tablet, or a named record, and the credit roll at the end gives each contributor his sentence. Robert's role is stated in his own framing, and it is not a small one: he is the one pulling it together, in this way — the pick of the tablet was his, the questions were his, the orders that sent each pass out were his, and the framing, the design, and the presenting of the whole are his. The discoveries belong to their researchers and are credited to them throughout. What the compiler adds is arrangement, checking, and prose — and the checking, as section 5 will show, cut in every direction, including against the house itself.

Two instruments of that checking deserve naming at the front, because the report's heart depends on them. The first is the house's own verification habit: claims are read against the fullest open text of the scholarship they rest on, verdicts are stated with their grounds, and a claim no road could reach is recorded as unverified rather than filled in from a paraphrase. The second is Robert's panel protocol: the same question put to independent engines, their answers compared by their reasoning and their grounds — never by counting votes. A Grok bot's stem search of the name, a desk verification of that search against the scholarship read entire, and a Perplexity verification run in its own thread and condensed by Robert's method into a six-page export he downloaded and carried back by hand: those three passes over the same eight claims are the spine of section 5, and their disagreements are printed where they stand, not smoothed.

One rider stands over the whole report, in Robert's own terms and by his standing instruction: the picture stays incomplete where the clay is silent, and the report says where. Nawir-ilum left no word of his own. What follows is a life assembled from other men's bookkeeping — from rosters, consignments, seals, and gift lists — and at every point where the bookkeeping stops, this report stops with it and names the silence.

## 2. The man and his world

His name is Akkadian, and it is a sentence: Nawir-ilum, "the god is radiant." The element *nawir* means "radiant, shining," as Audrey Pitts's onomastic survey of the period lists it among the name elements of the Ur III record. The scribes wrote the name more than one way — *na-we-er-dingir* on the dossier's tablet, *na-wi-ir-ilum* and *na-wi-ir-DINGIR* elsewhere — the final element, *ilum*, "god," written with the divine determinative in some witnesses and without it in others. The variation is orthography, not identity, and the identifications in what follows rest on the contexts the scholarship sets out — his titles, his household, his dog handler, his partner — and never on the name alone, since a name by itself, in an archive of some ninety-five thousand published tablets, proves nothing. That discipline matters more than usual in this report, because section 5 contains a live dispute over whether two tablets in his census spell a different name altogether.

Thirty-seven tablets that name him are identified individually, by their publication designations, in the scholarship the house has read. Six are Garšana tablets: the mourning twins CUSAS 3, 250 and CUSAS 3, 251; CUSAS 3, 270, which carries the seal of his partner Anaa; CUSAS 3, 982, which records his funerary offerings; CUSAS 3, 1116, which carries the seal of his servant, a scribe; and the grain account cited as SAT 3, 1316, in which the physician Ubartum is the sole recipient of grain provisions save for Nawir-ilum himself. Twenty-one belong to the dog archive of Puzriš-Dagan, the royal livestock agency at Drehem, enumerated in the tables of Christina Tsouparopoulou's study of the healing goddess, her dogs, and her physicians: seventeen lifetime records running from Amar-Suen 4 to the month of his death, three that name him after his death, and one undated summary. The remaining ten are the wider record of his life beyond Garšana and the kennels — a royal votive conveyed to Nippur, a ritual performed under the queen's courier's eye, two journeys to his city provisioned from the royal centre at Tummal, a commissionership for a sheep offered to Enki, two royal gifts, and the sheep accounts that include the two disputed BPOA texts. One further text stands behind a caution: Alexandra Kleinerman reports that he once delivered animals as a royal delivery in the company of a provincial governor, a prince, and Šu-Kabta himself, but the footnote citation for that text is only partly legible in the copy consulted — the designation Ontario 1, 127 stands in the footnote sequence at that point — and the tablet is therefore counted as a probable thirty-eighth, not a certain one. Against all these gatherings stand Kleinerman's own counts, and they are larger than any list this report can enumerate: she counts seven Garšana texts that mention him, where six are identified here, and she states that he is labelled explicitly as a doctor in fourteen accounts, of which nine can be matched to tablets in the census. The honest summary is the dossier's: thirty-seven tablets identified by designation in the sources read, thirty-eight counting the royal-delivery text whose designation is uncertain, and the true total higher than either figure, since Kleinerman's counts were made from the full Garšana concordance and could not be re-derived tablet by tablet from the open copies. Thirty-seven is a floor, not a ceiling.

He was a physician. That is the title the record gives him most often and most explicitly: *a-zu*, "doctor," in fourteen accounts by Kleinerman's count, spread across the archives of Garšana, Puzriš-Dagan, and Umma. What the title meant in his daily life the record does not show, and this report does not pretend otherwise. Kleinerman's survey of the period's physicians ends in a caution the house has adopted as its own: there is, so far, no evidence that any Ur III physician spent time practising medicine as a modern reader pictures it; what the texts show is men and one woman of considerable status, occurring in contexts with other high-ranking professionals. Of Nawir-ilum's healing — a patient treated, a remedy given, a cure wrought — not one line survives. His medicine is a title, a standing, and a goddess's service.

He held a second title, and a rare one: *sukkal i3-du8*, rendered "Secretary of the Doorkeepers." Kleinerman establishes it from the seals of his own household — the seal of Anaa, his partner, impressed on CUSAS 3, 270, styles him so, and the seal of his son Takil-ilissu gives the father the same title — and Tsouparopoulou, citing Wu Yuhong's 2008 study of the three men of this family across the three archives, likewise gives him both titles together. The secretaryship ran in the family: Tsouparopoulou, following Wu, records that his father Naram-ili bore the doorkeeper secretaryship before him, as his brother Šu-Kabta's seal proclaims it — "Šu-Kabta, the physician, son of Naram-ili, secretary of the doorkeepers," the legend as read on CUSAS 3, 220 — and her inference, printed as hers, is that in this house the two titles went together, the secretaryship of the doorkeepers and the physician's art travelling as one inheritance across two generations.

He was, for the crown's livestock agency at Puzriš-Dagan, the overseer — *ugula* — of the dog handlers of the healing goddess Gula. From the fifth year of Amar-Suen, the agency booked a regular consignment, some thirty animals a month, for Gula's cult at Ur, and the animals passed through the hands of dog handlers who received dead sheep for the goddess's dogs, under a named overseer; the overseer, from Amar-Suen 5 onward in the consignment series, is Nawir-ilum, and the consignments run in his name through two reigns. His dog handler, in text after text, is Puzur-Enlil: the CDLI catalogue's translation of Hirose 288 runs, "Under the authority of Puzur-Enlil, the shepherd of dogs, whose chief was Nawir-ilum." In BIN 3, 243 he himself conveys a year's account of the skins of the animals the dogs had eaten — 768 animals in the year's total as the table gives it — and in MVN 13, 422 his own seal closes a dog-consignment entry. He is, in Tsouparopoulou's showing, the first overseer of the corps who can be identified as a physician, and her article's thesis is that his tenure is the earliest documented association in Mesopotamia between the healing goddess, her dogs, and a physician — her priority wording, exactly, being that the article "presents the earliest documented association between the healing goddess, dogs and physicians in Mesopotamia." For the later benchmark she quotes Barbara Böck's summary of 2012, which dated the earliest attestation of the association to nineteenth-century BC Isin, where the ruler Enlil-bāni commemorated the building of a Dog Temple of Ninisina; measured at this desk, from his tenure to Enlil-bāni's reign (about 1860–1837 BC, middle chronology), the interval runs to roughly two centuries. She also records, for the priority's own history, that the Ur III association was first remarked already in 1992, by Sallaberger.

Two things he was not, on the evidence, and the report says them plainly because the questions arise naturally from the digs. He was not the foreman of the fullers: no text consulted titles him over the fuller crew or any craft crew, and the eighteen fullers of CUSAS 3, 250 were booked as a withdrawal of Šu-Kabta, under Ea-šar's commission, with Nawir-ilum standing in that tablet only as the occasion. And he is nowhere titled a general. Tsouparopoulou once remarks, in a footnote of her study of the army's dog handlers, that he "could well be a general as well," since he is attested in many texts as a doctor and his kinsman Šu-Kabta held both titles; that is her speculation, printed here as speculation. His son held the general's title, and his brother held it. He did not, so far as the record shows.

His remaining recorded functions are single acts, and they are the acts of a trusted man at the crown's business. In AUCT 1, 241 (Šū-Suen 2, month 7) he is the conveyor — the text's word is *ĝiri3*, "by the foot of" — of a royal votive of silver to the healing gods at Nippur: to Nintinuga, and, as Pitts's citation of the text gives the list, to Šumaḫ, Damu, and Gunura besides; Kleinerman describes the same act as his authorizing the delivery of royal offerings to Nintinuga and Damu, and the difference between "conveyor" and "authorizer" is a difference of the scholars' English for the one Sumerian function-word, recorded here rather than harmonized — it returns in section 5, where a search engine's garbling of this very act had to be corrected. Once, in CST 399 (Amar-Suen 9, month 11, day 23), he serves as commissioner, *maškim*, for a single sheep offered to Enki, the god of the incantations that medical practice used; Pitts notes the fitness of the assignment and says only that it perhaps increases the likelihood that this commissioner is the physician, and her "perhaps" is carried with the claim. And once, on Kleinerman's report, he delivered animals as a delivery of the king in the company of a governor, a prince, and Šu-Kabta — company that is itself a statement about where he stood.

The record gives him a household, and its outline is firmer than a memorial usually gets, though parts of it stand on inference and the inferences are labelled where they stand. His partner was Anaa. She is designated his *lukur* in MVN 11, 183 and on the evidence of her own seal on CUSAS 3, 270; Kleinerman calls her, with a scholar's care, his wife or mistress — a lukur was a cloistered priestess, a status normally bound to gods and kings, and the word itself is the record's, not this report's embroidery. In the text published as SMS 2–3, page 13, number 8 (Amar-Suen 9, month 4), Anaa, "lukur of Nawir-ilum the *a-zu*," received silver coils together with two boatmen, on the occasion of a ritual Nawir-ilum performed — the ritual's signs are partly illegible in the copy Pitts cites — and the release was authorized by Riṣ-ilum, the queen's mounted courier, acting as commissioner. Whatever he was doing that day, the crown was paying attention to it; that is Pitts's inference from the courier's presence, and it is a fair one, labelled hers.

He had a son: Takil-ilissu, a general. Kleinerman reports the filiation and the title, and reports further — citing Heimpel's Garšana volume at page 4, note 9 — that the son may have succeeded Šu-Kabta at Garšana after the great man's death. The son's own seal, like Anaa's, styles the father Secretary of the Doorkeepers: the household remembered his titles on its seals even where the accounts omit them.

He had a father, by the record's strongest inference. Šu-Kabta's seals name Šu-Kabta's father outright: Naram-ili. Nawir-ilum's seals do not name his. But Nawir-ilum held the same rare second title that Šu-Kabta held, and Wu Yuhong's study — as Tsouparopoulou reports its frame — treats Naram-ili himself as a bearer of that doorkeeper title. From the shared rare title and the shared household world, Kleinerman concludes that the evidence is strong that the two physicians were brothers. Piotr Steinkeller, as quoted by Pitts, calls Nawir-ilum "undoubtedly Šu-kabta's close relative (either his uncle or brother)." The kinship is therefore as certain as inference gets in this record — two scholars, independently framed, land on close kin — and the exact degree is not settled: brother, on Kleinerman's reading of the shared title; uncle or brother, on Steinkeller's. Both are printed; neither is crowned. If the brotherhood holds, his father was Naram-ili the doorkeeper, and the doorkeeper's title ran in the family like a trade. One caution rides with this paragraph and is developed in section 6: the companion memo of this same date, in its depth section, phrases the fatherhood in one sentence as though it stood booked in the seal legends' own words, and then in the same sentence carries it as inference still. The dossier's statement is the careful one — his seals do not name his father — and this report follows the dossier.

The household had servants and substance. Kleinerman records that he had a cook of his own, and that he purchased a slave for that cook — a man who buys a slave for his cook is a man with a table worth cooking for. His servant, a scribe, sealed tablets in his name at Garšana, and it is that servant's seal on CUSAS 3, 1116 that preserves his doctor's title in the home archive where the accounts themselves omit it; Kleinerman's statement of the mechanism is the census's own foundation: seven Garšana texts mention him, and "although they never mention his title, they certainly referred to Nawir-ilum, the a-zu. This is indicated by the seals of his servants on Garšana texts." Of Ubartum, the woman physician of the same Garšana generation, Kleinerman writes that it is tempting to speculate she was the two men's sister; the one hard datum beneath the speculation is the grain account in which she is the sole recipient save for Nawir-ilum. The speculation is printed as speculation, with its datum, and no more is claimed.

Standing in the Ur III record is not praised; it is booked. By that measure his was high, and the bookkeeping is specific. The crown gave him gifts, and the gift formula names them royal: five sheep in SANTAG 6, 228 (Šū-Suen 1), "a royal gift" in the text's own phrase as Pitts quotes it — "five sheep Na-wi-ir-DINGIR a-zu nig2-ba-lugal"; fruits and fish in RIAA 115 (Šū-Suen 3, month 9), likewise a royal gift; sheep in the two BPOA texts, which Kleinerman counts among the accounts that style him doctor. When he travelled — twice in three days in the eighth month of Amar-Suen 7 — he drew supplies for the journey to his city from the royal centre at Tummal, and the texts that record it style him *a-zu* as they provision him. He performed at least one ritual of sufficient moment that the queen's own mounted courier authorized the gifts that attended it. Steinkeller's general finding, as Pitts reports it, is that the Ur III physicians worked for the crown rather than for temples or private patrons, and frequently enjoyed high social status, and that Nawir-ilum fits the pattern; the record above is the pattern, item by item. The rarest mark of his standing is Anaa. Pitts's dissertation surveys the men of the period who had lukurs of their own, and the company is very small: apart from gods and deified kings, the men attested with lukurs are the general Ur-nigar, Arad-Nanna — the realm's second man, heir of the grand viziers — the governor Ur-Lisi, and, in her sentence, "the fourth man to have his own lukur was Nawir-DINGIR, a physician." A cloistered priestess-partner was an honour that tracked royalty and the highest office; that a physician stands fourth in such a list is the record's own testimony to where he stood, and it needs no adjective from this report. Pitts adds, labelling it herself as temptation, the speculation that the honour may have followed a successful exercise of his craft upon an ailing king or a member of the royal family. It is a beautiful guess and it stays a guess; the report prints it in her own framing and builds nothing on it.

He died in the tenth month of Šū-Suen's eighth year — ŠS 8, month 10, in the catalogues' assignment of the mourning twins' own month name and year formula, an assignment neither catalogue queries for CUSAS 3, 250 and both query for its twin, an asymmetry the dig carried openly and this report carries still. What followed the death, in the household and in the administration, is told at full length in the dossier and weighed in section 4 of this report; its skeleton belongs here. The household stood down two work gangs for a day of breast-beating, booked in the twin tablets that are section 3's subject. The administration, meanwhile, did not know — or did not say — for almost a year: Tsouparopoulou's 2014 note, "A note on Ur III administrative procrastination," and her later study establish from the dog archive itself that in Šū-Suen 8, month 12, MVN 13, 89 still books the Gula consignments under Nawir-ilum as overseer; that UDT 171 does the same in Šū-Suen 9, month 3, and PDT 1, 7 in Šū-Suen 9, month 9; and that only in Šū-Suen 9, month 10 — one year to the month after the death month — do AUCT 1, 224 and TSDU 94 enter a new overseer, Šu-Mamitum, in his place. A dead man's name thus closed the accounts of the goddess's dogs for a further year of bookings. Who Šu-Mamitum was, the record does not settle: a doorkeeper of that name is attested, and a carrier of animals of that name appears together with Šu-Kabta, and Tsouparopoulou raises — as a possibility only — that the doorkeeper-overseer may himself have been a physician, which would make the succession a physician's for a physician's; she also warns that Šu-Mamitum is "a common name, so that no specific individual can be easily identified," the record's own standing warning that a recurring name in this archive is usually another man. And the estate kept feeding his ghost: CUSAS 3, 982, dated to Ibbi-Suen 2, month 12 — more than three years after the death month — records sheep for his regular funerary offerings, the *sa2-du11 ki-a-nag*, the regular offerings at the funerary chapel. A *ki-a-nag* was not a pauper's portion; Pitts's survey of such funerary offering places notes that they are attested for kings, governors, priestesses, and their families, the common denominator being wealth and high status. His chapel offerings were still being booked when the dynasty itself had less than two decades to live.

Garšana around him was the estate of Šu-Kabta — general by his royal gift seal, physician by the same record that names his brother — and of Šu-Kabta's wife, the king's daughter Simat-Ištaran, who after his death managed the estate herself. Its economy was substantially cloth: Kleinerman records that the estate's textile industry was managed by a woman named Ataqqar, and the mourning tablets show the craft world whole — fullers who cleaned and thickened the woven cloth, women at the sesame press, leatherworkers, reed-workers, a potter, a smith, doorkeepers. Beyond Garšana stood the other half of his working life: the state livestock agency at Puzriš-Dagan and the cult of Gula at Ur. It is the earliest documented association, in Tsouparopoulou's showing, between a healing goddess, her dogs, and a physician — the pattern that later ages would carve in stone at Isin. Nawir-ilum stands at the head of that documentary line not because anyone praised him, but because the kennels' accounts were kept under his name for a decade.

What cannot be known must close this section, as it closes the dossier's memorial. It is not the case that no family survives: a partner, a son, a father by strong inference, a brother by contested degree — the record holds a household. What does not survive is everything else a life is made of. No birth is recorded, and no childhood, and no place he was born. No word of his own survives — not a letter, not a judgement, not a prescription; the seals that speak in his name speak the formulae of his household. No act of his medicine survives: no patient, no remedy, no cure. No cause of death is recorded, and no age at death; the career the tablets show runs from Amar-Suen 4 to Šū-Suen 8, some thirteen years of bookings, but a man is not thirteen years old when his bookings begin. No tomb is identified; the *ki-a-nag* that received his offerings is a word in an account, not a place on any map this report can point to. His face, his voice, his character, his faith — the record holds none of it, and this report invents none of it. What survives is a name that is a small sentence of praise, two titles, an overseership documented month by month for years, a partner whose status was itself an honour roll of four names, a son who bore the title his father never bore, a death fixed to its month in an age that did not record even its kings' death-days in the administrative hand — Pitts, following Hallo, notes that among the tens of thousands of Ur III administrative texts, not one gives the actual death date of any king — a mourning booked twice by two desks of one household, a name kept working the goddess's accounts for almost a year after the man was in the ground, and a funerary portion still delivered three years on. That is the whole of it, and it is not nothing.

## 3. The dig: CUSAS 03, 0250 and its twin

The Second Dig's object — Robert's pick — is a clay tablet from Garšana, 55 × 46 × 21 mm, still inside its clay envelope, the envelope's obverse repeating the tablet's obverse and bearing the impression of Šu-Kabta's royal gift seal (CDLI seal S006097). Its museum number is CUNES 49-15-337, formerly Cornell University; BDTNS now lists the Iraq Museum, Baghdad, the Garšana corpus having been returned to Iraq in August 2021. Its stable identifiers are CDLI P324343, BDTNS 163023, CUSAS 03, 0250. The text is a one-day labour-withdrawal record: eighteen fullers of the Garšana estate, graded in four rate classes, stood down for a day of breast-beating — the mourning rite — on the day recorded in its reverse line 1, the day Nawir-ilum died. Fifteen lines stand on the tablet, seven on the envelope's obverse, and the seal legend in two columns of four lines. The provenance caveat stands in the same words as the First Dig's: these tablets were looted at Garšana in antiquity's aftermath, their excavation context is lost, and their coherence as an archive is the editors' reconstruction.

The dig's basis is the transliteration published by Owen & Mayr in the primary edition (CUSAS 3, 2007 — transliteration and photograph, no translation), as carried in CDLI's catalogue record for P324343, its ATF revised across two decades of named hands, most recently Firth (2019) and Pagé-Perron (2024), and confirmed line by line against BDTNS record 163023 in normalized sign values. The photographs were studied directly: the CDLI record carries three — the tablet's faces, a high-resolution detail of the obverse at reading size, and the envelope's faces with the seal impressions rolled across them — so the dig does not rest on transliteration alone. What the photographs cannot do is replace the editors' collation: signs the ATF marks collated or damaged are reported with their marks, not re-read from the pictures by this desk.

Two residual checks the scout named were run to their ends. The first concerned renderings of the death line. Tsouparopoulou's CDLN 2014:9 note, read in full, establishes the death's date in these words: Nawir-ilum "died in Šu-Suen 8 x; see CUSAS 3, 251 rev. 3, and possibly also CUSAS 3, 250, on tablet Rev. 1." She therefore cites this tablet's reverse 1 by number as evidence for the death — and prints no transliteration of it and renders no line of it. The death line of this tablet has been pointed at in print, with a scholar's "possibly," and never translated. Her ZA 110 (2020) article quotes the twin's reverse 3 in transliteration and renders nothing. Heimpel's 2009 volume could not be re-read in full — no openly readable copy was located on the roads run — and the limit is stated, not hidden: his one published rendering in this dossier, at page 332 as quoted by Sallaberger (2011) note 30, attaches to the twin's reverse 3, the hard line, not to this tablet's plain one; Sallaberger's 2011 study, read in full text, treats the death expressly under "(no. 251)" and carries no rendering of this tablet; and neither catalogue's bibliography for P324343 lists any Heimpel item. The residual risk is exactly that wide and no wider: if Heimpel rendered reverse 1 somewhere inside his 2009 volume without the catalogues recording it, the roads available to this dig would not have caught it, and the dossier's label is worded to survive that possibility.

The second check settled the fuller count from the tablet itself. The transliteration's own structure shows four graded lines (obverse 1–4) predicated jointly by line 5, "they are fuller-men"; the fractions written in lines 2–4 — two-thirds, one-half, one-third of the labour rate each — are the descending grades of one crew, and line 1, carrying no fraction, is the ungraded head of the same series. The detail photograph confirms the physical record: the first line opens with the numeral 11, the following lines with 2, 4, and 1, and no total is written anywhere — the scribe counted classes. The eighteen are therefore 11 + 2 + 4 + 1 men, of whom the labour booked, at the written rates, is 11 + 2×(2/3) + 4×(1/2) + 1×(1/3) = 14⅔ day-rates — both figures computed from the list and labelled computed. One caveat rides with the settlement: line 1's final sign is collated in the ATF (*sag#-[tag]*) and the envelope's copy of the line restores its numeral in part; the numeral 11 itself is not the collated element and stands clear on the photograph.

The edition's collation note records where the two catalogue witnesses differ, and the differences are carried, not resolved. BDTNS agrees with the ATF in substance on every line of tablet and envelope; it differs in three particulars. It writes reverse 1's verb with its final vowel, *ba-uš2-a*, where the ATF brackets the verb without the final *-a*. It brackets the tablet's reverse more heavily than the ATF throughout — a more conservative statement of the same worn surface the photograph shows. And where the ATF marks the envelope's reverse simply "broken," BDTNS records a single envelope reverse line — the death line itself, heavily restored: *[u4 Na-wi-ir-DINGIR ba-uš2-a]*. On BDTNS's record, then, the death sentence stood twice on the sealed object, once on the tablet and once on its envelope; on CDLI's, the envelope copy is lost. The readings are not in conflict; the statements of preservation are, and both are carried.

The draft translation — labelled DRAFT on its face, derived line by line from the published transliteration, the two catalogue witnesses, the photographs, and the published dictionaries, with restorations flagged at the lines where they stand — reads straight through as follows. Obverse: "11 workers, of the sag-tag class [?]. 2 workers, each at a rate of 2/3. 4 workers, each at a rate of 1/2. 1 worker, at a rate of 1/3 each. The men are fullers. For 1 day, stationed to beat the breast." Reverse: "On the day Nawir-ilum died. Ea-šar was the commissioner. Withdrawal of Šu-Kabta. From Puzur-Akkum it was withdrawn. In Garšana. Month: 'Great Festival.' Year: 'The great barge was fashioned.'" The envelope repeats the tablet's obverse word for word, with the small variants the transliteration shows, and in its obverse 7 writes *gub-ba*, "stationed," in full where the tablet's copy is restored — the sealed duplicate supplying the tablet's loss, which is what a duplicate is for. The seal legend reads: "Šū-Suen, strong man, king of Ur, king of the four quarters — gave (this seal) to Šu-Kabta, the general, his servant." The translation's total length is the tablet's own: about ninety words of English for the tablet, forty for the legend.

The lines judged least certain are named in the dossier, and they are named here. Reverse 1 is not uncertain for its sense, which is plain, but for its state: the name's last sign and the verb are restored in the ATF, BDTNS brackets the line's envelope copy entire, and the restoration — though threefold-footed, on the surviving name stem, on the twin tablet where the same death is the same duty's occasion on the same date, and on Tsouparopoulou's citation of this very line as the death's evidence — is a restoration, and is marked as one everywhere it appears. Obverse 1's compound *sag-tag* is carried untranslated, because no compound gloss was retrievable from the dictionaries on the open roads run; its class sense — the crew's un-fractioned class, the men booked at the whole rate — is a labelled inference from its position at the head of the graded series. Reverse 8's year-formula verb is restored entire ([ba-dim2], "was fashioned"), and the twin's witness transmits a different verb for the same year name (ba-du3#, "was built," itself collated); both are printed as they stand, a variance recorded and not harmonized. Obverse 4's distributive *-ta* is editorially supplied, not written, on the witnesses as transmitted. And obverse 7's "beat" verb is read *sag3* by CDLI's ATF and normalized *sig3* by BDTNS — a disagreement of reading convention for one sign, not of sign identity, the translation unaffected; the breast-beating compound is not a dictionary-fixed idiom and its rendering stays literal.

The tablet dates itself by the month name "Great Festival" and the year formula "The great barge was fashioned." Both catalogues assign that combination to Šū-Suen year 8, month 10, and neither catalogue queries the assignment, where both query the twin's. The asymmetry is the catalogues' own, reported unexplained by the scout and reported the same way here. The assignment remains what it is — a catalogue inference from a month name and a year formula — and is cited as such: Šū-Suen 8, month 10, the catalogues' assignment, unqueried in both. Tsouparopoulou's dating of the death, "Šu-Suen 8 x," agrees. The archive's dated span runs 2031–2024 BC.

Set beside its twin, the pair documents a single occasion from two desks of one household. The twin, CUSAS 3, 251 — the First Dig's passed piece — stood down thirteen workers of mixed crafts: three women pressing sesame, one fuller, three leatherworkers, two reed-workers (the reed-workers' numeral queried in the edition, the least secure numeral on that tablet), one potter, one smith, and two doorkeepers; and it circled the death in a clause the scholarship still parses several ways. This tablet stood down the fuller crew entire, graded man by man, and said the thing plainly: on the day Nawir-ilum died. Between the two accounts, thirty-one worker-bookings across the two accounts were taken off their labour for the mourning — both figures computed from the lists, and with the overlap caveat the repaired companion memo established: both tablets book by craft and rate class, not by name, so whether the twin's one fuller stands among this tablet's eighteen cannot be settled, and if he does the distinct men numbered thirty. The twin's death clause itself — reverse 3 of CUSAS 3, 251 — has now been treated in print four ways, gathered in the dossier's memorial because the clause is this man's only epitaph. The First Dig's draft, adopted on contextual grounds and ranked by its verifier as the weakest of the three then on record, reads it as the day he was laid before the god. Heimpel, at page 332 of his 2009 volume as quoted by Sallaberger, reads the breast in the line: the day the breast of Nawir-ilum was made to settle by the god. Sallaberger himself, in his 2011 study at section 4.5.4, reads a god placed opposite Nawir-ilum, with his own question mark standing and his own verdict that the phrase is almost unintelligible. And Tsouparopoulou transliterates the line with the verb read as DUH — *u4 diĝir-re DUH na-wi-ir-DINGIR ba-a-ĝar-ra* — and renders it not at all, using it only to fix the death's date. Four treatments, none crowned here; the dispute itself is a kind of fame — scholars are still arguing, in print, over the grammar of the day this man died.

The primacy record for this tablet is restated in the dossier's Phase 4, on seven named roads: the CDLI record for P324343, read in full, whose complete bibliography is two entries — the primary edition and Mayr & Owen 2004, the royal-gift-seal study (HSAO 9, pages 145–174, citing pages 162 and 167 note 10), a study of the seal on this tablet's envelope, not an edition of its text — and whose translation facet is empty; the BDTNS record 163023, read in full, coding every item it lists — Mayr & Owen 2004 as edition (history, transliteration), Owen & Mayr 2007 as edition (transliteration), the CDLI record as a photograph resource, the ePSD2 record as a transliteration resource, Tsouparopoulou 2014 as a study — with no translation coded or linked anywhere; the ePSD2 resource itself, a transliteration carried as a dictionary corpus text; nine web searches under the designation, the museum number, and the content words, returning noise only; Sallaberger 2011, read in full text; Tsouparopoulou 2014, read in full; and Heimpel 2009, the one road that could not be walked to its end. ## 4. Why he mattered, the neighbouring fragments, and the Fragment Piles

The companion memo was commissioned to answer two questions and, at Robert's further order the same morning, a third: why Nawir-ilum mattered, with Robert's own reading of the funeral's scale tested fairly against the record; what neighbouring fragments might shed more light on his life; and whether he might be hiding in partial name forms, sorted into gated piles that assert no stitch. Its findings, as repaired after the Grok bot's audit of it, stand as follows.

Robert's reading — Tier 2, his — was that this was no ordinary funeral: a mass event, comparable to a public holiday. The record supports the first half of that reading in a bounded form and does not support the second half, and the bounds are worth stating exactly, because the same record that refuses the holiday also refuses the opposite shrinkage, the private family affair. What the Garšana archive itself preserves, in Pitts's catalogue of the period's breast-beating assignments, is three mourning accounts from a single year, Šū-Suen 8, and they form a ladder. In the seventh month, when the prince Šulgi-rama died, three male workers were stationed at breast-beating for one day (CUSAS 3, 246). In the tenth month, for Nawir-ilum, the household stood down two gangs: this dossier's tablet books its fuller crew entire, eighteen men in four graded rate classes, and its twin books thirteen workers of the mixed crafts — thirty-one worker-bookings across the two accounts, on the combined basis used throughout the memo, with the overlap caveat stated in section 3. In the eleventh month, for Šu-Kabta himself — the general, the physician, the princess's husband, the householder of the whole estate — the account stands down ninety-five full-time women weavers and fifteen more at a third rate for nine and a half days (CUSAS 3, 252; on the figure ninety-five, see the variance carried in section 6, where BDTNS's revised text reads ninety-seven). A prince of the blood drew three men for a day; the physician drew thirty-one worker-bookings for a day; the great householder drew a hundred and ten women, more or less, for over a week. Nawir-ilum stands on the ladder's middle rung — nearer the prince in size, nearer his brother in kind, since what was stood down for him was not a token party but two whole work gangs, entire, at the householder's expense.

Against the holiday reading, the wider record is plain in what it does not contain. No text found on the roads walked records a city-wide or realm-wide stand-down of labour for any death of the age, royal or common. Work at Garšana did stop, and not infrequently — Robert McC. Adams notes the project's suspensions of work for varying periods and a complete gap in its dated texts across one stretch (CDLJ 2010-2, §3.3, on Heimpel's calendar) — but the stops the record names are festivals, weather of administration, and deaths handled exactly as this one was handled: as booked mourning assignments inside one estate's accounts. Even a king's death enters this archive in the same small grammar: Adams, summarizing Heimpel's pages 332–333, writes that "the deaths of Šu-Kabta and Šu-Suen, for example, found rather laconic acknowledgement in the commissioning of breast-beaters to circulate by boat on nearby waterways, presumably as a rite of mourning" (CDLJ 2010-2, §6.6). Šu-Suen was the king; his death — some thirteen months after Šu-Kabta's by the usual placement, the king dying at the end of his ninth year, and no Ur III king's death-date fixed in the record — was acknowledged at Garšana by breast-beaters on boats, the same rite, in the same ledger hand, with no holiday declared anywhere the record reaches. For the earlier kings the pattern is the same in kind: what the record holds for the deaths of Šulgi and Amar-Suen is funerary offering and literary lament, not a public stand-down. A holiday is a thing the Ur III record knows how to book, in its festival calendars; it books no holiday for any man's death. The verdict, in the memo's one sentence: the record supports a full formal mourning of the estate — the largest observance the archive preserves for anyone who was not the householder himself, mounted by the household that employed hundreds and stood beside a royal garrison of some thirteen hundred soldiers (Adams, CDLJ 2010-2, §§3.1, 4.1) — and it does not support a public holiday of the city or the realm, for him or for anyone. Robert's instinct that this was no ordinary funeral is borne out; the ordinariness it escapes is the token party of three men that a prince received. What it does not escape is the estate's walls. His funeral was public to the world that knew him, and that world was Garšana.

The depth of the standing was tested item by item, and four items deepened materially; they stand in section 2 in their places — the dog corps as a standing state charge rather than a household kennel, its tenure the earliest documented association of the healing goddess, her dogs, and a physician; the doorkeeper secretaryship running in the family across two generations; and the funerary chapel, whose company in Kleinerman's Drehem note is Šulgi's libation place and Šu-Suen's — both kings named plainly, the company being the Drehem record's overall and not a grouping of the funeral year, since Šu-Suen was still on the throne when Nawir-ilum died and his own libation place belongs to the record after his death. The fourth deepening cuts against romance, which is why it belongs in the stack. The year's vacancy — the dead overseer's name closing the dog accounts for a year — was Tsouparopoulou's 2014 question before it was this house's, and she offers the record two readings and no third: either the news travelled slowly through the administrative sphere, or the succession itself took longer because no replacement stood ready. On either reading her conclusion is the same, and it is bracing: the accounting machinery ran for a year on a dead man's name because the form required an overseer and the administration supplied the one it had, dead or not. She calls it, in so many words, a fabrication of an overseer, and draws the moral that for the continuing functioning of government the overseers were not so important. The honest weight of the vacancy is therefore double, and both halves are carried in this report: it does not show a realm holding its breath for an irreplaceable man — the scholar who mined the archive reads it as bureaucratic stiffness, even procrastination, her title says so — and it does show the stiffness itself working in his favour for a year, his name remaining the name the state's forms required; institutions, as the companion memo infers and labels inference, do not keep the names of nobodies on their forms for a year out of inertia alone. Neither reading is crowned.

Was he beloved? The companion memo answers in tiers, in so many words, because the record books one kind of thing and is silent on the other, and confusing them is the one error that memo exists to prevent. Institutional honour is bookable, and his was large. Personal affection is not booked anywhere, and no tablet in his record books it. Between those two sentences lies everything that can honestly be said. For the leaning toward something warmer than honour, four items stand. The strongest is new in the companion pass: in Šū-Suen 5, three years before his death, a boat on the estate's waterways bore his name — CUSAS 3, 220 records three fullers withdrawn for two and a half days to haul "the boat Nawir-ilum" from Nippur to the E2-dŠu-dSuen, the withdrawal booked as Šu-Kabta's, drawn from Puzur-Akkum's charge, and sealed with Šu-Kabta's own kinship seal; a boat carrying a living subordinate's name, hauled by his brother's booked labour on the estate's business, is the closest thing the ledger world affords to a public compliment, with the caveat riding with it that a boat-name is a namesake attestation, not an appearance of the man, and whether the boat was named for him cannot be decided from the text. The second item is a scholar's judgement, printed as one: Kleinerman, as the dossier reports her, reads the mourning assignment itself as showing the high esteem in which Nawir-ilum was held. The third is the chapel: three years and more of funded offerings, expenditure being the ledger's only vocabulary for regard. The fourth is the scale itself, honestly bounded: thirty-one worker-bookings against the prince's three. Against the leaning, the counter-evidence stands in the same ledger and is not wished away: the mourning is booked as labour accounting, a withdrawal drawn from a charge, in the same grammar as the boat journeys and the mill rosters — the tablet records a cost, not a grief; the year's posthumous overseership is read by the scholar who knows the archive best as procrastination and fabrication; no lament for him survives, no hymn, no letter, no first-person word of any kind; and the scale itself tracks rank as much as love, his brother's mourning dwarfing his precisely as a householder's should dwarf a household officer's, which is the arithmetic of hierarchy, not of hearts. The section's verdict is its opening two sentences, and nothing warmer is crowned. He was honoured institutionally at a level the record usually reserves for royalty's kin. Whether he was beloved, the tablets do not say; they say only that the people who kept his chapel funded, who named a boat in his name's keeping, and who stood a crew down entire on the day he died, behaved in every way their bookkeeping could express as men behave toward a man who mattered to them.

The neighbouring fragments were gathered from the catalogue neighbourhood of the twins, walked in BDTNS by record number — six records read in the stretch around the dossier tablet's own record, of which BDTNS 163023 is the dossier tablet's own, four are catalogue neighbours of the cluster, and one, BDTNS 163026, is a neighbour by record number only, a fact that is itself one of the pass's findings: BDTNS's numbers run in entry order, not catalogue order, as BDTNS 163026 — CUSAS 3, 622, two women taken in charge, Šū-Suen 7 — proved by sitting in number between the mourning records. The nearest new neighbour is the boat tablet, CUSAS 3, 220 (CDLI P324339; BDTNS 162965), already put to work above; its seal is the kinship seal itself, carrying a small open variance in its second legend — the CUSAS 3 heading gives the servant Šu-Eštar as "the courier" for texts 220, 221, 224, and 584, while the volume's own seal catalogue at page 436 reads "Šu-Eštar the scribe," and BDTNS follows the catalogue, as Manuel Molina's remark on the record says. The second neighbour is the seal-mate, CUSAS 3, 223 (CDLI P324344; BDTNS 163024), an envelope whose two *sag-tag* workers and a third at two-thirds rate are booked for a boat journey from Garšana to Nippur and back, a withdrawal of Šu-Kabta's from Puzur-Akkum's charge, sealed with the very royal gift seal that seals the mourning tablet; BDTNS's bibliography for this record cites Mayr and Owen 2004 at "p. 163, 167 no. 10," coded as a seal reference — the same page-and-number citation that attaches to the mourning tablet itself, coded there as an edition, a pair of codings whose meaning section 5's Pettinato resolution unfolds. The third neighbour is a working tablet of the same purse, CUSAS 3, 224 (CDLI P324340; BDTNS 163017), dated Šū-Suen 5, month 11: a fuller withdrawn from Puzur-Akkum for four days, fixing the pattern the mourning tablets also obey — the fullers of Garšana were drawn, man by man and day by day, from Puzur-Akkum's charge, in peace years as in the death month; the mourning withdrawal is the same administrative act at eighteen times the head-count, and in labour eighteen man-days against his four, four and a half times the size — both measures printed, as the bot audit required. The fourth neighbour is the Šu-Kabta mourning tablet itself, CUSAS 3, 252 (CDLI P324729; BDTNS 163025), read in full in the companion pass: ninety-seven women of the head-cloth class on BDTNS's revised reading and fifteen at a third rate, "the women are weavers," with a third class following whose count is lost — so many women, workers in flax — stood nine and a half days to beat the breast "on the day Šu-Kabta died," Ea-šar commissioner, drawn from Iškur-illat's charge on the tablet and from Ašta-kar's on the envelope, at Garšana, Šū-Suen 8, its seal a woman's royal gift seal whose legend survives in part: "…his beloved sister, his *lukur*, has given as a gift" — the princess's seal. The Festschrift web reaches further than the pass could read: the CDLI records for CUSAS 3, 227 (P324342) and CUSAS 3, 650 (P324341) both carry history references to HSAO 9 at pages 163 and 167 note 10 — 650 also at page 170 note 10 — so the seal's attestation web, as the Festschrift article spins it, includes at least those two further tablets, neither of which the pass could read in text, and the Festschrift volume itself was not openly readable; that nil is stated, not filled.

For Garšana in Šū-Suen 8, months 7 to 12, the gathered set stands as the dossier left it, and the companion pass added no new tablet to it: month 7 holds the prince's mourning (CUSAS 3, 246), month 10 the twins, month 11 the householder's mourning, and for months 8, 9, and 12 no further Garšana text was found on the roads walked — the BDTNS record-number walk, the CDLI artifact pages adjoining the dossier tablet's (which proved to run in no catalogue order), and the general web searches, which returned noise. The nil is honest and bounded: the CUSAS 3 volume's own chronological index, which would answer the question directly, is among the indices not openly readable in the pass, and Heimpel's construction archive was reachable only through Adams's summary, on whose report significantly less than a quarter of the days' records for Šū-Suen 8 have been recovered at all. What months 8, 9, and 12 held at Garšana, the open roads do not say.

Of the five household members the companion order named, one silence broke, two trails deepened, and two silences are re-recorded as silences. Puzur-Enlil is the broken silence, and his trail, assembled from Tsouparopoulou's ZA 110 study where the memorial carried none, runs as follows. He was a dog handler — *sipa ur-gi7-ra*, handler of Gula's dogs — at Puzriš-Dagan, and he came to the work by inheritance: a seal impressed on a tablet from Adab dated Šū-Suen 6 (MVN 3, 271) styles him son of Ilum-bāni, himself a known dog handler of the preceding generation. He replaced the handler Išme-ilum in the consignments sometime between Amar-Suen 5 month 6 and Amar-Suen 7, under the overseer Nawir-ilum — the Išme-ilum transactions begin at RA 9, 55 = SA 228, Amar-Suen 5 month 6 day 26. In Amar-Suen 9 month 9 he appears at Umma beside his overseer, the two of them receiving a *gur* of semolina as feed for the dogs (UTI 4, 2409; the text is uncollated, and Tsouparopoulou flags the semolina as strange fodder — both cautions are hers and ride with the datum). He is booked under Nawir-ilum's supervision until Šū-Suen 9 month 10 — which is to say, on Tsouparopoulou's own showing, under a dead man's name for the year's vacancy entire; the working foreman of the dog corps through the procrastination year was Puzur-Enlil. And if he is the same Puzur-Enlil who disburses animals in other Drehem texts — the identification is Tsouparopoulou's question, not her assertion — then he did not cease at the succession: in Ibbi-Suen 2 a Puzur-Enlil is responsible for expenditures for the cult of the gods of Gula's own circle, Ninisina, her son Damu, her husband Pabilsaĝ, and others (PDT 1, 310). Ea-šar deepens by one small, exact datum: on the Šu-Kabta mourning tablet, the envelope styles him "Ea-šar, the scribe, commissioner," while the tablet inside styles him commissioner only — the mirror image of the twins' variance, where the styles divide the other way; across the three mourning accounts of Šū-Suen 8, the chief commissioner's scribal title appears and disappears by copy, not by occasion, and the man who ran all three funerals of that year was the estate's scribe-commissioner. Puzur-Akkum deepens as the purse: read in three further tablets beyond the mourning pair — the boat of CUSAS 3, 220 drawn from his charge in Šū-Suen 5, the fuller of CUSAS 3, 224 withdrawn from him for four days, and the boat crews of CUSAS 3, 223 booked from his charge under the royal gift seal — the disbursing officer from whose account the estate's labour was drawn, in the peace years and in the death month alike; no title for him was found, and none is supplied. Anaa's silence is re-recorded: no new attestation of the lukur was found on the roads walked — not in the BDTNS walk, not in the web searches, not in Tsouparopoulou's studies, where she does not appear; her record stands exactly where the dossier left it, with the companion memo adding the two further Garšana tablets the dig's pass identified (CUSAS 3, 380 and 589). Takil-ilissu's silence is likewise re-recorded: the general, the son, appears in no text the pass opened, his attestation stands on Wu Yuhong's study as the scholarship relays it, and the trail's next step is the same locked door as his father's biography — JAC 23 (2008), pages 113–134, not openly readable on the roads available here.

The physical archive must be stated plainly, because it governs every cluster claim this report makes. Garšana's tablets were never excavated. They surfaced through the antiquities market, and everything said about "the Garšana archive" must be discounted by that fact before it is believed. The discovery history, in the editor's own words (Owen, in the openly posted *Signs Before the Alphabet* catalogue excerpt): the archives "were first identified in the late 1990's by Dr. Rudi Mayr, assistant curator at the Rosen Collection in New York City," and consist of some 1,400 tablets spanning twenty years of the Ur III dynasty's final decades; "as with the majority of cuneiform texts from the Third Dynasty of Ur, the Garshana Archives are devoid of an excavated context," and the place itself — "somewhere in the Sumerian province of Umma," west of the provincial capital and nearer Zabalam, on Heimpel's internal evidence as Adams reports it — has never been located. The tablets were kept for a generation at Cornell University; a remark that Manuel Molina's BDTNS revisions append to the Garšana records states the sequel in one breath: "Tablet formerly kept in Cornell University, Department of Near Eastern Studies. Presumably returned to Iraq in August 2021," with Cornell's public statement of 4 August 2021 and the *New York Times* report of 3 August 2021 cited for the return — some 1,400 tablets among a larger repatriation — as material assumed looted from Iraq after 1991. BDTNS's collection field for the tablets read in the companion pass now reads Iraq Museum, Baghdad, the museum number line blank where the CUNES number stood. What this does and does not let anyone claim about "the cluster" around Nawir-ilum is then plain. The cluster — the thirty-seven tablets of the census, gathered from Garšana, Puzriš-Dagan, Umma, and Adab — is not a shelf anyone found. It is a reconstruction, woven by the editors and the scholars from internal evidence — names, seals, dates, accounts, and hands — across tablets that the market scattered. The weave is strong where its threads are seals and dated accounts, and it is honestly a weave: a tablet surfacing tomorrow in a sale room or a drawer could add a thirty-eighth attestation, move a date, or break an identification, and nothing in the cluster's nature forbids it. What the market origin does not do is weaken what the tablets say once read — a looted tablet's text is not less true than an excavated one, only less accompanied. The cluster licenses claims about the man's record, tablet by tablet, each with its source. It licenses no claim of completeness, no claim about what the unrecovered three-quarters of Garšana's days contained, and no crown of any kind.

The Fragment Piles were Robert's further order: could this man's story be older — a retelling — and could he be hiding in partial name forms, a fragment beginning NA-WI-IR with the ending lost, or ending in -ILUM with the beginning lost, that patient stitching could associate with him? The piles answer with their gates printed first, and the gates govern every pile: no stitch is asserted anywhere; a Pile 2 or Pile 3 item is a candidate for a future collation against a photograph, nothing more. The sweep was run for the stems *na-wi-ir-* and *na-we-er-* with any ending or none, for the broken writings, and for endings *-ilum* / -DINGIR whose preceding signs are broken, across the roads the pass could walk: the BDTNS record walk and its full texts; the CDLI artifact pages adjoining the dossier tablet's number; Tsouparopoulou's two studies, the second resting on her own mining of the relevant Puzriš-Dagan texts — a stem sweep of the dog archive in scholarly form; Kleinerman's and Pitts's treatments as the dossier carries them; and full-text web searching on the name and its transliterated stems. Pile 1 — SECURE — is the thirty-seven attestations of the census, carried as the sweep's baseline and not re-listed; count 37, the dossier's figure, unchanged by anything in the piles. Pile 2 — PROBABLE — holds partial names, or names in transferred use, with the context gates passed: right archive or site, date inside or adjoining Amar-Suen 4 to Ibbi-Suen 2, his circle or his titles in the text. Two candidates stand in it. The stronger is the boat of CUSAS 3, 220: the name is whole but in transferred use, a vessel's name, not the man acting, which is why it stands in this pile and not in Pile 1; the gates all pass on the tablet's own text; and its limit is printed with it — a boat-name could in principle be theophoric without reference to the man, no text found says for whom the boat was named, and the tablet is counted nowhere in the census. It may also be, wholly or in part, the seventh Garšana mention of Kleinerman's count; that possibility is recorded, not decided. The second candidate is "Garšana 1130," admitted on Tsouparopoulou's citation alone: "One can also find a Nawir-ilum in a text from Garšana (Garšana 1130), dated to ŠS.05, in connection with textiles associated with Gula" — the gates pass on her description, but the desk has not seen the tablet, its name-form is unverified, and the designation belongs to Heimpel's numbering of the construction archive, a corpus the pass could not open; it stands as a candidate for collation, counted nowhere, and it may be the unrecovered seventh of Kleinerman's count or an eighth attestation beyond it — the two candidates cannot both be the seventh unless her count and Heimpel's text coincide in ways the open roads cannot show, and the pile leaves the arithmetic open exactly there. Pile 3 — POSSIBLE — stands empty, count 0, and its headnote is its content: the stem sweep that would fill or empty this pile properly cannot be run from the open roads — the CDLI search interface, tried twice, returned its unfiltered facet dump both times and answered no name query; the BDTNS records are walkable only by entry numbers that run in no catalogue or prosopographic order; the ePSD2 and ORACC onomastic resources, where a broken-name search would properly live, were not walked and are named as an unwalked road. What the partial sweep did run surfaced no broken Nawir-stem that is not already inside a Pile 1 tablet. One near-miss is recorded so that no later reader mistakes it for a find: the web's full text offers *na-wi-ir-tam*, "a light-coloured (slave girl)," in an Old Babylonian letter as Gelb quotes it — the adjective *nawirtam*, from the same root of shining as the man's name, and no person at all. The pile stands open; its emptiness is a statement about the roads, not about the clay. Pile 4 — NAMESAKES AND EXCLUSIONS — counts earlier or other bearers found: 0, after research on the roads named. No bearer of the name earlier than the physician, and no contemporary namesake in a different circle, was found in anything the passes read: not in Tsouparopoulou's mining of the dog archive, not in Kleinerman's concordance-based counts, not in Pitts's onomastic survey (which lists *nawir* among the age's name elements — the element is common coin; the full sentence-name, on present evidence, is his alone in the record), and not on the web's full text. The exclusions the record does offer are the adjective *nawirtam* — a word, not a man — and the Old Akkadian personal name Ur-Gula, "man of the goddess Gula," in CUSAS 20, 40, noted by Tsouparopoulou in passing: an older name built on his goddess's name, borne by another man in another age — kinship of devotion, not identity, and not his name. The retelling question itself then answers as the memo answers it: no earlier tale, lament, hymn, or namesake stands behind Nawir-ilum in anything found; his story is not a retelling in any direction the evidence reaches; and if a pattern in his dossier repeats across the centuries, it runs the other way — the association of the healing goddess, her dogs, and her physician, which Böck's 2012 summary dated earliest to nineteenth-century BC Isin, is documented first in his lifetime's records, in his own tenure of the dog corps. On present evidence he is not the copy of an older figure; he is, in this one thread of the record, the original the later centuries echo — a sentence bounded like every other in the memo: it claims the documented priority of an association, after research, on the roads named, and it crowns nothing else.

The tablet's own text is a Garšana regular-offering account (sa2-du11) of that month, sealed with the son's gift seal. **CUSAS 3, 270 (the Anaa tablet of the Garšana six).** The tablet is CDLI P324077 (CUNES 49-15-071). Its page dates it to Ibbi-Suen 1, month 12, day 15.
