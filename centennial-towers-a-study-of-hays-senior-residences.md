## 1. The land

Centennial Towers stands on a lot of 164,657 square feet — 3.78 acres — platted as part of the Centennial Estates 6th Addition & Replat, assessor's parcel number 138-34-0-20-02-009.00 (the parcel figures in this report are county-record-derived — aggregator tier, one step below the county's own record card; see the references). The subdivision's name is the parcel's one piece of readable history: the land was laid out as part of a staged development called Centennial Estates, which fits, in name at least, a building called Centennial Towers raised in 1977. No document found names the subdivider, and the fit is recorded as consistency, not as proof of a shared developer.

What the land was before 1977 is not known. No prior-use record surfaces in any public source consulted. The two records that would tell that story are the plat itself, at the Ellis County Register of Deeds, and the newspaper and aerial record of 1976–78. Until one of them is read, the land's earlier life — farm, pasture, or something else — is a blank this report declines to fill.

What can be measured is how lightly the building sits on its land. The building's footprint is about 16,600 square feet on a lot of 164,657, which is a lot coverage of about ten percent, and its floor-area ratio is about 0.40. Roughly nine-tenths of the parcel is open: lawns, drives, a patio, shuffleboard grounds, and surface parking for 150 cars, about 1.67 spaces per home. On raw area, the lot could physically hold a great deal more building. What it could lawfully hold is a narrower question, taken up in Section 11.

## 2. The raising, 1977

The building went up in 1977, in the middle of the federal government's great era of building housing for older Americans: four stories, ninety homes, a concrete frame wearing a brick skin, built to be lived in by people over fifty-five for the rest of the century and beyond. Every current listing of the property still gives 1977 as its year, and nothing in the record contradicts it.

It was built the way its program built everywhere in those years: a double-loaded corridor, repeated one- and two-bedroom plans stacked floor over floor, a community room and laundry and an office at the ground level, and structure meant to be permanent. The arrangement is the type's standard one — the community room and the laundry are in this building's own listings — and no plan of this building's floors is in the public record. Published descriptions of the building call it a concrete-and-brick structure, and the construction the record best supports — a cast-in-place concrete frame with a brick veneer that carries nothing but itself — is taken up in the next section, where it decides the arithmetic.

Who drew it is not known. No public source found names the architect of record, the original developer, or the builder of this building — not a listing, not the press coverage of its sister property, not any directory searched. The search that establishes that emptiness, and the single document most likely to end it, are set out in Section 10.

## 3. The building by the numbers

Ninety homes in four stories. One-bedrooms of 561 square feet; two-bedrooms of 759. Gross floor area 66,426 square feet on a footprint of about 16,600. Independent living for adults fifty-five and over — not assisted living and not nursing care, whatever the headings on listing websites say. Rent is set at thirty percent of a household's income, utilities included, under the subsidy structure described in Section 4.

**The bricks.** No record counts a building's bricks; the figure has to be derived, and the derivation is the kind any reader can check. From the footprint, the perimeter runs about 550 to 590 feet; at a structural height of about 36 to 39 feet the gross wall is roughly 21,400 square feet; taking out eleven to fifteen percent for windows and doors leaves about 18,600 square feet of brickwork; at the standard six and a half modular bricks to the square foot, the wall holds **on the order of 120,000 face bricks — defensibly between about 100,000 and 140,000**. The estimate assumes a single wythe of facing brick over the frame; any block backup behind it is uncounted, and a bare exact figure, here or anywhere, would be fabrication.

**The concrete.** The same open method, applied to the frame. Three elevated floors of slab at 7 to 8 inches thick come to roughly 1,180 to 1,350 cubic yards with their beams and drop panels; the roof deck and the ground slab add about 515 to 615; columns, the elevator and stair cores, and the foundations add the rest. The total is **an estimate, and is labelled an estimate wherever it appears: about 2,000 to 2,500 cubic yards of concrete — roughly 4,100 to 5,100 tons, with a central figure near 2,270 cubic yards and 4,600 tons**. A cross-check keeps it honest: the central figure works out to about 0.92 cubic feet of concrete per square foot of floor area, squarely inside the published rule-of-thumb band of 0.7 to 1.1 for concrete-frame residential construction.

One honest fork stands in the arithmetic. If the county's record card were to show that the walls, not a frame, carry this building — load-bearing masonry with precast plank floors — the concrete falls to roughly 1,100 to 1,600 cubic yards, about 2,200 to 3,200 tons, and the two estimates do not overlap. The structural-system line on the Ellis County Appraiser's property record card, which codes the building's structure in so many words, is the paper that selects between them, and until it is read the frame estimate stands as the better-supported reading, not as a settled fact.

## 4. The second birth, 1998

The building's first life ran from 1977 to 1998. Its second began on 1 August 1998, the formation date of **Hays Senior Residences, L.P.**, the Kansas limited partnership that has owned it ever since. In 1998 the partnership acquired the building and rehabilitated it under federal Low-Income Housing Tax Credits allocated by the Kansas Housing Resources Corporation (KHRC). The state's official development list carries the line to this day: Centennial Towers, 2502 Sherman — 1998 — housing tax credits — acquisition and rehabilitation — senior — completed. The credits recorded against the project were $93,000; the rehabilitated building was placed in service in 1999.

A limited partnership formed on a single date, holding a single property, administered from its manager's office, is not an oddity in this record. It is the standard legal shape of a tax-credit property: the credits are delivered through a partnership so they can flow to the investors who fund the work, and the partnership exists for the property and ordinarily for nothing else. This partnership's federal registration — required because it receives federal housing assistance — is current, most recently renewed in May 2026 and running to May 2027. Its mailing address on that registration is its manager's office in Wichita. A family of such partnerships, one per property, administered from one office, is what the record shows across the manager's Kansas portfolio.

Since 1998 the building has been carried by two programs layered together. Section 8 Project-Based Rental Assistance pays the difference between what each household pays — thirty percent of its income, utilities included — and the contract rent, on all ninety homes. The tax-credit covenants recorded against the land hold the homes to low-income use for a thirty-year term that runs, on the public record, to approximately 2029. Day-to-day management is by Key Management Company of Wichita. The building is privately owned; it is not public housing, and it is not run by the Hays Housing Authority. In the federal records the same address is carried under the project name Hays Senior Residences, classified as insured and subsidized.

Two records in this section deserve their flags in the open. The state list counts the 1998 project as 78 total homes, 56 of them tax-credit homes and 22 market-rate; the federal tax-credit database counts 90 total homes and 56 low-income; every current source counts ninety homes standing today. The figure 56 agrees everywhere; the totals do not, and no public record reconciles them. KHRC's 1998 project file in Topeka is where the answer lives, and any future application should reconcile the counts first, because unit count drives credit sizing. And the 1998 rehabilitation's scope and budget — what was renewed, what was left, what it cost — are published nowhere. That silence matters, and it returns in Section 7, because what the second birth renewed is exactly what decides how much of 1977 survives inside the walls today.

## 5. The roof

The building today wears a pitched roof — an on-site observation reported to this research, and one any passer-by can repeat. A 1977 building of this type would originally have been capped flat; everything else about the cap is harder. No public source found names the cap, dates it, or describes its construction. The original roof's flatness is itself an inference — an era-typical one, since four-story federally financed senior buildings of 1977 were near-universally built with flat built-up or membrane roofs — and it is labelled an inference here rather than promoted to a fact.

The candidate windows for the work are the 1998–99 rehabilitation and a later stand-alone re-roofing. A pitched cap over a flat deck is the standard answer to a chronic flat-roof history — ponding water, membrane failure, recurring leaks — and that motive is a reasonable inference for a building of this age and type, but for this building it is an inference, not a documented diagnosis. The records that hold the answer are named and ordinary: the KHRC project file's scope of work would show whether a roof cap was part of the 1998 rehabilitation; the City of Hays building-permit file for this address would carry a re-roofing permit if one was pulled later; the county appraiser's record card codes the roof structure and material the county believes is up there now. Until one of those is read, the cap is present and undated, and any account that assigns it a year is inventing one. Its construction type, its venting, and the condition of the original deck beneath it are documented nowhere public, which is why the roof belongs on the needs list in Section 12.

## 6. The bones

What the construction buys, stated without borrowing any romance, is considerable. A concrete-framed, brick-skinned building is noncombustible construction — in the modern building code's terms it sits in the Type I and Type II family, in which the frame, the floors, and the walls are of materials that do not burn. Two working principles follow. The structure does not burn: a fire in this building is a fire in its contents, not in its frame, and the frame's endurance is measured in hours. And the building is compartmented: concrete floors and fire-resistive partitions between homes are designed so a fire is contained to the home it starts in — subject to doors and openings, which is the honest qualification, because a compartment is only as closed as its doors, its penetrations, and its duct connections. It should also be said that at four stories and roughly thirty-six to thirty-nine feet, this is not a high-rise in the code sense, and nothing here borrows high-rise rules that do not apply to it.

The building has already demonstrated the principle and its limit in a single, documented event. In January 2013 the Hays Fire Department answered a fire alarm at Centennial Towers in the early morning; per the Hays Post's archive, an elevator motor had produced smoke in the corridors. There was no fire spread. The building's response was to move its people to the community room while the smoke was cleared. The frame and the compartments were never the issue. The smoke was the event.

Residents of the building relay that the city's fire chief has told them, in a safety briefing, that this is one of the most rock-solid buildings in the city. No public source found corroborates the briefing or the words, so that verdict is carried here exactly as what it is — a judgement relayed by the people who live with the building, consistent with everything the public record says about its construction type, and not confirmed as an official statement. What the record offers on its own is narrower and still worth having: noncombustible, compartmented concrete construction is, as a class, the most fire-forgiving ordinary construction there is.

## 7. The systems, and the calendar inside them

A frame built to outlast its century is only half a building. The other half is the systems hung on it — pipes, wires, plant, elevators, windows — and systems run on a calendar that does not negotiate.

The clearest case is the water piping. Published service-life references place galvanized steel water-supply piping at forty to fifty years of service. A system installed in 1977 stands at year forty-nine in 2026 — at the top of its published range — and systemic corrosion at that age is the expected condition of such a system, not an exceptional one. Full repiping is a standard capital-needs line for a building of this vintage.

Two honest qualifications ride with that paragraph, in the same breath. First, expectation is not inspection: no public source reports the condition of this building's pipes, and this report asserts nothing about them beyond what the calendar and the references support. Second, the 1998 rehabilitation's unpublished scope is the missing fact — how much original 1977 piping survives depends on what the second birth renewed, and the document that answers it is KHRC's 1998 project file. The engineering expectation stands for the vintage; the building's own answer waits for the assessment described in Section 12.

The same calendar logic runs through the rest of the plant, and the town holds a worked example of it. This building's sister tower across Hays — the subject of Section 9 — underwent a full tax-credit renovation in 2019–21 whose published scope reads like a census of 1970s systems coming due at once: windows, elevators, heating and cooling plant, the sewer system, a generator, and every unit's interior. That scope is not evidence about this building, and it is not borrowed as such. It is offered as an analogue checklist, because buildings of the same program and near the same age tend to come due for the same systems in the same decades — and because it proves the systems can, in fact, be renewed wholesale when someone applies for the money.

What cannot be reported is this building's official condition grade, because it was not retrieved. The federal housing department inspects its assisted properties and publishes physical inspection scores; the route to this building's score runs through the National Housing Preservation Database, and no score is quoted here rather than estimated, inferred, or borrowed from the sister property. A single score is a snapshot in any case, not a condition survey. The survey is Section 12's business.

## 8. The safety gaps its era left open

Three gaps belong to the building's code era rather than to anyone's neglect, and each is a named, fundable item of the kind housing funders routinely pay for.

**Sprinklers.** The extent of this building's sprinkler coverage is, on the public record, an open question. A published resident review describes full sprinkler coverage; accounts relayed from inside the building describe sprinklers in the common areas rather than throughout the homes. No inspection record available to this research settles the conflict, and it is carried here unresolved, with its settling route named: the fire marshal's and insurer's records, the federal inspection file, and any capital-needs assessment. What the documented record does supply is the era's frame. Residential sprinkler standards were young when this building was designed — the residential-occupancy sprinkler standard, NFPA 13R, dates to 1989, twelve years after the building opened — and model codes did not require sprinklering of buildings of this kind until the mid-1980s. Partial coverage in a 1977 building would be the era's norm, not a scandal. It is also, by modern standards, the largest life-safety gap such a building carries, and an in-unit sprinkler retrofit is the standard remedy.

**Smoke.** The documented primary hazard in fires in buildings of this form is not flame travel but smoke travel. Smoke inhalation accounts for roughly seventy-five percent of fire deaths in United States building fires, on figures carried in the fire-engineering literature; about half of fire deaths occur outside the room where the fire started; and in buildings with vertical shafts, smoke moves upward through stair, elevator, and duct shafts under stack effect — shaft routes account for roughly ninety-five percent or more of the upward movement of smoke in the studied buildings. The building's own 2013 incident, in which an elevator motor's smoke and not any flame was the whole event, is a small and harmless rehearsal of exactly this behaviour. Smoke control — shaft and duct dampers, door-leakage treatment, detection coverage verified against current standards — is therefore a real line in any serious scope, funded as part of a rehabilitation rather than by any standalone grant.

**Storm shelter.** The building has no dedicated storm shelter and no rated safe room, in a county where that sentence carries weight. The directed refuge is the interior stairwell on the lowest floor, and that direction is consistent with National Weather Service and Ready guidance for buildings without a shelter: lowest floor, small interior space, away from windows, no elevator. An interior concrete core is the strongest ordinary space such a building has. But guidance and rating are different things. A FEMA P-361 safe room or an ICC 500 storm shelter is an engineered, debris-impact-tested structure — the federal criterion is near-absolute protection, tested against a fifteen-pound two-by-four missile at a hundred miles an hour in 250-mile-an-hour design winds — and an unrated stairwell, however sensibly chosen, is a best-available refuge, not a rated shelter. For a population of older adults who cannot be asked to move fast or far, a rated safe room sized to the building is a legitimate capital need, and Section 12 lists it as one.

## 9. The sister towers, and the Methodist thread

The name that follows this building around Hays is Epworth, and the story behind it is a neighbourhood story worth telling straight, because it is so easily told wrong.

There is no man named Epworth in it. Epworth is a place — Epworth in Lincolnshire, England, the home of Methodism and the birthplace of John and Charles Wesley — and the Hays church whose housing ministry took the name says so in its own published history. More than fifty-five years ago, members of Hays First United Methodist Church saw a need for housing and created a nonprofit entity, Epworth Village Inc, exempt as a charity by an Internal Revenue Service ruling of October 1982, its stated mission to provide housing on a nonprofit basis for persons of low to moderate income. On nine and a half acres east of Vine Street it built Epworth Village: seventy-five homes in twelve buildings for families, financed by the federal housing department at a subsidized one percent, overseen to this day, on the church's account, by a board of its own members. The nonprofit is active and planning still: it holds an exclusive option, running through the end of 2027, on city-owned land at 26th and Main, where it proposes two dozen or so new two-bedroom homes if it can raise the funds — tax credits intended — and win the city's approval of a design.

The sister tower is Epworth Towers, at 2800 Augusta Lane: ninety homes in six floors, senior housing, built in 1980 or 1981 — the sources conflict by a year, and the conflict is flagged rather than resolved. Its owner of record in the federal-derived dataset is given as Epworth Senior Residences LLC; the Hays Post states that Epworth Village Inc has provided low-income housing for many years at two locations in Hays, Epworth Village and Epworth Towers; and the precise legal relationship between the nonprofit and the limited-liability company — sponsor or general partner is the standard tax-credit pattern — is stated in no document found, and is carried here as an open question with its settling documents named in the sources note. What is fully documented is the renovation: a tax-credit acquisition and rehabilitation through KHRC of about 6.9 million dollars, begun in July 2019 and completed in the spring of its final year — contemporaneous reporting places completion in April 2021, while one earlier working record said April 2020, a discrepancy flagged and never smoothed. The scope was top to bottom: new windows and elevators, the boiler replaced by per-unit heating and cooling control, the sewer replaced, a generator added, security cameras, and every unit remodelled.

This building's connection to that circle is management history, and it is documented in the most human of sources. A published obituary from February 2012 records that Grace Murphy and her husband Paul began a career in rental property management taking on the management of Epworth Village, then adding Centennial Towers, Epworth Towers, and two other Hays properties, and that Grace served on the Methodist church's housing board for many of those years and was active in the development of the church's Epworth projects. Read plainly, the obituary places this building inside the Epworth and Methodist housing orbit in the most ordinary way a building gets there: the same local people managed the church's projects and this building alike. Generations of the building's people hearing "Epworth" spoken in the same breath as their home's management would produce exactly the story that circulates — without any Epworth body ever owning this building.

The sisterhood of the two towers today is a management sisterhood and nothing more: a shared manager in Key Management Company, a shared program shape, a shared city. This building is owned by Hays Senior Residences, L.P.; the Towers' owner of record is the Epworth company named above; no shared owner appears in any record found, and the word Epworth attaches to this building nowhere in its own record. Whether this building's original 1977 developer had any Methodist or Epworth connection is a reasonable question the management history raises, and no source found answers it. The 1977 deed and permit file would.

## 10. The unnamed architect, and the paper that survives

The finding of this section is one sentence long: **no public source found names the architect of record, the original developer, or the builder of this building.** The rest of the section is the account of how hard that was looked for, so the sentence can be trusted.

General searching on the building's name, address, and year returned listings only; no source names a design firm, a developer, or a contractor for the 1977 project, and none names one for the sister tower either, so no sister-property thread exists to pull. The state historical society's newspaper microfilm catalogue is the one fully public route to the likeliest source — reporting in the Hays Daily News across 1976 to 1978, when local construction of this scale was news as a matter of course — but the catalogue is an index of reels, not a searchable text, and reading those reels is a reading-room task in Topeka, not a web task. The society's digital collection, Kansas Memory, holds material that predates this project by decades. A commercial newspaper archive carries Hays titles behind a paywall that this research did not open, and the paywall is recorded as a fact of the landscape rather than stepped around. The architects' national historical directory is biographical — it can confirm a firm's principals and dates once a firm is suspected, but it indexes no projects. Federal construction reporting of the 1970s is not publicly searchable. One tempting lead was checked and excluded, and is recorded so nobody chases it twice: a 1977 steel-construction journal article on a "Centennial Towers" describes a nine-story, L-shaped home for the elderly somewhere else. This building's name is shared — a 1977 senior tower of the same name stands in Jacksonville, Florida, and a third stands in Montrose, Colorado — and every fact in this report was pinned to the address, never to the name alone.

A handful of Wichita practices were indisputably in the right city, at the right scale, in the right years designing Kansas institutional work — Schaefer & Associates, Dudley Williams & Associates, and LK Architecture are the names the firm histories support. They are recorded as candidates and nothing more: no document found connects any of them, or any other firm, to this project, and a candidate is not an answer.

As for the drawings, the honest assessment runs holder by holder. The City of Hays most probably holds the permit file — applications, permits, inspection cards — while full drawing sets were commonly returned to the applicant or kept only for a term; Kansas law fixes municipal retention for only eleven record series by statute, and building plans are not among them, and no Hays retention schedule for plan sets was found. KHRC's 1998 project file holds the rehabilitation's scope and budget, not drawings. The county appraiser's property record card is documented and valuable beyond any drawing for the building's physical facts: measured dimensions, gross area, year built, the structural and wall codes, the roof codes, and a dimensioned sketch, all at 718 Main Street in Hays, where the appraiser is now Eugene Rupp, appointed in 2025 — the office changed hands since this building was last written up, and the errand itself stands. A full set of original drawings, if one survives anywhere, most probably survives in the owner's or the manager's own files. The federal housing file from the insured-mortgage era would name the design team outright, but those are agency records, reachable by the owner or by request, not published data.

The single inquiry most likely to settle the authorship question is the **1977 building-permit application for 2502 Sherman Avenue, at the City of Hays**: permit applications of the era name the architect, the contractor, and the owner in one document. If the city no longer holds it, the next inquiry is the owner's own file, and the one after that the federal case file. Until one of those is read, the authorship of this building is unknown, and this report leaves it unknown rather than guessed.

## 11. The land's second question: could it grow?

Section 1 gave the physical arithmetic: a building covering a tenth of its 3.78 acres, parking at 1.67 spaces per home, room on the ground for a great deal more. The legal arithmetic is less generous, and an honest report has to print both.

The parcel's actual zoning district was not established by this research — the city's published zoning map does not render into readable form from outside, no listing states the district, and a county land-use code that aggregators print for the parcel is not city zoning and is not relied on. What the city's published code does give is the standard the parcel would most likely be judged against: in the Residential Multi-Family district, the Unified Development Code sets a minimum open-space ratio of thirty percent and a maximum gross density of 19.20 homes per acre. Against that standard, the existing property already runs at ninety homes on 3.78 acres — **23.8 homes per acre, above the current cap**. The conclusion follows exactly as far as it goes and no further: the property is either zoned under a different or planned designation, or its density is lawful as a condition that predates the code — but either way, new homes on this lot would be judged against the current code, and the existing over-density may leave no by-right headroom at all. An expansion would most likely require Planning Commission and City Commission action, through a planned-development or rezoning route, and not merely a building permit. The current parking schedule for senior housing and the district's setback and height tables should be confirmed at the city's planning office before any expansion is drawn, let alone proposed.

Growing upward is a different question again, and this report makes no prediction about it. For a concrete frame of this era, adding a storey requires a structural engineer's verification that columns, beams, and the lateral system can carry another floor — frames of 1977 were typically designed for the height built, with no documented reserve — a foundation investigation against the added load, the cascade of code consequences an added floor triggers in elevators, stairs, sprinklers, alarms, and accessibility, and the removal or rebuilding of the pitched-roof cap the frame already carries. If the building proves to be masonry-bearing rather than frame, the question becomes harder still. No public document settles any part of it. It is recorded here as a scoped question for a Kansas-licensed structural engineer with the permit file in hand — which is where the question belongs.

## 12. What this building needs

Stripped of program language, the needs are plain, and they are the needs of a sound frame carrying old systems.

It needs its paper read. The county appraiser's record card, to settle its structure and dimensions. The 1977 permit application, to recover its authors — and with them, credit where credit is fifty years overdue. KHRC's 1998 project file, to learn what its second birth actually renewed. Its own federal inspection score, read and quoted with its date. None of this costs much, and all of it is the ground any serious decision about the building stands on.

It needs a capital-needs assessment: an architect or engineer walking it system by system — roof cap and the deck beneath it; structure; elevators, the most load-bearing system after the frame for a population of older adults; heating, cooling, and ventilation plant; plumbing stacks, water distribution, and the sewer lateral; envelope, across roughly 120,000 face bricks in want of a repointing survey, and windows; accessibility against current standards for an aging population; energy. That assessment is the document every funder asks for first, and until it exists, every condition statement about this building beyond its age and its documented history is speculation. This report has made none.

It needs the life-safety gaps of its era closed: its sprinkler coverage verified home by home and retrofitted in the units if the coverage proves partial, as its era suggests; smoke control across its shafts and ducts brought to current practice; and a rated safe room — FEMA P-361 or ICC 500 — sized for people who cannot be asked to move fast or far, so that the best-available refuge of a stairwell is no longer the best the building can offer in a Kansas spring.

It needs its systems renewed before they fail on their own schedule rather than a planned one — above all the water piping, which stands at the end of its published service life, and behind it the plant, elevators, windows, and sewer that its sister tower's renovation enumerated for the whole generation of these buildings.

And it needs its calendar respected. The building crosses the fifty-year line in 2027, and with that crossing the landmark question in the next section changes from impossible to arguable. Its recorded affordability covenants run to about 2029, and the legal window through which those restrictions could be ended has stood open for years already — a matter of architecture in the law, stated here as calendar, not as any prediction about any person; the record searched for this report contains no notice of default, no enforcement action, no pending sale, and no sign of distress of any kind, and its owner's federal registration stands current. Calendars, unlike pipes, can be beaten simply by being read in time. That is, in the end, what this report is for.

## 13. The landmark question, without flattery

Today the answer is short: **not eligible**. The National Register of Historic Places works to a fifty-year guideline, and a property younger than fifty can be listed only on a showing of exceptional importance. This building is forty-nine. It crosses the guideline in 2027, and on current evidence no exceptional-importance case exists to make before then — no documented event, person, or design distinction of exceptional weight attaches to this address.

Criterion by criterion, the architecture will not carry it, and this report declines to pretend otherwise. This is a standard-issue late-1970s federally financed senior mid-rise: concrete frame, brick skin, double-loaded corridor, repeated plans. No architect is named, no distinctive style or method or craftsmanship is documented, and the undated roof cap has altered the original silhouette in any case. If a case is ever made for this building, it will be made on history instead — on its being a surviving, working example of the federal subsidized senior-housing era in rural Kansas, insured, project-assisted, tax-credit rehabilitated, and still serving its original purpose at forty-nine years. That context is plausible and honestly stateable. It is also, today, an argument rather than a finding: a Register case would need the comparative work — how many such towers Kansas built, how many survive, in what integrity — done before the argument could carry weight. The state-level route is the realistic one: the Kansas Register of Historic Places applies the same family of criteria, and a state or thematic case is the natural first step once the age gate opens in 2027. Whether the City of Hays offers any local landmark designation could not be assessed; no local preservation ordinance or program was found in the city's published code as searched, and that is carried as unverified rather than as a proven negative.

What listing would unlock, if it ever came, is money of an unusual size for a small Kansas city — stated with its condition in the same breath: listing alone produces nothing, and the credits attach only to an approved, qualifying rehabilitation reviewed in advance against the Secretary of the Interior's Standards. The federal historic tax credit returns twenty percent of qualified rehabilitation expenditures for a listed, income-producing property. The Kansas credit, under the statute as amended in 2025, returns **forty percent** of qualified expenditures for structures in cities of fifty thousand or fewer people, on plans placed in service on or after 1 July 2025 — the older general description of the credit is twenty-five percent, and both figures are carried here because the rate that applies depends on when a plan is placed in service; Kansas credits are transferable, which matters to a partnership whose own tax appetite is limited. The state's Heritage Trust Fund adds 80/20 matching grants whose maximum the Historical Society's own pages state inconsistently — ninety thousand dollars on one Society page, one hundred thousand on its program page, both checked 25 October 2026; the variance is noted, and the lower figure, ninety thousand, is the one relied on. Stacking historic credits onto a property already carrying rental assistance and a tax-credit history is a specialist structuring exercise with interacting compliance periods and recapture clocks; it is neither declared compatible nor incompatible here, only flagged as work for a preservation-finance consultant — a consultation the 2027 window and the forty percent tier make worth having before, not after, the next rehabilitation is scoped.

What listing would not do deserves equal plainness. It does not protect a privately owned building against its own owner's private money; preservation review attaches to federal undertakings and federal funding, not to private acts. It does not fund operations. It does not pay for any condition the owner does not choose to rehabilitate. And it is no substitute for the assessment in Section 12, which every one of these programs will ask for first anyway.
