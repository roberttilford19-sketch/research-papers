# Elias Lönnrot — Kanteletar (1840–41)

*The Boneyard is a collection of works left unfinished, unpublished, or unread at their authors' deaths — dry bones in the valley, in Ezekiel's image. Each presentation tells the scholar's story, weighs what survives of his work, and states plainly what completing it would take, so that a book the world never received whole can be met at last. Each of them was one of one. There will never be another like him, and his light will not shine again except through the work he left behind.*

## Dedication

To Elias Lönnrot, the Finnish physician and folklorist who built the *Kalevala* and then gave its sister volume to the songs the epic could not hold. In the *Kanteletar* he preserved nearly seven hundred lyric poems and ballads from Finnish oral tradition, women's songs above all. This presentation is dedicated to him, and to the singers whose work, love, weddings, laments, and charms he carried into print.

## The author's own terms

*Kanteletar*, the name Lönnrot gave the collection, means "the Kantele-daughter," after the kantele, the Finnish harp-like instrument.

## The story

Most readers who know Finland's great book know the *Kalevala*, the heroic epic. Fewer know its sister. Elias Lönnrot (1802–1884), the Finnish physician and folklorist who built the *Kalevala*, published the *Kanteletar* in 1840–41 — its name means "the Kantele-daughter," after the kantele, the Finnish harp-like instrument. Where the *Kalevala* is the heroic epic, the *Kanteletar* is the people's lyric diary: a treasury of nearly seven hundred lyric poems and ballads from Finnish oral tradition.

These are, above all, women's songs — songs of work, of love, of weddings, laments, charms, and the Virgin Mary ballad cycle. Together they preserve how ordinary people sang their joys and griefs. A collection of that size and character deserves to be read whole, in its own order. In English, so far, it has been read only in part, and the part is much smaller than the whole.

## The work and what survives

The complete Finnish text is in the public domain and openly held. Project Gutenberg carries the *Kanteletar* — noted in the Wikipedia article's database references — and Finnish scholarly editions, from the Finnish Literature Society (SKS), are in print. The work is in the public domain: an 1840–41 imprint, and the compiler died in 1884.

So the Finnish book is safe and available. The gap is English, and it is a gap of scale.

## Its English fortunes

Keith Bosley's *The Kanteletar* (Oxford World's Classics, 1992) was the collection's first appearance in English — and it is expressly a selection: 224 pages against some 700 poems. It is now out of print. Bosley and Senni Timonen's *I Will Sing of What I Know* offers 50 poems. Fifty poems, chosen well, can introduce a treasury; they cannot be the treasury.

The bounded finding of this research, verified on 14 October 2026, is this: **No complete English translation found after research.**

## What completing it would take

Completing the work would mean a complete verse translation of the roughly 700 poems from the 1840–41 Finnish, in the collection's own three-book order. The work is big but perfectly divisible into instalments: Book I lyrics, Book II, and Book III ballads.

Craft in the Kalevala metre is the whole game; the poems live in that measure, and an English version has to find a way to sing in it rather than merely report what the Finnish says. A Finnish reader as reviewer is mandatory. And the honesty rules that this collection applies to folk-language work apply double here: a draft without native review must be labelled a draft, and no edition may present itself as more than it is. The songs were carried in Finnish oral tradition before Lönnrot published them. The least a complete English *Kanteletar* owes them is completeness, and an honest title page.
